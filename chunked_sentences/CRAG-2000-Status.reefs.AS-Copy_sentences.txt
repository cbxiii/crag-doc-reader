#### American Samoa's Exclusive Economic Zone

!

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
The Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) of American Samoa is a valuable territorial asset.

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
Extending up to 200 miles seaward from land or half the distance to a neighboring country, EEZs were designated so that the parent country has a degree of control over the resources upon which the people of that land may rely as well as jurisdiction over vessel traffic close to their shores.

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
The EEZ of American Samoa is approximately 197,000 square kilometers, close to the size of the state of Utah.

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
This area will become even more important in the future as pharmaceutical companies continue to find natural sources of medicine in the seas, man extends his explorations of the uses of the sea, and fish stocks worldwide continue to be depleted.

Just like other living organisms, corals can, in fact, become so sick that they die.

--- SENTENCE 46 ---
Oftentimes this is brought about by some sort of a disease, other times the corals just 'bleach'.

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
Diseases on coral reefs often manifest in similar ways as they do in humans.

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
They are generally visible in the form of a tumor or disfiguration.

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
Diseases are generally spread from one organism to another and may be 'nearby' frequently.

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
However, it has been documented that most corals do not catch these diseases and become sick until they are under other stresses- usually what are termed environmental stresses.

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
Recognizing these conditions on our reefs may be a key to the continuation of healthy coral reefs in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
Coral diseases are commonly identified by a change in the coral polyp- the individual coral organism which, in combination with its neighbors, makes up dense coral colonies.

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
The polyp may be engulfed by some sort of bacterium, and display an unusual color.

--- SENTENCE 54 ---
it may have a 'tumor', it may be 'wasted away' by a fungus, or something inside it may have triggered it to release it's normal companion- a live inside algae called zooxanthallae.

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
These diseases carry fear-inspiring names such as redband disease, bleaching, rapid wasting disease, and white pox.

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
Coralline algae also suffer from a couple of known lethal diseases.

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
Diseases are believed to spread through the water column and across colonies.

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
Some believe they may even be introduced in atmospheric dust.

Sea Level Rise: The temperature on the surface of the Earth has reportedly risen about 0.5 degrees Celsius in the last one hundred years.

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
This phenomenon of increased average Earth temperature is known as global warming.

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
While many consider it to be largely due to human activities, there is little evidence to uphold a significant increase in global temperatures being solely humankind's responsibility, rather than continued natural climatic variability.

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
Ice ages have occurred longer than man has been recorded on the planet, with contrasting warming cycles also occurring.

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
The effect of global warming on the surface of the Earth has been noticed, most

arguably, in sea level rise.

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
As glaciers shrink and legendary snowcapped mountains begin to melt, sea level has risen approximately 15-20 cm in the last century.

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
It is believed that it will continue to be rise at the same rate of 1-2 mm/year.

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
Increased temperatures lead to this rise in a couple of manners; causing surface water molecules to physically take up more space as they heat and by causing water in ice (in icebergs, ice floes, glaciers, etc.) to melt and add to seawater.

--- SENTENCE 76 ---
Some scientists believe that even if more of the ice melts, warmer temperatures will cause increased snowfall, putting water back into solid forms that will alleviate the increased sea level.

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
However, there is no question that humans have caused an increase in global Carbon Dioxide levels.

--- SENTENCE 78 ---
Carbon Dioxide serves as a heat trapping gas in the atmosphere and has risen more than 30% since the beginning of the industrial age.

--- SENTENCE 79 ---
This may have begun to effect organisms like coral that rely on other carbon-oxygen molecules for their structure, since these molecules are not as available in a sea more rich with carbon dioxide.

--- SENTENCE 80 ---
Industrial countries like the United States are among the worst producers of atmospheric Carbon Dioxide.

--- SENTENCE 81 ---
The increase of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere is not likely to change soon because international environmental treaties do not call for reductions in Carbon Dioxide emissions for years.

Perhaps in small areas this is a necessary disturbance event, much like small scale fires in forests, but when COT numbers increase they can be a daunting force on a coral reef.

One additional disturbance to the marine ecosystem occurs annually when the humpbacks whales arrive.

--- SENTENCE 207 ---
The whales take advantage of the shallow 'roughness' of the reef flat and

roll around to rid themselves of the cumbersome barnacles clinging to their skin, slowing them down as they move across the vast ocean.

--- SENTENCE 208 ---
This pattern creates patches of reef on which the corraline algae may once again cement the reef and provide habitat for corals and associated organisms.

### Longliners in Pago Pago Harbor

In 1991 Hurricane Val graced the eastern shores of Pago Pago Harbor with nine unsightly longliners.

--- SENTENCE 210 ---
After years of local efforts to call attention to and remove these derelicts, the federal government responded with emergency protocol.

--- SENTENCE 211 ---
The United States Coast Guard (USCG) initially removed hazardous fluids from the ships, but by the middle of 1999 thousands of gallons of oil and an unknown amount of ammonia were still on the ships.

--- SENTENCE 212 ---
Once the USCG completed removal

of these hazardous materials, they coordinated with NOAA's Office of Removal and Restoration which obtained funding from the Oil Spill Liability Trust Fund to remove the remaining debris and aid in coral reef restoration.

--- SENTENCE 213 ---
Bulldozers, cranes, and other heavy equipment were used to haul debris from the water by building artificial causeways to the sites of many of the vessels.

--- SENTENCE 214 ---
This debris was then piled on a large barge and taken to an offshore deep water site and dumped.

--- SENTENCE 215 ---
Two of the vessels, which ran aground in the outer harbor, were removed by helicopter and then pulled out to sea.

--- SENTENCE 216 ---
Efforts have been made to restore the coral reefs in these outer areas through coral transplantation.

--- SENTENCE 217 ---
Further artificial reef stabilization is still a possibility.

Marine turtles lay their eggs on beaches around the world, returning to the beaches where they were born.

--- SENTENCE 233 ---
Eggs are laid in conjunction with the phases of the moon at specific tides in order to best ensure the survival of the baby turtles.

--- SENTENCE 234 ---
When the turtles hatch, they move rapidly towards the brightest horizon, generally that of the light of the moon reflecting from the waves on the surface of the ocean.

--- SENTENCE 235 ---
This light is their primary indicator for reaching safety and avoiding the overwhelming predators on land.

--- SENTENCE 236 ---
Dogs, crabs, birds, and humans are among their worst enemies during the few

moments after hatching in which the baby turtles orient and reach the sea.

--- SENTENCE 237 ---
Throughout Florida, the Caribbean, and the Pacific, it has been demonstrated that the light from cities and roads causes the turtle hatchlings to become disoriented during their crucial initial stages of life.

--- SENTENCE 238 ---
In order for American Samoa to continue hatching turtles in the future, we need to be careful to protect our beaches and not light the shorelines too much.

#### Wetland Restoration and Protection

The territory of American Samoa is unique in Polynesia, as it's economy stems largely through local business rather than tourism.

--- SENTENCE 288 ---
As a result, areas of natural beauty such as the *Nu'uuli Pala* have not been recognized for their potential commercial value and continue to be developed for residential uses in a rapidly growing population.

--- SENTENCE 289 ---
The Wetlands of American Samoa include mangrove swamps, marshes (saltwater and fresh), and numerous streams.

--- SENTENCE 290 ---
The wetlands are critical habitat for numerous species of birds, mammals, reptiles, amphibians, fish, mollusks, and other invertebrates, many of which are considered threatened and perhaps endangered.

--- SENTENCE 291 ---
They are being lost to fill and land reclamation at alarming rates.

--- SENTENCE 292 ---
Piggeries line the streams entering the lagoon and there is evidence of septic overflow from various homes.

--- SENTENCE 293 ---
A number of landromats, agricultural stands, and aquaculture ponds are found near wetlands.

--- SENTENCE 294 ---
Many automotive repair facilities directly border the wetlands/mangrove area.

--- SENTENCE 295 ---
Old refrigerators, air-conditioners, cars, engines, gas tanks, etc., may be found in various stages of decomposition.

--- SENTENCE 296 ---
Eutrophication is a potential problem within the wetlands of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 297 ---
Soils do not have the ability to handle sustained nutrient input.

--- SENTENCE 298 ---
Biodiversity has and will continue to be decreased as the entire foodweb is affected by increased nutrient loading.

--- SENTENCE 299 ---
Crabs which have been shown to play a key role in tree presence or absence are endangered, while microfauna will be smothered as soils go anaero-

bic.

--- SENTENCE 300 ---
Coral reef species around the world depend upon wetlands as nurseries and feeding grounds.

--- SENTENCE 301 ---
Coral reefs surrounding our islands can be directly damaged by land-based activities, and so land and sea environments cannot be viewed as being separate from one another.

--- SENTENCE 302 ---
The rehabilitation of wetlands involves massive solid waste removal along streams and in the mangrove forest.

--- SENTENCE 303 ---
Planting of trees along streams and at the edge of the mangroves is a second crucial step in rehabilitation.

--- SENTENCE 304 ---
Restoration and public education about these wetlands is necessary to preserve this important ecosystem

!

#### Village Management of Resources

!

--- SENTENCE 320 ---
Societies worldwide have long recognized the importance of the ecosystem to the culture.

--- SENTENCE 321 ---
Samoan legends include stories about the importance of forest and reefs.

--- SENTENCE 322 ---
The village is traditionally considered to run from the "tuasivi" to the "sami", the shoreline to the sea.

--- SENTENCE 323 ---
As past generations have recognized the need to maintain the balance of their resources, it is important to recognize that the health of the islands still depends upon the vitality of the entire ecosystem.

--- SENTENCE 324 ---
No one questions the destruction that the sea may have upon the land in natural forms such as hurricanes and tsunamis.

--- SENTENCE 325 ---
However, one does not often consider the concordant havoc reeked from land upon the ocean.

--- SENTENCE 326 ---
Sedimentation resulting from mining, development, and tree removal has been demonstrated to have a negative effect on coral reef health.

--- SENTENCE 327 ---
Toxins have been found in many marine species; fish and invertebrates, surrounding the island.

--- SENTENCE 328 ---
Increased nutrients from fertilizers, piggeries and cattle farms lead to eutrophication of bays.

--- SENTENCE 329 ---
Continued alteration of the shoreline from sandmining, seawalls, and jetties affects current patterns.

--- SENTENCE 330 ---
This change in water movement may lead to burial of some reefs, and lower sources of necessary inputs to others.

--- SENTENCE 331 ---
Trash from the land falls gradually into the ocean on a steady basis yet streams quickly into the sea during large rain events.

--- SENTENCE 332 ---
In an effort to encourage village participation in preserving the reefs, DMWR is going out to the villages; training them about management, working on changing the legislation to give villages power to legally pursue illegal fishing activities, teaching them to take steps to monitor fish reserves, prepare rules against destructive fishing, and establish land-based coral reef ecosystem protection.

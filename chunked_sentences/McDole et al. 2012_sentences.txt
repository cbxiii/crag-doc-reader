Abstract

The majority of the world's coral reefs are in various stages of decline.

--- SENTENCE 3 ---
While a suite of disturbances (overfishing, eutrophication, and global climate change) have been identified, the mechanism(s) of reef system decline remain elusive.

--- SENTENCE 4 ---
Increased microbial and viral loading with higher percentages of opportunistic and specific microbial pathogens have been identified as potentially unifying features of coral reefs in decline.

--- SENTENCE 5 ---
Due to their relative size and high per cell activity, a small change in microbial biomass may signal a large reallocation of available energy in an ecosystem; that is the microbialization of the coral reef.

--- SENTENCE 6 ---
Our hypothesis was that human activities alter the energy budget of the reef system, specifically by altering the allocation of metabolic energy between microbes and macrobes.

--- SENTENCE 7 ---
To determine if this is occurring on a regional scale, we calculated the basal metabolic rates for the fish and microbial communities at 99 sites on twenty-nine coral islands throughout the Pacific Ocean using previously established scaling relationships.

--- SENTENCE 8 ---
From these metabolic rate predictions, we derived a new metric for assessing and comparing reef health called the microbialization score.

--- SENTENCE 9 ---
The microbialization score represents the percentage of the combined fish and microbial predicted metabolic rate that is microbial.

--- SENTENCE 10 ---
Our results demonstrate a strong positive correlation between reef microbialization scores and human impact.

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
In contrast, microbialization scores did not significantly correlate with ocean net primary production, local chla concentrations, or the combined metabolic rate of the fish and microbial communities.

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
These findings support the hypothesis that human activities are shifting energy to the microbes, at the expense of the macrobes.

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
Regardless of oceanographic context, the microbialization score is a powerful metric for assessing the level of human impact a reef system is experiencing.

### Introduction

The relationship between increasing human activity and decreasing fish biomass is well-established in coral reef systems

--- SENTENCE 24 ---
Although herbivore reduction due to overfishing probably facilitates coral to algal transitions, the mechanistic link between overfishing and coral mortality is not clear [4].

--- SENTENCE 25 ---
Much uncertainty about the mechanisms of reef decline linked to eutrophication and climate change also still exists [5–6].

--- SENTENCE 26 ---
In addition to increasing algal cover relative to hard coral cover, other effects of anthropogenically-driven disturbances include disease outbreaks, fewer links in trophic webs, and loss of physical structure and habitat complexity

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
Reef-associated microbial communities have been shown to respond to all of the above disturbances (overfishing, nutrient enrichment, thermal stress) by becoming less beneficial and more pathogenic, i.e. the proportion of sequences related to known pathogens typically increases [10–17].

--- SENTENCE 28 ---
Despite the epidemiological evidence linking the microbial ecology of coral reef systems to human activity, the largest study of coral reef microbial communities included only four coral atolls in the Line Islands, all clustered within one oceanographic region [14].

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
In this island chain a 10-fold increase in microbial and viral abundances in the overlying reef-water correlated with increasing human disturbance and was accompanied by decreased fish biomass [1,14].

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
Further, a large proportion of the microbial 16S rDNA sequence similarities on the most disturbed reefs were most closely related to known pathogens [14].

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
These reefs also had the highest incidences of coral disease and the lowest percent coral cover.

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
Other studies have also suggested that the total carbon flow through microbial pathways via detritus is inversely related to coral cover [18–19].

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
Ecosystems exhibit higher-level properties resulting from lower-level phenomena [20].

--- SENTENCE 34 ---
The energy available to a higher trophic level, for example, is reduced by the amount required to support the individual organisms in the lower level.

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
The Metabolic Theory

of Ecology (MTE) predicts the metabolic rate of individual organisms based on the observation that most variation in an individual's metabolic rate can be explained by body size and temperature

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
Whole organism metabolic rate (*I*), defined as the amount of energy per unit time that an individual organism requires, is calculated using Equation 1:

$$I = i_0 M^{\alpha} e^{-E/kT} \tag{1}$$

Where  $i_0$  is the mass-independent normalization constant, M is the wet weight of the organism in grams, and  $\alpha$  is the scaling exponent.

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
The effects of temperature on metabolic rate are accounted for by  $e^{-E/kT}$

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
[21,23] where E is the activation energy, k is Boltzmann's constant  $(8.62 \times 10^{-5} \text{ eV K}^{-1})$ , and T is the water temperature at the site at the time of collection (in Kelvin).

--- SENTENCE 39 ---
Distinct scaling exponents have been derived for different physiological states and evolutionary groups

--- SENTENCE 40 ---
The process of replacing macroorganisms with microbes has been termed microbialization

--- SENTENCE 41 ---
In this study, Equation 1 was used to predict metabolic rates for all individual fish and microbes present in a 10 m<sup>3</sup> volume of reef water.

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
*Microbialization* refers to an increase in the percentage of the combined fish and microbial predicted metabolic rate that is microbial.

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
Island-level microbialization scores were derived for 29 islands (99 sites) within four oceanographic regions of the Pacific Ocean.

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
Our data show a strong significant positive correlation between microbialization scores and the NCEAS cumulative human impact scores at each island.

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
In comparison, microbialization scores did not correlate with the net primary production values.

--- SENTENCE 46 ---
These findings support the hypothesis that human activities rather than variation in oceanographic conditions are causing microbialization of coral reefs and that the microbialization score is a powerful metric for assessing and comparing reef health.

### **Materials and Methods**

### Site descriptions

The twenty-nine islands included in this study were surveyed following the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Association (NOAA) 's Rapid Ecological Assessment (REA) protocol as part of the Coral Reef Ecosystem Division (CRED) and Pacific Reef Assessment and Monitoring Program (Pacific RAMP)

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
Multiple coral reef sites (average depth: 10 m) were sampled at each island in four broad regional groups: the Main Hawaiian Islands (MHI), Guam and the Mariana Islands (MARIANA), the American Samoa region (SAMOA), and the Pacific Remote Island Areas (PRIA) (Fig. 1, Table 1).

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
Microbial samples were collected during the 2008-2010 Pacific RAMP monitoring cruises: MHI (2008), MARIANAS (2009), SAMOA (2010), PRIA (2010).

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
For fish, belt survey data from 2001-2009 was used for all islands.

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
Because the REA survey protocol switched to the Stationary Point Count (SPC) method in 2009, 2010 fish data was not included.

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
Microbial and fish data collection sites at each island are not necessarily co-located.

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
Due to the variability inherent with observational fish data, the standard approach for estimating island means for fish abundance requires a large sample size.

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
To have an adequate sample size, this fish data was pooled from all sites and years.

--- SENTENCE 54 ---
Island-level averages and standard errors for fish and microbial biomass are provided in Table S2 and Fig.

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
Microbial metabolic rates were calculated per site then averaged by island.

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
Island-level averages for fish and microbial predicted metabolic rates were used to calculate one microbialization score for each island.

### Collection of microbial data

At each site, 4 replicate 2 l seawater samples were collected  $\sim$ 1 m above the benthos using polycarbonate Niskin bottles.

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
Microscopy grade glutaraldehyde was added to a final concentration of 0.3% v/v. Microbial cells were collected from each sample by filtration using a 0.2  $\mu$ m Anodisc filter (Whatman) and then stained with 5  $\mu$ g ml $^{-1}$  DAPI (Molecular Probes, Invitrogen) within 2 hours of collection

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
Filters were mounted on microscope slides and stored at  $-20^{\circ}$ C. For each site, 10 fields of view (5 fields for each of 2 replicate filters,  $\sim$ 200 cells per field) were examined by epifluorescence microscopy (excitation/emission:

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
358/461 nm) at 600× magnification.

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
Cell counts and dimensions were collected using ImagePro Software (Media Cybernetics) set for a size range of 0.00001–10  $\mu$ m for both length and width.

--- SENTENCE 61 ---
Cell volume (V) was calculated by considering all cells to be cylinders with hemispherical caps using Equation 2:

$$V = \pi/4 \times w^2 (l - w/3) \tag{2}$$

where l is length and w is width

--- SENTENCE 62 ---
No correction was made for possible cell shrinkage as a result of fixation.

--- SENTENCE 63 ---
Individual microbial cell volumes  $V(\mu m^3)$  were converted to mass in wet weight (g) using previously established size-dependent relationships for marine microbial communities [32].

--- SENTENCE 64 ---
Each cell volume V was next converted to dry weight using the linear relationship derived from data reported in Simon and Azam (1989) and shown in Equation 3.

$$\log(y)

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
= 1.72 \log(x) - 12.63 \tag{3}$$

where x is cell dry weight and y is cell volume ( $r^2 = 0.99$ ).

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
Then cell wet weight (z) was calculated using the linear relation shown in Equation 4 [32] ( $r^2 = 0.99$ ):

$$\log(z) = 1.63 \log(x) - 2.0 \tag{4}$$

### Collection of fish data

This study includes fish data from all surveys performed at REA sites during the years 2001-2009.

--- SENTENCE 67 ---
The number of REA sites surveyed over this time period is provided for each island in Table S2.

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
Visual surveys provided a census of the reef fish community

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
Surveys were restricted to shallow-to-moderate depths along the forereef between 10-15 m with a majority of surveys completed along the 10 m isobaths.

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
At each site, a total of three 25 m long belt transect surveys were conducted by two different divers.

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
For each survey, the diver made two passes: during the first pass, all fish >20 cm in length were recorded in adjacent 4 m wide belts; during the second pass all fish ≤20 cm were recorded in 2 m wide belts.

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
Lengths were recorded to the nearest cm for fish < 5 cm and in 5 cm bins for all others [34].

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
Species-specific mass values for individual fish were calculated from length-weight relationships using FishBase [35-36].

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
The fish data was provided for each family as mean biomass (g m  $^{-2}$ ) and mean abundance (# individuals m<sup>-2</sup>), from which the mean mass per individual (g) was calculated.

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
Because surveys were carried out at an average water depth of 10 m and surveyors counted all fish in the water column up to the surface, the mean abundances (individuals per m<sup>2</sup>) represented the total number present in a 10 m<sup>3</sup> water column.

### Metabolic rate calculations

At each REA site, community-level metabolic rates were calculated by summing the individual metabolic rates (I) for all fish or microbes present in a standard volume of water  $(10 \text{ m}^3)$ .

--- SENTENCE 81 ---
Individual metabolic rates (I) in watts were calculated using Equation 1.

--- SENTENCE 82 ---
The mass independent normalization constant for fish,  $i_0$ ,  $(\ln[i_0] = 18.47)$  was extracted from the plots in Brown et al.

--- SENTENCE 83 ---
[21], while those for basal and active microbial states  $(4.61 \times 10^{16})$  and  $1.08 \times 10^{21}$ , respectively) were calculated from previously reported individual prokaryote metabolic rate values [25].

--- SENTENCE 84 ---
The predicted scaling exponents ( $\alpha$ ) used for microbes were 1.72 (basal) and 1.96 (active)

--- SENTENCE 85 ---
[25], while 0.71 was used for fish [21].

--- SENTENCE 86 ---
The activation energies (E) used were 0.61 eV for microbes [25] and 0.69 eV for fish [21].

## Quantification of human impact

The level of human impact was assessed from the cumulative global human impact map generated by the National Center for Ecological Analysis and Synthesis (NCEAS; http://www.nceas.ucsb.edu/globalmarine/impacts).

--- SENTENCE 87 ---
Using ArcGIS 9.3, "NoData" pixels corresponding to the land mass of each island were identified and converted into polygon format.

--- SENTENCE 88 ---
A 10 km zone was

then calculated for each of these polygons, representing the immediate 10 km of sea surface around the border of each island in the study.

--- SENTENCE 89 ---
Using these zones, statistics were then performed on the NCEAS human impact raster in order to calculate the mean impact score.

--- SENTENCE 90 ---
These scores incorporate data related to: artisanal fishing; demersal destructive fishing; demersal non-destructive, high-bycatch fishing; demersal non-destructive low-bycatch fishing; inorganic pollution; invasive species; nutrient input; ocean acidification; benthic structures (e.g., oil rigs); organic pollution; pelagic high-bycatch fishing; pelagic low-bycatch fishing; population pressure; commercial activity (e.g., shipping); and anomalies in sea surface temperature and ultraviolet insolation.

--- SENTENCE 91 ---
Other indicators of reef system health were also considered in this study using benthic survey data collected at the same time as the microbial data.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
Benthic surveys were performed using the survey methodology described in Vargas-Angel [37–38].

--- SENTENCE 93 ---

### Estimation of net primary production

Productivity estimations for net primary productivity (NPP) (mg C m<sup>-2</sup> day<sup>-1</sup>) were derived from Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) satellite data using the Vertically Generalized Production Model (VGPM: http://www. science.oregonstate.edu/ocean.productivity/standard.product.

--- SENTENCE 126 ---
This model, based on an algorithm by Behrenfeld and Falkowski (1997) calculates net primary production from satellitebased measurements of surface chla concentrations, while also taking into account sea surface temperature, daily photosynthetically active radiation, and a temperature-dependent photosynthetic efficiency factor [42].

--- SENTENCE 127 ---
Because these satellite data sets are less accurate for near-shore measurements, the satellite-based NPP values used here were estimated from the data for a 50 km radius ring surrounding each island, with the first 10 km around each island removed.

--- SENTENCE 128 ---
The nearshore chla concentrations (µg l<sup>-1</sup>) used in this study were obtained using fluorometric analysis [43].

--- SENTENCE 129 ---
The chla samples were collected in conjunction with the microbial samples at each site.

Discussion**

### Predicted metabolic rates for the fish and microbes

Field surveys carried out at 99 coral reef sites at 29 Pacific islands (Fig. 1) were used to calculate the biomass (g per 10 m<sup>3</sup>) and basal metabolic rate (W per 10 m<sup>3</sup>) for both the water column-associated microbial and fish communities (Table 1).

--- SENTENCE 131 ---
The high and low values for microbial biomass occurred on the islands of Oahu (1.53 g per 10 m<sup>3</sup>) and Wake Atoll (0.12 g per 10 m<sup>3</sup>), respectively.

--- SENTENCE 132 ---
This difference in microbial biomass equates to a 76fold increase in the rate of energy flux (W per 10 m<sup>3</sup> or J sec<sup>-1</sup> 10 m<sup>-3</sup>) on Oahu (0.076 W per 10 m<sup>3</sup>) relative to Wake Atoll (0.001 W per 10 m<sup>3</sup>).

--- SENTENCE 133 ---
The highest fish biomass was found on Kingman (514.84 g per 10 m<sup>3</sup>) and the lowest on Guam (17.98 g per 10 m<sup>3</sup>).

--- SENTENCE 134 ---
The metabolic requirements predicted for the fish communities on Kingman and Guam were 0.015 and 0.002 W per 10 m<sup>3</sup>, respectively.

--- SENTENCE 135 ---
This difference equates to a 7.5-fold reduction in the metabolic requirements of the fish community.

--- SENTENCE 136 ---
The largest differences in the predicted metabolic rates between each island represent a 100-fold change for the microbes, as compared to a 14-fold change for the fish (Table 1).

# Microbialization scores versus the NCEAS human impact score

Based on the predicted metabolic rates for fish and microbes (Table 1), we are proposing a separate metric called the *microbialization score*, which represents the microbial share of the total predicted metabolic rate.

$$(W 10 \text{ m}^3_{microbes})/(W 10 \text{ m}^3_{microbes} + W 10 \text{ m}^3_{fish})

--- SENTENCE 139 ---
Although both increased microbial biomass and decreased fish biomass affect microbialization scores, microbial biomass has a proportionately greater impact on the combined predicted metabolic rate.

To further examine whether or not accounting for oceanographic context would improve our ability to predict reef microbialization, multiple linear regression analysis was performed.

--- SENTENCE 189 ---

--- SENTENCE 191 ---
The only variable that was significant was the NCEAS score, having a highly significant p-value in all of the models (p-value<0.0001).

--- SENTENCE 192 ---
The model which included both chla and NPP as variables (y =  $\beta_0 + \beta_1$ (NCEAS)+ $\beta_2$ (chla)+ $\beta_3$ (NPP)) gave a multiple R<sup>2</sup> value of 0.706, which was not a significant improvement over the simplest  $(y = \beta_0 + \beta_1(NCEAS))$  which explained 68.4% of the variability of the microbialization score.

--- SENTENCE 193 ---
Next, Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC) was used for model selection between the 4 different statistical models.

--- SENTENCE 194 ---
AIC is the most widely known and used model selection criterion which consists of a "goodness-of-fit" term and a "penalty" term for increased number of model parameters [46].

--- SENTENCE 195 ---
The model with the lowest AIC value is selected as the best model.

--- SENTENCE 196 ---
The model having the smallest AIC was the model which did not include additional variables ( $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 (NCEAS)$ ).

Although the exact mechanism(s) underlying

the *process* of microbialization remain unclear, these analyses support the hypothesis that human activities alter the energy budget of the reef system, specifically by altering the allocation of metabolic energy between microbes and macrobes.

--- SENTENCE 198 ---
The finding that microbialization scores did not significantly correlate with ocean net primary production, local chla concentrations, or the combined metabolic rate of the fish and microbial communities suggests that the microbialization score may be a powerful metric for comparing and assessing reef degredation, particularly at large spatial scales.

### **Conclusions**

Overfishing, eutrophication, and global climate change are important drivers of the global loss of coral reefs.

--- SENTENCE 216 ---
However, the precise mechanism(s) by which these perturbations lead to coral decline have remained elusive.

--- SENTENCE 217 ---
We and others have previously argued that human activities are favoring the coral reef-associated microbes at the expense of the macrobes, a process called *microbialization*.

--- SENTENCE 218 ---
The data presented here supports this hypothesis over a wide swath of Pacific coral reefs and suggests that microbialization is a general process of reef decline.

--- SENTENCE 219 ---
Although the exact mechanism(s) driving the process of microbialization remain unclear, the microbialization score provides a way to diagnose the degree of microbialization that has occurred on reefs.

--- SENTENCE 220 ---
Fish were the primary movers of energy in the most pristine

locations (i.e. fish were responsible for 97 and 87% of the total predicted metabolic rate on Wake (PRIA) and Kingman (PRIA), respectively) but made up only 3% of the total predicted metabolic rate on Oahu (MHI).

--- SENTENCE 221 ---
Microbialization scores reflect both increased microbial biomass and decreased fish biomass; however microbial biomass has a proportionately greater impact on metabolic rate.

--- SENTENCE 222 ---
This means that even a minor increase in the microbial load results in a substantial shift in community energy use; up to a 100-fold increase in the metabolic requirements of the microbes in the most heavily impacted reef systems.

--- SENTENCE 223 ---
This study has significant implications for the protection of coral reefs.

--- SENTENCE 224 ---
The degree of microbialization a reef is experiencing may be important for predicting its response to perturbation.

--- SENTENCE 225 ---
On Pacific coral reefs, microbialization may be set in motion by an increase in the percent cover of turf algae resulting from the loss of herbivorous fish.

--- SENTENCE 226 ---
Turf algae release large amounts of dissolved organic carbon (DOC) into the water column, a source of energy almost exclusively available to the microbes

--- SENTENCE 227 ---
Consequently, the process of microbialization is likely to have stabilization effects in the system once a castastophic regime shift to an algal-dominated state has occurred.

**Abstract**

 Benthic communities remain relatively healthy with moderate live coral cover, low macroalgae and no recently dead coral, and a small increase in live coral cover in 2008.

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
Core transects were repeated on 12 sites around Tutuila at 8 m depth on the reef slope.

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
Benthic cover categories including coral species, and invertebrates, were recorded.

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
Similar data were also recorded on reef flats at 12 sites, most of which corresponded with the reef slope sites.

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
Data was recorded separately on outer and inner reef flats at some of the sites.

--- SENTENCE 15 ---
Also, recording continued on the reef slopes at 4 and 18 m using the same methods as at 8 m, and all sites on the south side of Tutuila finished (plus one on the north side).

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
Baseline data was taken at 4 m depth in a particularly rich stand of table corals in the middle of Fagatele Bay, and at 16 m on Taema Banks near the green navigation buoy.

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
The Fagatele Bay site had nearly 75% live coral cover, but the Taema site had about 26% cover.

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
For the 12 core sites the mean coral cover was 30.5%, and for the original 11 sites 32.4%, slightly higher than the 27-28% seen in the previous three years.

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
This small increase in coral might be a real increase that is continuing recovery from a series of disturbances, the most damaging of which was the 1978 crown-of-thorns outbreak.

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
Or it could be due to some slightly changed transect locations.

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
The Key Reef Species Program has also recorded an increase in coral.

--- SENTENCE 22 ---
But annual changes have been smaller in this program than in the previous long-term monitoring by Birkeland and Green.

--- SENTENCE 23 ---
The larger amount of crustose calcareous algae on the south side than on the north continued as in previous years, and shows no trend.

--- SENTENCE 24 ---
A comparison of the western and eastern halves of the island show more turf on the western half, but this is because the three sites with the highest turf are all on the northwest part of the island.

--- SENTENCE 25 ---
Combined categories of turf + macroalgae, and CCA + coral, show no trends over the four years of the program.

--- SENTENCE 26 ---
The Live Coral Index was very slightly higher than in 2007, ending a slight decreasing trend that was likely due to increasing attention to recording older dead standing coral skeletons covered with algae.

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
But the live coral index continues to be at high levels, higher than the average for the Pacific, supporting the view that our reefs are healthy.

--- SENTENCE 28 ---
The increase in corallimorph at Tafeu that was recorded in Tafeu as happening in 2007 at the expense of turf stabilized in 2008 with little change.

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
In Nu'uuli, an increase in branching coralline algae that occurred between 2005 and 2007 at the expense of crustose calcareous algae (which was probably overgrown but still there and healthy) stabilized with no change in 2008.

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
Changes at other sites were very small and likely not real.

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
Examination of coral lifeforms revealed that there was more encrusting coral on the north side than on the south, which was the major cause of the higher coral cover on the north than the south.

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
There were more table corals on the north than the south.

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
Encrusting continued to be the most common coral lifeform, followed by column, branching, table, and massive in that order.

--- SENTENCE 34 ---
There was a small increase in the amount of branching lifeform corals in 2007 and 2008, and table corals increased in the same way.

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
There was more *Montipora* on the north side than south, which accounts for the larger amount of encrusting coral on the north than south because almost all *Montipora* is

encrusting.

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
There was more *Porites* on the north than south, but more *Acropora* on the south than north.

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
Montipora* was the most common genus, followed by *Porites, Acropora, Pavona, Pocillopora* and *Leptastrea*, in that order.

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
There were no dramatic changes in relative generic composition of the coral over the four years of the program.

--- SENTENCE 39 ---
Over the four years, *Porites* cover increased, while *

--- SENTENCE 40 ---
Acropora* increased in the last two years.

--- SENTENCE 41 ---
Encrusting *Montipora* species was more common on the north side than south, while *Porites rus* was a little more common on the north than south.

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
There was no trend in the number of coral species in transects over time, but there were more coral species on the south side than north side, and this was stable over the four years of the program.

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
Encrusting *Montipora* species remained the most common species, followed by *Porites rus*, and *Pavona varians*, in that order, there was no change in the relative proportion of the different species over the four years of the program.

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
The total *Porites rus* cover increased slightly over the four years, while massive *Porites* decreased.

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
*Montipora turgescens* is the only species to show a consistent downward trend over the four years of the program.

--- SENTENCE 46 ---
The Shannon-Weaver diversity index (H') was higher on the south than the north, just as the number of coral species was higher on the south than north.

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
Evenness (J) was also higher on the south than north, though the difference was smaller.

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
There was no trend over the four years of species diversity (H'), evenness (J), and the difference in diversity between north and south and the difference in evenness.

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
Outer reef flats had just over 20% coral cover, with turf dominating.

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
Utulei (Gataivai) was very unusual with very high coral cover.

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
There was a slight decrease in turf from 2007 to 2008.

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
The north side had more turf than the south side on the outer reef flat, as was the case in 2007.

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
The live coral index remained high for outer reef flats.

--- SENTENCE 54 ---
There were considerable changes in the benthic categories recorded at individual sites on the outer reef flats, because of the difficulty of relocating them accurately and the small number of transects so far.

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
Branching was the most common coral lifeform on the outer reef flats, followed by encrusting, and foliose in that order.

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
This was the same order as in 2007.

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
The proportion of different genera was the same as in 2007, with *Acropora* most common, followed by *Pocillopora*, *Montipora*, *Porites*, and *Pavona*, in that order.

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
The north side had more encrusting *Montipora*, *Acropora hyacinthus*, and *Porites cylindrica* on the outer reef flat, while the south side had more *Pocillopora verrucosa*, *Acropora aspera*, *Pavona frondifera*, and *Pocillopora damicornis*.

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
Overall on the outer reef flat, encrusting Montipora sp. were the most common, followed by *Pocillopora verrucosa, Acropora aspera, Pavona frondifera, Pocillopora damicornis, Acropora hyacinthus, Porites rus*, and *Porites cylindrica*, in that order.

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
There was a small increase in the Shannon-Weaver diversity index (H') on outer reef flats, which is likely to not be real.

--- SENTENCE 61 ---
Inner reef flats were dominated by turf, with rubble the second most common category.

--- SENTENCE 62 ---
There was an increase in rubble recorded from 2007 to 2008, but it was likely due to a small shift in transect locations.

--- SENTENCE 63 ---
The live coral index declined slightly but continues to be high.

--- SENTENCE 64 ---
The live coral index was low in Faganeanea in both 2007 and 2008, because low tides had killed coral on the reef flat there in earlier years.

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
Outer reef flats had more coral and less turf than inner reef flats.

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
Trends in benthic cover on the inner reef flat at individual sites was variable, likely due to small changes in transect locations.

--- SENTENCE 67 ---
In Leone, however, there was a massive decrease in sponge cover from nearly 90% to about 20%.

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
It appeared that the encrusting grey sponge that had covered nearly everything had largely disintegrated.

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
It was replaced by turf and rubble, which was probably underneath the sponge in the

previous year.

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
At 4 m depth, the sites on the south side plus Fagasa on the north averaged about 34% coral cover, with crustose coralline algae being the dominant cover category.

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
At 18 m deep, coral covered about 34% on the average with coralline algae covering slightly less.

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
When cover is compared across all zones, it is lowest on the inner reef flat, rises to the outer flat and then 4 m depth, then stays steady at 8 m and 18 m depths.

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
Coralline algae is greatest at 4 m depth and decreases with increasing depth, but is much less on the reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
Turf is greatest on the reef flat, but is steady at low levels on the slope.

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
Rubble is greatest on the inner flat, followed by outer flat, and very small amounts are on the slope at all depths.

--- SENTENCE 76 ---
The live coral index is high in all zones, but increases from inner reef flat to outer reef flat to slope, where it is constant and high at all depths.

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
Coral cover was particularly high at Faga'alu at 18 m depth, about 63%, while it was low at shallower depths where coralline algae grows on dead rubble that appears to be from staghorn.

--- SENTENCE 78 ---
At Fagatele, coral cover decreases with depth and is replaced by coralline algae.

--- SENTENCE 79 ---
Overall, branching lifeform corals are most abundant on the outer reef flat, foliose corals are most abundant at 18 m, and column is most abundant at 8 m. *Acropora* is most common at 4 m depth, *Montipora* most common at 8 m depth, *Porites* most common at 8 m, *Mycedium* most common at 18 m, and *Isopora* most common at 4 m. For invertebrates, there were more kinds of invertebrates recorded on north sites (at 8 m) than on the south.

--- SENTENCE 80 ---
The small urchin *Echinostrephus* was the most common invertebrate, followed by barnacles in coral, mysid shrimp, the orange sponge *Stylissa*, the encrusting sponge *Dysdea*, tiny orange didemnid sea squirt colonies, *Atriolum robustum*, tiny white didemnid colonies, and the colonial ascidian *Diplosoma simile*.

--- SENTENCE 81 ---
The total abundance of invertebrates recorded has risen steadily and sharply over the years, as more kinds were recognized and added to what was recorded.

--- SENTENCE 82 ---
Actual abundance has likely remained constant.

--- SENTENCE 83 ---
The abundance of invertebrates on the inner and outer reef flats, and the slopes at 4 m and 18 m is also reported.

--- SENTENCE 84 ---
Non-cryptic, diurnal macroinvertebrates are not only uncommon at 8 m depth on the slope, they are also uncommon on the reef flat and at 4 m and 18 m on the slope.

--- SENTENCE 85 ---
There appears to be a small increase in invertebrate abundance with distance from the shoreline.

--- SENTENCE 86 ---
Individual invertebrates show different abundance patterns across the zones.

--- SENTENCE 87 ---
The small urchin *Echinostrephus molaris* was most common on the outer reef flat followed by 4 m, then 8m, and not found at 18 m. The sponge *Styllisa* was found only on the reef slope, but roughly evenly with depth.

--- SENTENCE 88 ---
The chitons that produce structures in the crustose calcareous algae are present only on the outer reef flat or crest.

--- SENTENCE 89 ---
The sea cucumber *Stichopus chloronotus* was found only on the inner reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 90 ---
The sponge *Dysdea* was most common on the upper reef slope, and not present at 18 m or on the reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 91 ---
Alpheid shrimp cracks were only common at 8 m deep.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
Water visibility was greater in the south than the north.

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
There was no consistent trend in visibility over the years.

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
Attempts to find correlations between the impact ratings of watersheds and benthic variables were once again unsuccessful at finding any significant correlations.

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
If non-point pollution has effects on reef slope benthic communities, they are small.

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
The average coral cover reported from many relatively pristine reefs is between 35 and 40%, so while the present coral cover is slightly less than this, the 63% estimated before crown-of-thorns may be an unusually high level due to a lack of disturbances for many years.

--- SENTENCE 97 ---
There is published evidence that crown-of-thorns outbreaks are caused by nutrient runoff, and that reefs in no-take MPA's have fewer outbreaks.

The locations of some seagrass beds are documented.

A minor coral death event in the airport pool is documented, but the cause remains unknown.

--- SENTENCE 101 ---

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
The table tops grew from an average of 7.6 cm diameter in 2007 to 20.3 cm in 2008, and no mortality was seen.

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
A group of the table coral recruits on the outer reef flat of Fagasa were found to have rags caught on them.

--- SENTENCE 104 ---

--- SENTENCE 105 ---

# **Methods**

The original 11 core sites are shown in the map below.

--- SENTENCE 108 ---
All are on Tutuila and nearby Aunu'u.

!

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
The benthic methods were the same as in 2007, with a few minor changes.

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
In the core monitoring, four 50-m tapes were laid on a depth contour between 8 and 10 m deep.

--- SENTENCE 111 ---
A space between them of about 15 m was kept.

--- SENTENCE 112 ---
Benthic categories were recorded under each 0.5 m point on the tape.

--- SENTENCE 113 ---
Benthic categories included live coral, dead coral, dead coral with algae, crustose calcareous algae, branching coralline algae, fleshy macroalgae, turf algae, rock, sand, rubble, soft coral, and sponge.

--- SENTENCE 114 ---
"Branching coralline algae" included a soft feathery species that was the most common in that category.

--- SENTENCE 115 ---
That species is *Cheilosporum spectabile*.

--- SENTENCE 116 ---
Corals were identified to lifeform, genus, and species when possible, and if the macroalgae was *Halimeda* or *Dictyot*a, that was recorded.

--- SENTENCE 117 ---
Soft corals were recorded to genus when possible.

--- SENTENCE 118 ---
Lifeforms included encrusting, massive, foliose, branching, columnar, submassive, mushroom, *Millepora*, *Acropora* branching, *Acropora* table, *Acropora* digitate, and *Acropora* encrusting.

--- SENTENCE 119 ---
Horizontal visibility was recorded using the tape.

--- SENTENCE 120 ---
Two transect tapes were done on the first dive, and an additional two tapes were done on the second dive.

--- SENTENCE 121 ---
Invertebrates were recorded on a return pass.

--- SENTENCE 122 ---
Sites were re-located using the GPS and markers as indicated in the 2005 report.

--- SENTENCE 123 ---
One day was required for each site.

--- SENTENCE 124 ---
The same 11 core sites on Tutuila as recorded in 2005 were repeated, which are shown in the map above, plus a site in Masacre Bay on the northwest coast.

--- SENTENCE 125 ---
As in 2007, the rugosity measurements which were omitted, because a third team member was not available and when included it lengthened dive times to the point where running out of air was a distinct possibility, thus reducing the margin of safety.

--- SENTENCE 126 ---
Further, it appears that the measurement depends primarily on exactly where the chain falls, and that changes in rugosity caused by coral growth will take quite a few years before they would be detectable.

--- SENTENCE 127 ---
A hurricane could make changes in rugosity quickly by removing corals, and if significant hurricane damage occurs, the rugosity measurements can be repeated.

--- SENTENCE 128 ---
Until changes in coral cover or other rugosity changes are apparent, repeating the measurement of rugosity is not worth the increased risk of running out of air.

--- SENTENCE 129 ---
In future years it is hoped that an additional team member can record the rugosity measure.

--- SENTENCE 130 ---
When laying the tape, the primary consideration is to keep the tape between 8 m and 9 m deep.

--- SENTENCE 131 ---
The tape is passed along the sides of projections, including live corals such as *Pocillopora* and table corals, which usually have an overhanging side.

--- SENTENCE 132 ---
If it is passed around first one side of one projection and then the other side of another, it is anchored securely from wave action moving it either way at that point.

--- SENTENCE 133 ---
An attempt is made to anchor the tape in this fashion as often as possible, but in some areas there is little to anchor the tape on.

--- SENTENCE 134 ---
A continuing problem is what to do about clefts in the reef.

--- SENTENCE 135 ---
A cleft that is narrow and deep is crossed straight to an anchoring point on the other side.

--- SENTENCE 136 ---
If it is large, then the tape may be laid along one side of it, going up toward shallower water but staying at 8-9 m depth, and then when the bottom rises to that depth, crossing to the other side and continuing on that side out of the canyon.

--- SENTENCE 137 ---
The principle problem with that is finding an anchoring point near the head of the canyon that can hold the tape at the head.

--- SENTENCE 138 ---
The tape is read at each point by reading the substrate under the point at the time at which the diver is directly above the point.

--- SENTENCE 139 ---
A string and weight are not used, as surge and the movement of the tape in the surge makes that a much more difficult procedure.

--- SENTENCE 140 ---
If the tape is stretched between two points far apart and the surge is heavy, the tape can move a meter or more in either direction with each wave.

--- SENTENCE 141 ---
This opens up an opportunity for bias, as the point on the tape sweeps across a variety of benthic patches.

--- SENTENCE 142 ---
If the point on the bottom is recorded that is first seen from a vertical viewpoint, then bias is minimized.

--- SENTENCE 143 ---
An attempt is made to minimize bias in laying the tape by choosing a route based on depth and anchoring points for the tape, not the substrate.

--- SENTENCE 144 ---
The direct observation underwater of what is under points makes it easier to identify species, and so allows greater taxonomic resolution than video techniques.

--- SENTENCE 145 ---
In addition to the 11 core sites, one site was added at Masacre Bay on the northwest in 2007; that was continued in 2008.

 Work was also begun in 2007 to survey the benthic communities at 4 m and 18 m depth at each of the 12 core sites.

--- SENTENCE 170 ---
It was deemed a lower priority than collecting the annual core 8 m deep data, and so when weather, boat problems, boat driver shortages, etc occurred, there was not time to complete it.

--- SENTENCE 171 ---
It was continued in 2008 to complete the data set for 4 m and 18 m. Zonation is readily apparent at many sites.

--- SENTENCE 172 ---
Further, disturbances can easily affect one zone and not another, because a disease may afflict only one or a few species, or hurricane wave surge may only damage corals in shallow water or only damage delicate corals that are found in one zone but not another, or bleaching may kill one kind of coral that lives in one zone but not the coral in a different zone.

--- SENTENCE 173 ---
It is important to get a baseline of different depths and different zones, so when a disturbance occurs, it is possible to measure the effects even if they are not apparent at 8 m depth.

--- SENTENCE 174 ---
4 m was chosen as half way between 8 m and the surface.

--- SENTENCE 175 ---
In some locations like Nu'uuli, data at 4 m can only be taken in calm weather, and even in moderate weather it is near the limits of capability for a diver.

--- SENTENCE 176 ---
Shallower transects would not be safe in most years.

--- SENTENCE 177 ---
18 m was chosen as about twice as deep than the 8 m transect (which is actually set between 8 and 10 m deep, mostly set between 8 and 9 m deep, and so nominally probably best referred to as 8.5 m deep), and deep enough to be in a deep zone that is different from the 8 m zone.

--- SENTENCE 178 ---
At most sites the reef extends down below 18 m, but often not much farther before it joins the shelf.

--- SENTENCE 179 ---
For those sites where the reef does not extend to 18 m, the tapes are placed as deep as the reef extends.

--- SENTENCE 180 ---
The methods for 4 m and 18 m transects are similar to that at 8 m. However, in each dive, first one tape is done at 18 m, and then one at 4 m. A single tape is used, and the diver completely finishes at 18 m and picks up the tape before moving to 4 m depth.

--- SENTENCE 181 ---
The 4 m depth is a perfect safety stop depth, and with the time there makes it a safe dive.

--- SENTENCE 182 ---
Although two tapes at 18 m done consecutively would run some risk of running out of no-deco time or even air, done with first one 18m tape and then a second 4 m transect it is safe.

--- SENTENCE 183 ---
In one day, one such dive is done and then a one hour lunch break is taken between the two dives.

--- SENTENCE 184 ---
In the second dive, there is less time allowed at 18 m, but still enough to complete it safely.

--- SENTENCE 185 ---
The diver wears a spare air on all dives.

--- SENTENCE 186 ---
The rest of the method is the same, 50 m tape, data taken at half meter points, half meter wide belt invertebrates on the way back.

--- SENTENCE 187 ---
It takes one day to get a total of two tapes done at 18 m and two at 4 m.

--- SENTENCE 188 ---

--- SENTENCE 599 ---

## **Baselines**

 The 2007 report presented comparisons between the average live coral cover found in American Samoa (27%) and that in other places and other times.

--- SENTENCE 833 ---
The only record for American Samoa before a crown-of-thorns starfish outbreak in 1978, is data from Wass (1982) who gave estimates of coral cover for several sites.

--- SENTENCE 834 ---
Those estimates average 63%.

--- SENTENCE 835 ---
McManus et al (1995) collated coral cover reports from as many near-pristine coral reefs as they could find information on.

--- SENTENCE 836 ---
The average coral cover on those reefs was 40%.

--- SENTENCE 837 ---
Miller et al. (2008) report coral cover on six remote U.S. Pacific islands and reefs, and report an average coral cover of 35%.

--- SENTENCE 838 ---
Coral reefs have been found to undergo repeated cycles of natural disturbance (such as hurricane) followed by recovery.

--- SENTENCE 839 ---
A survey of many pristine reefs would include reefs in all stages of recovery, so the average would be less than the asymptotic maximum that can be reached long after the last disturbance.

--- SENTENCE 840 ---
Photographs of reefs being eaten by crown-of-thorns starfish in American Samoa in 1978 show lush growths of table and staghorn *Acropora*.

--- SENTENCE 841 ---
*Acropora* is among the most vulnerable corals to most major disturbances, such as crown-of-thorns starfish (it is preferred prey), bleaching, and hurricanes (delicate structures are easily damaged).

--- SENTENCE 842 ---
Thus, the 63% live coral cover recorded in 1978 is likely to be an unusually high coral cover for American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 843 ---
Average coral cover is more likely to be in the 35-40% range.

--- SENTENCE 844 ---
This is still higher than the current coral cover, so it is consistent with the conclusions drawn from the 63% coral cover reported before the outbreak in 1978, that recovery is not yet complete.

--- SENTENCE 845 ---
However, 63% is probably an unrealistically high goal to set for the reefs of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 846 ---
If 35-40% cover is a more realistic goal for coral cover, then our goal should be to have coral cover increase by about 8-13%.

#### **Algae, Nutrients, and crown of thorns starfish**

 If the observed blooms of brown macroalgae near shore and in a narrow bay are caused by increased nutrients, there is a second major threat.

--- SENTENCE 847 ---
That threat is of a crownof-thorns outbreak.

--- SENTENCE 848 ---
Birkeland (1982; 1989) hypothesized that increased nutrient runoff from high islands produced by unusually heavy rains following dry spells can fuel plankton blooms.

--- SENTENCE 849 ---
Plankton blooms in turn could provide food for the pelagic larvae of crown-of-thorns starfish.

--- SENTENCE 850 ---
In outbreaks, these starfish appear suddenly as large numbers of adults on reefs.

--- SENTENCE 851 ---
The newly settled larval starfish live in holes in the reef and cannot be seen.

--- SENTENCE 852 ---
Eventually as they grow they begin to come out at night and feed on corals.

--- SENTENCE 853 ---
Only as adults are they visible by day.

--- SENTENCE 854 ---
It takes about 3 years for them to grow to the point that they stay out during the day.

--- SENTENCE 855 ---
Birkeland was able to show that outbreaks don't occur on low islands (where there would not be nutrient runoff) and there is a correlation between outbreaks and unusual rainstorms following dry periods, with a lag of three years.

--- SENTENCE 856 ---
Recently, the information was reviewed for the outbreaks on the Great Barrier Reef, and strong evidence supporting each of the links in the logical chain were found except one for which evidence is lacking (Brodie et al. 2005).

--- SENTENCE 857 ---
Clearly, if terrestrial nutrient runoff increases substantially, it could fuel plankton blooms that could feed starfish larvae.

--- SENTENCE 858 ---
Thus, there is some risk with increasing nutrient runoff of encouraging a crown-of-thorns outbreak.

--- SENTENCE 859 ---
The last outbreak, in 1978, ate almost all corals on the reefs except on the reef crest where the starfish cannot withstand the heavy wave surge (the only other known

outbreak was in 1938 (Flanigan and Lamberts, 1981).

--- SENTENCE 860 ---
Thus, an outbreak has very serious consequences for the reef.

--- SENTENCE 861 ---
However, water on the reef slopes remains relatively clear with no obvious signs of a plankton bloom.

--- SENTENCE 862 ---
An EPA study documented ocean oligotrophic nutrient levels on the reef slopes, though the sampling wasn't done to coincide with major runoff events.

--- SENTENCE 863 ---
The harbor, however, still has dense plankton as documented in the 2007 report.

--- SENTENCE 864 ---
It appears likely that significant additional increases in nutrient runoff on the slopes would be required to produce a plankton bloom sufficient to have a major risk of a crown-of-thorns outbreak, but any increase likely carries with it some amount of increased risk.

--- SENTENCE 865 ---
In another recent study, Sweatman (2008) found that there were fewer crown-ofthorns outbreaks on the middle reefs of the Great Barrier Reefs which were in no-take areas than in reefs where fishing is allowed.

--- SENTENCE 866 ---
The link between fishing and crown-ofthorns is not known for sure, but the fishing on these reefs targets groupers exclusively.

--- SENTENCE 867 ---
He speculates that removal of predatory fish could lead to increases of smaller fish such as wrasses that might eat the juvenile crown-of-thorns hiding in reef holes.

--- SENTENCE 868 ---
However, this should predict fewer starfish in areas with fishing.

#### **Red Tides in the Harbor**

 A series of red tides have been observed in the harbor.

--- SENTENCE 869 ---
They involve a clear rust-red discoloration of the water, usually in patches, which change in location with time during the event.

--- SENTENCE 870 ---
They are produced by the dinoflagellate protist (single cell) algae, *Ceratium furca*.

--- SENTENCE 871 ---
No dead fish have been sighted during these events, nor smell or irritation reported by people.

--- SENTENCE 872 ---
They appear to be non-toxic.

--- SENTENCE 873 ---
Most of the red tides are visible in the inner harbor from about Fagatogo to the head of the harbor.

--- SENTENCE 874 ---
The dates when the author observed red tides are:

June 20, 2007 Aug 15, 2007 Sept 22, 2007 Feb 26, 2009-April 18, 2009 Mike King reported seeing red tide in the harbor in 2000.

--- SENTENCE 875 ---
The cause of the red tides is not known.

--- SENTENCE 876 ---
One possibility is nutrients.

--- SENTENCE 877 ---
Although nutrient levels in the harbor have decreased since the cannery wastes have been diverted to the mouth of the harbor (Craig et al. 2005), the harbor waters are still green and quite turbid at the head of the harbor.

--- SENTENCE 878 ---
This indicated high phytoplankton levels in the inner harbor, with are due to nutrient inputs and low levels of flushing.

--- SENTENCE 879 ---
Small oysters along the shoreline feed on the plankton and are a good bioindicator of the plankton, and are most abundant in the inner harbor.

--- SENTENCE 880 ---
The phytoplankton as measured by water transparency, is an even more direct bioindicator of nutrients.

--- SENTENCE 881 ---
It is likely that the red tide events are fueled by the nutrients.

--- SENTENCE 882 ---
High levels of nitrogen are correlated with patches of red tide (Don Vargo, personal comm.).

--- SENTENCE 883 ---
Recent increases in nutrient inputs include fertilizer applied to a soccer field in Pago Pago, and the use of high-phosphate detergent imported from Asia.

--- SENTENCE 884 ---
The governor signed and Executive Order banning the import of such

detergents, but enforcement has initially been lacking.

--- SENTENCE 885 ---
The details of the red tide story are given in the 2007 report (Fenner, 2008b).

#### **Seagrass**

 The only seagrass observed by the author was *Halophila* sp.

--- SENTENCE 887 ---
This seagrass produces very small blades, perhaps 2 cm long and 1 cm wide, on widely dispersed stolons.

--- SENTENCE 888 ---
It takes careful observation to even see a seagrass bed, and because of the small size and low density of the grass, it does not appear to be habitat for any other organisms.

--- SENTENCE 889 ---
It was observed at Faga'alu in front of the park, starting at the shoreline in very shallow water and going down on sand to perhaps 3-5 m depth.

--- SENTENCE 890 ---
It was also observed in Alofau on the sand bed near shore out to a depth of 1-2 m. In Alofau, a species of *Caulerpa* is in the same sandy area, and careful observation is needed to distinguish the two.

--- SENTENCE 891 ---
The author also observed seagrass on a sandy bed at the base of the reef in Fagatele at about 30 m depth.

--- SENTENCE 892 ---
Thus it has a depth range of at least 0-30 m. It was also seen near the shore in the deeper area ("borrow pit") of the Aua transect, and on the sand on the reef flat at Matafau School.

--- SENTENCE 893 ---
It is likely to be many other places, but it would require careful survey of sand beds to find it.

--- SENTENCE 894 ---
Two species of *Halophila* are reported and illustrated in Skelton (2003).

--- SENTENCE 895 ---
Ellison (2009) reports that there are three species of seagrass in American Samoa, but the source of the information for the third species (*Syringodium isoetifolium*) is not clear, as the author has not seen it or heard of it locally.

--- SENTENCE 896 ---
Upolu, (independent) Samoa, has larger seagrass (*Cymodocea serratulata* and/or *Syringodium isoeiifolium*) at the eastern end (species from Ellison, 2009).

#### **Major Disturbances**

 On May 18, 2008, observation of the airport backreef pool to record bleaching revealed that there was a large amount of white staghorns in the outer part of the pool.

--- SENTENCE 897 ---
Subsequent observation revealed that the white coral was almost entirely dead, not just bleached.

--- SENTENCE 898 ---
By one week later the grey-green color of filamentous algae growing on it was obvious, and by two weeks it was sufficiently dark grey to be less obvious.

--- SENTENCE 899 ---
Thus, the death would have had to have occurred between May 18 and the previous data when it was checked, April 27.

--- SENTENCE 900 ---
The fact that it was still very white on May 18, but filamentous algae was obvious within a few days indicates that it likely died just a few days before May 18.

--- SENTENCE 901 ---
The area was visually assessed and the dead coral was about 22% of the staghorn coral in that outer portion of the pool.

--- SENTENCE 902 ---
The inner portion of the pool had no dead coral.

--- SENTENCE 903 ---
Further, nearly all coral that was not killed was a healthy dark brown.

--- SENTENCE 904 ---
It was the end of the bleaching season and the amount of bleaching was in the process of rapidly decreasing.

--- SENTENCE 905 ---
This event occurred at a time of unusually calm weather, with no wind most days, and waves had been decreasing to the point that they were unusually small and very little if any water would have been pumped over the crest into the pool and there would have been no current in the pool.

--- SENTENCE 906 ---
There had been a period of about 2 weeks of unusually calm weather preceding the event.

--- SENTENCE 907 ---
Calm conditions are known to contribute to bleaching.

--- SENTENCE 908 ---
However, it was at a time when the corals were recovering from bleaching, and no newly bleached corals were found.

--- SENTENCE 909 ---
The corals would have had to bleach and die completely in a very short period, too short to seem realistic.

--- SENTENCE 910 ---
Another possibility is that the lack of wave action would have meant that no new water would have been flowing through the pool bringing oxygen.

--- SENTENCE 911 ---
Cloudy weather would have meant that the corals would have produced lower amounts of oxygen in the day, and at night oxygen levels might have fallen low.

--- SENTENCE 912 ---
Perhaps low oxygen kills in an all-or nothing fashion that could account for the pattern.

--- SENTENCE 913 ---
A similar phenomenon happened in 2006 when three weeks of cloudy weather and still conditions lead to heavy silt runoff and arrested circulation in Faga'alu Bay.

--- SENTENCE 914 ---
Staghorns there died at that time around the bases of clumps, so it wasn't due to fresh water, which floats.

--- SENTENCE 915 ---
In the 2006 report the deaths were attributed to sediment.

--- SENTENCE 916 ---
The clouds decreased light levels, and sediment would have decreased the light reaching coral further, and decreased it most for deep corals.

--- SENTENCE 917 ---
This cannot be used to explain the coral deaths in the airport pool this year because the water is clear- there is no sediment input.

--- SENTENCE 918 ---
Low oxygen levels might be able to explain both.

--- SENTENCE 919 ---
However, oxygen levels have been recorded in the backreef pools at Ofu, and they don't go especially low even at night at low tide when there is no wave input into the pools.

--- SENTENCE 920 ---
However, this was during a period when every 12 hours at high tide there was oxygenated water coming in from waves.

--- SENTENCE 921 ---
More investigation will be required to reach firm conclusions about the cause of these deaths.

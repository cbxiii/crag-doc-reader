### Introduction

ount socu Fishes are an important resource in tropical shallow waters, especially in developing countries where low-income fishers with few options for employment need to make a living.

--- SENTENCE 4 ---
Although many South Pacific island communities have shifted from subsistence to cash-based economies, fishing remains an important subsistence and commercial venture (Munro 1984).

--- SENTENCE 5 ---
As island populations increase and economies become less stable (SPC 1994), maintaining viable fish stocks becomes even more important.

--- SENTENCE 6 ---
Many small islands in the South Pacific are undergoing intense population growth (SPC 1994, UNDP 1994).

--- SENTENCE 7 ---
With this growth comes increased pressure on the coral reef ecosystem not only from overfishing, but also from various types of habitat degradation (Maragos 1993, Craig this volume).

--- SENTENCE 8 ---
Where soils are frequently disturbed, as buildings and farms keep pace with growing populations, sedimentation poses a great threat to coral reef communities.

--- SENTENCE 9 ---
Turbidity reduces available light, recruitment of

coral larvae is inhibited, and growth is slower in areas of high sediment loading (Rogers 1990).

--- SENTENCE 10 ---
Indirectly, in response to the alteration and loss of habitat, the coral reef fishes are affected as well.

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
Most South Pacific island communities have limited human and monetary resources to direct into fisheries research and management.

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
In order to most effectively use the resources at hand, it is important to know in what direction management efforts should proceed.

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
This paper describes a declining coral reef fishery in American Samoa, emphasizing the indicators that help to identify the causes of decline.

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
Data are used from an assessment of the fishery in 1979 (Wass 1980), and from a monitoring program conducted since 1991.

--- SENTENCE 15 ---
The data are used to address two questions: (1) What is the cause of the large decline in the fishery, and (2) What is an appropriate management direction?

The inshore fishery was monitored on the main island of Tutuila (14°S, 171°W), American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
It is a high island, 29 km long and an average of 4 km wide with a total land area of 135 sq. km.

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
Nearly 70% of the land on the island has a slope of greater than 30% (Volk et al. 1992).

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
Rainfall on Tutuila is heavy, averaging 320 - 500 cm a year.

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
The human population of American Samoa is approximately 55,000, and growing at a rate of 3.7% per year.

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
The economy of Tutuila is dominated by two employers: two tuna canneries and the local government, which employ 33% and 32% of the labor force, respectively (EDPO 1991).

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
Most of the food, fuel, and materials on island are imported (EDPO 1991).

--- SENTENCE 22 ---

Nearly 55 km of the 125 km coastline of Tutuila (44%) is bordered by 90 - 300m wide fringing reefs (Volk et al. 1992).

--- SENTENCE 23 ---
The reefs are partially exposed at low tide and most have seaward running channels (ava), where water is drained from the reef flats.

--- SENTENCE 24 ---
Coral reefs are an important natural resource in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 25 ---
Not only are they important habitat for fishes, but for traditional and recreational activities as well.

--- SENTENCE 26 ---
Fish and shellfish are harvested most days of the year on the reef surrounding Tutuila Island.

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
Compared to other fisheries on Tutuila (pelagics, bottomfish, and tournament), the inshore fishery makes up more than 50% of the landings (Craig et al. 1993).

--- SENTENCE 28 ---
In recent years, there have been numerous destructive impacts on coral reefs in Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
Natural events include a crown-of-thorns starfish infestation in 1978, hurricanes in 1987, 1990, and 1991, which reduced much of the coral to rubble, and a massive coral bleaching event in 1994, presumably due to high sea temperatures.

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
Live coral cover has dropped from 60% in 1979 (Wass 1980) to 3-13% in 1993 (Maragos et al. 1994).

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
Added to this are continuing land-

based human-induced impacts such as eutrophication and sedimentation which inhibit recovery of the coral reef ecosystem.

The fishery was assessed in 1979 (Wass 1980) and has been monitored weekly since 1991 by the Department of Marine and Wildlife Resources (Ponwith 1991, McConnaughey 1993, Saucerman 1994).

--- SENTENCE 34 ---

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
There are 7 principle gear types in the fishery: rod and reel, handline, bamboo pole, throw nets, gillnets, spear diving, and gleaning.

## Other Fishery Indicators: Species Composition and Size Frequencies

Information on catch and effort, species composition, and other biological characteristics provides clues to identifying problems in a fishery.

--- SENTENCE 90 ---
The catch and effort data show an overall decline and suggest that immediate management steps are needed to alleviate pressure on the fishery.

--- SENTENCE 91 ---
However, there are other signs in the fishery that indicate it is not overfished.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
Overfishing and habitat degradation can be distinguished by using indicators in the fishery.

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
Common indicators of overfishing include increases in effort along with drops in catch, reductions in biomass, in overall CPUE, in relative abundance of large predatory species, and in size of fisheries species (Munro and Williams 1985, Russ 1991).

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
When data show decreases in CPUE and biomass, but none of the other problems associated with overfishing, the cause of decline might be habitat degradation.

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
Indicators of habitat degradation include lower abundances of fishes and decreases in species which rely on the structural complexity provided by healthy coral reefs (Carpenter et al. 1981).

--- SENTENCE 96 ---

on: onc

Of the indicators of overfishing listed above, decreases in total catch and in CPUE are the only examples observed in our fishery.

--- SENTENCE 97 ---
Classic indication of overfishing is a decrease in catch coincident with an increase in effort.

--- SENTENCE 98 ---
Our data show that even though effort has gone down, there is still a large decline in

total catch.

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
It is also expected that where an area is overfished there will be a decline in relative abundance of large, desirable fishes (Munro and Williams 1985, Russ 1991).

--- SENTENCE 100 ---
However, relative abundance of species has changed little in the fishery from 1979 to 1994 (Fig. 5).

--- SENTENCE 101 ---
The species groups presented in figure 5 do not include invertebrates or migratory species such as bigeye scad.

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
For serranids (groupers), lutjanids (snappers), and lethrinids (emperors), which are considered to be desirable species (Russ 1991), proportions in the catch either stayed the same of increased from 1979 to 1994.

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
Acanthurids (surgeonfishes and unicornfishes), which are relatively small herbivores and planktivores, made up the bulk of the catch both years, though less in proportion in 1994.

--- SENTENCE 104 ---
Another common indicator of overfishing is a decline in size structure of fisheries species (Munro and Williams 1985, Russ 1991).

--- SENTENCE 105 ---
This has not occurred for at least one important species, the bluelined surgeonfish (Acanthurus lineatus), which makes up approximately 30% of the commercial artisanal fishery (Saucerman, unpubl. data).

--- SENTENCE 106 ---
Length frequency data for this species from 1987 to 1995 show that the average size has not declined over nine years (Fig. 6, Craig in prep.), but has actually increased slightly.

These data do not strongly demonstrate that overfishing is the cause of the decline in the fishery.

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
However, there is evidence in the fish community structure that suggests that habitat degradation is important.

--- SENTENCE 111 ---
Habitat degradation is indicated by the response of fish communities and species to loss -of live coral cover (Russ 1991).

--- SENTENCE 112 ---
For example, abundances of fishes that feed on live coral decrease in coral reef areas that have been damaged (Russ and Alcala 1989).

--- SENTENCE 113 ---
Additionally, overall abundance of reef fishes has been correlated with structural complexity (Carpenter et al. 1981).

--- SENTENCE 114 ---
It is often small, non-fisheries species that display changes in abundance in response to habitat degradation.

--- SENTENCE 115 ---
In American Samoa, there has been a change in relative abundances of fish species over the last 15 years (Green in prep.).

--- SENTENCE 116 ---
Green (in prep.) found that a damselfish species closely associated with live corals, Dick's damsel (Plectroglyphidodon dickii), decreased dramatically in number of individuals per ha from 1980 to 1994.

--- SENTENCE 117 ---
In the same study, the princess damsel (Pomacentrus vaiuli), a species closely associated with coral rubble, showed a striking increase in numbers.

### Management options

lunc

noo

Clearly there are warning signs for the inshore fishery of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 118 ---
There are decreases in total harvest biomass, and in CPUE across the board for gear types employed in the fishery.

--- SENTENCE 119 ---
There is ample indication that management steps need to be taken in

order to allow the fishery to recover, but it does not appear that the problem is overfishing.

--- SENTENCE 120 ---
There is, however, substantial evidence of wide-spread habitat destruction of the reefs in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 121 ---
This evidence is seen not only in the loss of live coral cover, but also in the responses of some fish species to that loss.

--- SENTENCE 122 ---
The coral reefs of Tutuila Island have been heavily damaged by hurricanes, crown-of-thorns starfish, and bleaching events.

--- SENTENCE 123 ---
Heavy anthropogenic sediment loading may hinder recovery from these natural disturbances.

--- SENTENCE 124 ---
Although it is not yet quantified, this sediment is clearly visible as large plumes in most bays after heavy rainfall, and on the reefs under water most of the time.

--- SENTENCE 125 ---
Turbidity reduces light for photosynthesis by zooxanthellae in the tissues of corals and other organisms.

--- SENTENCE 126 ---
Recruitment of coral larvae is affected adversely, as the larvae cannot settle on loose sediment (Birkeland 1977 in Rogers 1990).

--- SENTENCE 127 ---
Corals are also prone to bleaching and suffocation (Rogers 1990), and have slower growth (Randall and Myers 1983) in areas of high sedimentation.

Standard fisheries management measures such as size limits, closed seasons and areas, and gear restrictions are of little value when the fishery habitat is continually degraded.

--- SENTENCE 132 ---
With habitat degradation there are drops in fish abundance, and in species that rely on living coral for food or protection, regardless of fishing activity.

--- SENTENCE 133 ---
In situations such as exist in American Samoa it might be more beneficial to turn our management and research energies towards habitat conservation.

--- SENTENCE 134 ---
The steep topography of Tutuila renders the reefs particularly vulnerable to sedimentation.

--- SENTENCE 135 ---
The amount of sediment runoff entering coastal areas depends on watershed size and slope, volume of rainfall, soil type and condition, and land use practices (Hubbard 1987).

--- SENTENCE 136 ---
Tutuila is a very steep island and most of the flat-land areas are already inhabited.

--- SENTENCE 137 ---
It is likely that as the population grows, the steeper areas will be developed and farmed, increasing the sedimentation on the reefs.

--- SENTENCE 138 ---
A primary management objective is to identify the major causes of reef degradation and to determine which of these can be mitigated.

--- SENTENCE 139 ---
Sedimentation from land-based activities appears to be one of the largest potential sources of human-induced reef degradation in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 140 ---
Research should be directed towards establishing that sediment adversely affects the coral reef habitat and associated fish communities in American Samoa.

### Conclusion

10

It is important to make sound management decisions in any fishery, but particularly where the livelihoods of many people depend upon it.

--- SENTENCE 150 ---
Where overfishing is a problem, regulations such as closed areas, closed seasons, gear limitations and size limits may be appropriate.

--- SENTENCE 151 ---
Where reef degradation is important, it is more appropriate to locate the source and effects of the degradation, and to minimize further damage.

--- SENTENCE 152 ---
American Samoa is facing a rapidly growing population supported by few economic resources.

--- SENTENCE 153 ---
Coral reefs play an important role in traditional Samoan culture and supply a large percentage of all locally caught fish (Craig et al. 1993).

--- SENTENCE 154 ---
With the high human population and economic instability, coral reef resources may become more important in years to come.

--- SENTENCE 155 ---
However, along with the increasing population comes more pressure on the coral reef ecosystem, particularly in the form of proliferating impacts from poor land-use practices.

--- SENTENCE 156 ---
I suggest that habitat degradation may be

the main limiting factor in the inshore fishery and that conservation of the reef environment is essential to managing the fisheries.

### Summary

1 lun

1 CO/

On Tutuila Island, in American Samoa, we have monitored the inshore fishery since 1991.

--- SENTENCE 157 ---
Although there has been a decline in CPUE and total catch, we are not detecting many of the classic "red flags" that signify overfishing.

--- SENTENCE 158 ---
In recent years, however, some major environmental catastrophes occurred which caused a great deal of reef degradation.

--- SENTENCE 159 ---
Under normal conditions we would expect the reefs to recover from these environmental events.

--- SENTENCE 160 ---
However, added to these natural disasters are human induced impacts which inhibit normal recovery of the reef habitat.

--- SENTENCE 161 ---
If degradation of reefs persists, fisheries yields will probably decline no matter what other regulatory management measures are implemented.

--- SENTENCE 162 ---
Some of the data indicate that habitat degradation is the cause of the decline in the fishery.

--- SENTENCE 163 ---
Management measures, therefore, should be directed towards reducing the magnitude of human impact on the reefs, especially sedimentation.

--- SENTENCE 164 ---
In order to accomplish this we are attempting to assess the fisheries problems related to sedimentation, and to work with the agencies responsible for controlling this impact.

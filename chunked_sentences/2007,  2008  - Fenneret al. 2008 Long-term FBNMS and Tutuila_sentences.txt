# **Executive Summary**

### Corals:

- The main finding of our survey of the corals in Fagatele Bay is that they are still resilient (capable of recovering from damage) and doing quite well.

--- SENTENCE 2 ---
The abundance and population density of corals (14.5 colonies per m2 ) and the living coral cover (64%) in Fagatele Bay in August 2007 are typical of very healthy reefs.

--- SENTENCE 3 ---
We encountered 94 species within the areas specifically within our transects, a small part of Fagatele Bay.

--- SENTENCE 4 ---
A total of 164 species of corals were previously recorded from Fagatele Bay, but this latest survey found yet another four species not previously recorded: *Acropora formosa, Montipora hispida*, *Fungia concinna* and *Fungia klunzingeri*, bringing the total number of species in Fagatele Bay to 168.

--- SENTENCE 5 ---
The number of species in Fagatele Bay is not yet decreasing.
-

--- SENTENCE 6 ---
The size distribution of coral colonies in Fagatele Bay also indicates a healthy situation with a few large colonies and an abundance of smaller colonies, indicating a healthy recruitment.

--- SENTENCE 7 ---
However, some of the smaller colonies are of species that do not usually grow large, e.g., *Porites* sp. 2 and *Galaxea fascicularis*.

--- SENTENCE 8 ---
The numerical pattern of relative abundance of species, the relative abundance of coral species representing the different families of corals, and the population densities of corals along the depth distribution are all fairly constant between 2004 and 2007, indicating that the population has reached a temporary equilibrium as a relatively healthy coral community.

--- SENTENCE 9 ---
The proportion of corals in the smallest size category (< 5 cm diameter) has decreased and the proportion in the higher size classes have increased since 1995, showing that the coral community has grown in size distribution over the past decade.
-

--- SENTENCE 10 ---
The corals in Pago Pago Harbor were surveyed both along the permanent transect in front of Aua village and also in front of the Sadie's by the Sea Hotel.

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
There has been successful recruitment and growth of some colonies of *Acropora hyacinthus* at both sites.

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
The coral cover typical of a fully normal and healthy reef community has returned to the area of the Aua transect wherever there are solid substrata, i.e., on the reef crest and on large coral blocks on the reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
The dominant species on the Aua reef crest is *Acropora nana* because of a major successful recruitment in 1999.
- Surveys of Fagatele Bay should be shifted back to March/April for a better chance of having enough days with good sea state to get the surveys accomplished.

### Benthic Communities

- Percent cover of all algae has decreased from a high in 1985.

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
Diversity also appears to have decreased.

--- SENTENCE 15 ---
No obvious patterns are apparent in this survey year with respect to depth or position with FBMS.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
*Hydrolithon onkodes* appears to be the most common species of crustose coralline algae in FBMS and this is similar to what was reported in 1985.
- Higher coral cover and lower turf cover at the Aua site compared with the Rainmaker site appear to indicate that the sites are recovering at different rates.

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
The high

 Benthic communities at Fagasa appear to represent typical high disturbance communities with moderate coral cover, high encrusting algal species, moderate turf/fleshy algae species.

### Fish communities

- Changes in fish communities over time may be partly due to changes in coral communities as a result of habitat destruction by cots, hurricanes etc.

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
This mostly affects small specialized species that are closely associated with *Acropora* and *Pocillopora* corals (eg. *Plectroglyphidon dicki* – see Green 2002).

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
- Changes in density (particularly peaks in abundance in 1985 and 2001) were primarily due to surgeonfishes (see Fig 10, Green et al 2005).

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
These pulses are associated with mass recruitment events of *C. striatus* (Birkeland et al 1987; Green et al 2005).
- Coral reef fish communities in FB and elsewhere around Tutuila are still showing signs of overfishing.

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
Several large species that are characteristic of unfished reefs in the Indo-Pacific remain conspicuous by their absence or small size.

--- SENTENCE 22 ---
They include species such as maori wrasse (*Chelinus undulatus*), sharks, and larger species of serranids and scarids, all of which are known to be particularly vulnerable to fishing.

### **INTRODUCTION**

The coral reefs of American Samoa have suffered many destructive events in the last few decades, including a major outbreak of the corallivorous starfish *

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
Acanthaster planci*, several hurricanes varying from devastating to relatively mild, periods of high water temperature, and exposure during unusually low tides (Birkeland et al 1996, 2003, 2004, 2008, Green 1996, 1997, 2002, Green et al 1999).

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
The reefs in some locations, such as Pago Pago Harbor, have also been subjected to chronic human influences including sedimentation, eutrophication, pollution and overfishing (Birkeland et al, 1996, 2003, 2004, Green 1996, 1997, 2002, Green et al 1997).

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
As a direct result of these disturbances, the reefs of Fagatele Bay National Marine Sanctuary (FBMNS) and other locations around Tutuila Island have undergone some major changes in the last 30 years (Birkeland et al 1996, 2003, 2008, Green et al 1997, 2005).

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
Of particular concern have been the physical and biological changes to the coral communities in some locations.

--- SENTENCE 39 ---
Coral communities provide important habitat for reef fishes and changes in their condition may have important consequences for the associated fish fauna.

--- SENTENCE 40 ---
The coral reef communities of Fagatele Bay and other sites around Tutuila have been the subjects of a long term monitoring program since the late 1970's, supported by the Fagatele Bay National Marine Sanctuary program.

--- SENTENCE 41 ---
The purpose of the ongoing monitoring of coral reef communities is to document the recovery, or further decline, of coral and fish communities in Fagatele Bay, and elsewhere around Tutuila, and to determine if reef communities have changed substantially since the combined effects of *A. planci* outbreaks and hurricanes that devastated American Samoa reefs in the past 30 years.

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
The results of the monitoring program have been used to describe the changes in the coral and fish communities of American Samoa during the 26 years from 1978-2004 (Birkeland et al 1987, 1994, 1996, 2003, 2004, Green et al 1999).

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
This report documents the results from surveys in Fagatele Bay National Marine Sanctuary conducted in November, 2007 and May, 2008.

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
In 1917, Alfred Mayor established the permanent transect across the reef flat in front of Aua village and it has become the oldest long-term coral-reef quantitative transect in the Pacific Ocean.

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
In August 2007, with our assistance, the U.S. Geodetic Survey established an official benchmark at the offshore end of the transect.

--- SENTENCE 46 ---
We replicated another survey of the corals and other aspects of the benthic community on its 90th year.

### **METHODS**

### **Survey techniques – Fagatele Bay National Marine Sanctuary**

### *Corals*

From 1985 to 2001, corals were surveyed using point-quarter method (Birkeland et al 1987).

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
In 2004, the coral survey method was changed to belt transects because they produce a much larger sample in an equivalent time period than the point-quarter method (Green et al 2005).

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
This method is also directly compatible with archipelago-wide surveys (Mundy, 1996; Fisk

and Birkeland, 2002).

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
Belt transect methods were used to survey coral communities in 2007/2008.

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
We recorded all corals whose center occurred in a 0.5 m wide belt transect laid perpendicular to four of the six permanent transect lines in FBNMS (Transects 2-5, Figure 1).

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
Transect tapes were 20 m long, but on occasion dive time did not allow the completion of the full 20 m. When that happened, the length that was recorded was noted and the area adjusted accordingly.

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
Each coral was identified to species, and the maximum diameter measured.

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
Corals were then categorized into one of seven size classes based on the maximum diameter of the colony; <5cm, 5-10cm, 10-20cm, 20-40cm, 40-80cm, 80-160cm, and >160cm.

--- SENTENCE 54 ---
We collected data at Sites 2 to 5, at 3, 6, 9, and 12 m depth.

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
Weather and time did not allow collecting data on transects 1 and 6.

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
Coral data were collected in November, 2007 by Charles Birkeland and Douglas Fenner, and May, 2005 by Douglas Fenner.

### *Benthos*

Few benthic surveys have been conducted in Fagatele Bay and for those that have been undertaken, methods, sampling intensity and time of year have all varied.

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
These differences make it very difficult to draw conclusions about changes over time, especially for the algae, which are typically seasonal and show changes in diversity and biomass throughout the year.

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
Therefore the goal in this survey was to provide data that could be easily compared to previous studies and in particular, the study undertaken by Green et al in 2004, where benthic organisms were identified to functional form.

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
Consequently, for the purposes of this study, benthic organisms were also identified to functional form.

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
Additionally, macroalgae were identified to the lowest taxonomic level possible, with the exception of crustose coralline algae (CCA) and turf.

--- SENTENCE 61 ---
Benthic surveys were undertaken for Fagatele Bay, Pago Pago Harbor (at Rainmaker and Aua) and for Fagasa (at Cape Larsen and Sita Bay).

--- SENTENCE 62 ---
Following the method established by Green et al (2004), forty-eight points were sampled along the 30 m fish belt transects.

--- SENTENCE 63 ---
The substrate under the tape was identified every two meters and the substrate at a point approximately 1 m on either side of, and perpendicular to the tape at 1m was also identified (NB: except at Fagasa, where three 30 m transects were laid end to end, for a total of 144 points per transect).

--- SENTENCE 64 ---
The benthos was classified into one of the following 23 categories:

Corals (branching, digitate, encrusting, foliaceous, massive, mushroom, plate) Macroalgae (encrusting, blue green, Halimeda, macroalgae, crustose coralline algae - CCA) Invertebrate (ascidian, clam, hydrozoan, soft coral, sponge, urchin, zooanthid); and Non-living (crevice/hole; reef matrix, rubble, sand)

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
Additionally, macroalgae were identified to the lowest level possible.

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
For species that could not be identified in the field, representative voucher samples were collected.

--- SENTENCE 67 ---
Representative samples of CCA were collected in the vicinity of the transects, when the surveys were complete.

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
Coralline algae require extensive laboratory analysis in order to make definitive identifications.

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
Tentative identifications of specimens collected in this survey are included in the report and final identifications will be will be submitted as an addendum when they are complete.

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
Cheryl Squair collected the benthic data.

### *Reef Fishes*

Reef fishes were surveyed using the same underwater visual census techniques used in previous surveys: 30 x 2m belt transects laid perpendicular to the four permanent transect lines in FBNMS (Transects 2-5, Figure 1).

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
This method is described in detail by Birkeland et al. (1987, 1994).

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
In addition to recording fish species and abundance on each transect as in all previous surveys, fish sizes have also been recorded since 1998.

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
Due to bad weather and limited boat access, only one of the permanent transect lines (Transect 2, depths 3, 6, 9, 12 and 18m) was surveyed by Alison Green in November 2007 (this report).

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
The remaining permanent transect lines will be surveyed by Ben Carroll, and results of these surveys will be provided when they have been completed.

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
Alison Green also conducted the fish surveys in 1995, 1998 and 2001, while two other observers conducted the surveys in 1985 and 1988 (Richard Wass and Steve Amesbury respectively).

#### Survey techniques – Pago Pago Harbor

The two sites in Pago Pago Harbor (Rainmaker Hotel and Aua, Figure 2), which provide useful information for management (Birkeland et al 2004), were resurveyed in 2007 in conjunction with the Fagatele Bay surveys.

--- SENTENCE 78 ---
At these two sites, corals were surveyed at 3 m and 6 m depths on the reef slope.

--- SENTENCE 79 ---
Survey methods were as described above, with corals surveyed in a single 30 x 0.5 m belt transect at each depth and with each colony identified to species and categorized into one of seven size classes.

####

--- SENTENCE 80 ---
The Aua Transect

The methods are described in detail in Green et al. (1997).

--- SENTENCE 81 ---
The corals were counted in 0.25 m<sup>2</sup> quadrats that were tossed haphazardly within about 10 m to either side of the transect, with an equal number of quadrats on each side, within a set of zones between the shore and the reef crest.

--- SENTENCE 82 ---
The transect now began 91 m from shore because this was now the seaward extent of the burrow pit.

### Survey techniques – Historic fish transects around Tutuila

Fish communities were surveyed on the reef slope at each of three sites (Fagatele Bay 12 m, Sita Bay 5-6 m and Cape Larsen 8-9 m) on eight occasions from 1977 to 2007.

--- SENTENCE 83 ---
These sites were initially surveyed as part of an assessment of the impacts of the crown-of-thorns starfish, *Acanthaster planci* (see Birkeland et al. 1987, 2003).

--- SENTENCE 84 ---
We have continued to survey these sites as part of the Fagatele Bay surveys, because they form the oldest continuous survey data for coral reef fishes from American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 85 ---
One large transect (100 m x 2 m) was surveyed at each site from 1977 to 2001, because they were originally designed as part of a different project (see Wass 1982).

--- SENTENCE 86 ---
In 2004, survey methods were modified to include three replicate 30m x 2m transects at each site, rather than a continuous 100 m x 2 m transect.

--- SENTENCE 87 ---
This allowed us to survey a similar area, while introducing replication into the survey design.

--- SENTENCE 88 ---
The exact location of the start of each of the transects is described in Birkeland et al. (1987), and GPS co-ordinates were recorded at each site in 2004 to facilitate relocation (Appendix 1, Green et al 2004).

--- SENTENCE 89 ---
Alison Green conducted the fish surveys in 1995, 1998, 2001, 2004 and 2007, while two other observers conducted the surveys in 1977 and 1985 (Richard Wass) and 1988 (Steve Amesbury).

### **Data analysis**

Coral data from Fagatele Bay and the two sites in Pago Pago Harbor were summarized to determine the abundance and percent coral cover at each depth.

--- SENTENCE 91 ---
Percent cover was calculated according to Mundy (1996), based on using the maximum diameter as the diameter of a circle, and using the mid-point of each size class.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
Percent cover was calculated from benthic data by dividing the number of points of a given category, by the total number of points on the transect and multiplying by 100.

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
To allow for comparison between years, data were grouped into the above categories, even where more detailed information was available.

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
Fish data from Fagatele Bay and the three historic fish transects (Fagatele Bay, Cape Larsen and Sita Bay) were summarised to determine the relative abundance and species richness at each site and each depth.

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
Fish data from the historic transects were also converted to density estimates (no. individuals per hectare) to make the data among years more comparable (to adjust for the change in sampling area in the 2004 surveys), and to match international standards for fish survey data.

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
Long term trends in coral and fish communities in Fagatele Bay, Pago Pago Harbour and other sites around Tutuila were compared from 1977-1985 to 2007.

--- SENTENCE 97 ---
Fish size data in Fagatele Bay are only available for the three most recent surveys (2001, 2004 and 2007).

--- SENTENCE 98 ---
These data will be summarised at the family level to determine the size-frequency of four important fisheries families over the last seven years once all the data have been collected: Acanthuridae (surgeonfishes), Scaridae (parrotfishes), Lujanidae (snappers) and Serranidae (groupers, see Green 2002 for further details).

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
Size frequency of two important fisheries species will also be compared over the last seven years: *Cephalopholis argus* (Family Serranidae) and *Ctenochaetus striatus* (Family Acanthuridae).

### **RESULTS**

# **Fagatele Bay National Marine Sanctuary**

*Corals in Fatatele Bay: 2007/8*

A total of 2417 coral colonies belonging to 94 species were recorded during surveys in Fagatele Bay in 2007/8.

--- SENTENCE 100 ---
The most common coral in the bay (531 colonies) was *Galaxea fascicularis*.

--- SENTENCE 101 ---
Three other species were common in the bay and were represented by more than 200 colonies each, including *Montipora grisea* (368 colonies), *Porites lichen*

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
(213 colonies), and *Porites rus* (227 colonies).

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
Most species (67 of 94 species) were numerically rare, and represented by less than 20 colonies (Table 1).

--- SENTENCE 104 ---
In diverse ecosystems, most species are rare.

--- SENTENCE 105 ---
This pattern is similar to that in the 2004 survey.

--- SENTENCE 106 ---
Coral cover was relatively high in Fagatele Bay based on the belt transect data (64.1% cover, Table 1).

--- SENTENCE 107 ---
However, the numerically abundant corals did not always have the highest percent cover.

--- SENTENCE 108 ---
For example, although *Galaxea fascicularis* was the most abundant coral in Fagatele Bay, it covered only 2.1% of the area surveyed.

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
In contrast, we recorded only 10 colonies of *Acropora clathrata*, but it covered 12.7% of the area surveyed.

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
*Acropora hyacinthus*, *Montipora grisea*, and *Pocillopora eydouxi* each had 5-10% cover in the bay (Table 1).

At the family level, Fagatele Bay coral communities were dominated by Acroporiids (905 colonies).

--- SENTENCE 147 ---
Oculinids (536 colonies), Poritiids (569 colonies), Faviids (112 colonies), and Pocilloporiids (158 colonies) were also an important component of the coral communities.

--- SENTENCE 148 ---
This is the same pattern as in the 2004 survey.

--- SENTENCE 149 ---
The density of corals (number of colonies per square meter) varied across transects and depths (Table 3).

--- SENTENCE 150 ---
We recorded an average of 14.5 colonies/m<sup>2</sup> across all transects and depths compared to 15.3 colonies/m<sup>2</sup> recorded in 2004 (Green et al, 2005).

--- SENTENCE 151 ---
The highest density (40.8 colonies/m<sup>2</sup>) was at 6 m depth on transect 3.

--- SENTENCE 152 ---
The lowest density was (4.9 colonies/m<sup>2</sup>) was at 12 m depth on transect 5.

--- SENTENCE 153 ---
This is the same pattern as in the 2004 survey.

--- SENTENCE 154 ---
Coral cover recorded in Fagatele Bay in 2007/8 ranged from 27.1% at 12 m on Transect 3 to 108.0 at 3 m deep on Transect 4 (Table 4).

--- SENTENCE 155 ---
Note that due to the nature of the calculation of coral cover based on the largest diameter, areas are likely to be somewhat overestimated and total cover can exceed 100%.

In general, Fagatele Bay coral communities are dominated by small colonies (<20 cm diameter).

--- SENTENCE 161 ---
Almost one third (32.3%) of corals in Fagatele Bay were in size class 2 (5-10 cm), and less than 1% were over 80 cm diameter (see Table 5).

--- SENTENCE 162 ---
In general, recruitment (gauged as number of colonies <5 cm diameter) was highest in shallow water at all sites.

--- SENTENCE 163 ---
However, a confounding factor is that the most common species, *Galaxea fascicularis*, only grows to a maximum of about 20 cm in diameter here, and it is most common in shallow water.

--- SENTENCE 164 ---
*Alveopora* sp. and *Porites* sp. 2 also are small, rarely growing larger than about 10 cm.

--- SENTENCE 165 ---
*Porites rus* also frequently produces clusters of many small colonies.

### Trends in Fagatele Bay coral populations 2004-2007/8

Coral communities on the reef slope showed a decrease in coral cover (Table 4) from 2004 to 2007/8, consistent with the community having largely recovered from previous disturbances and having reached equilibrium.

--- SENTENCE 166 ---
A graph of the change in coral cover since 1985 shows a period of rapid increase in coral cover, and a smaller decrease in the present survey (Figure 3).

!

--- SENTENCE 167 ---
Coral cover change from 1985 to 2007/8 in Fagatele Bay

Coral density varied considerably among years and sites.

--- SENTENCE 168 ---
Of the 14 transects surveyed, seven showed increases in density and seven showed decreases.

--- SENTENCE 169 ---
Most of these changes were not dramatic, but Transect 3 at 6 m depth increased from 25.8 to 40.8 colonies/m<sup>2</sup> and Transect 5 at 6 m increased from 7.7 to 21.6 colonies/m<sup>2</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 170 ---
Most of the changes likely represent variation in the exact sampling site between the two years.

--- SENTENCE 171 ---
Coral colony size distributions for 1995, 2004 and 2007/8 are compared in Figure 4.

--- SENTENCE 172 ---
The proportion of corals in the smallest size category (<5 cm) decreased over this period, and the proportion of corals in categories 3-5 increased.

--- SENTENCE 173 ---
This supports the view that corals were growing in size during this period.

### *Benthic Cover in Fagatele Bay*

Approximately 9 species of algae in 7 genera were recorded from the 14 transects (Table 6).

--- SENTENCE 228 ---
The assemblage is very similar to that recorded from the 1995 survey, though algal diversity has decreased since then.

--- SENTENCE 229 ---
Macroalgae were generally encrusting (e.g. CCA, Peysonnelia spp.

--- SENTENCE 230 ---
or Lobophora spp.), with few turf or fleshy species other than Halimeda occurring on the transects and this was the same for both 2004 and 2007 survey years.

--- SENTENCE 231 ---
Approximately 5 species of CCA (Appendix 2) were identified and these species are similar to those identified by Wilkins in 1995 (Birkeland et al, 2003).

--- SENTENCE 232 ---
Surveys showed that corals and macroalgae form the bulk of the fish habitat at all depths and transects, which was the case in the 2004 study (Tables 6, 7).

--- SENTENCE 233 ---
Coral cover ranged from 14.6% to 55.3%, while macroalgae cover ranged from 17.8 - 83.3 %.

--- SENTENCE 234 ---
Macroinvertebrate cover ranged from 0.0 – 27.1% and non-living surfaces ranged from 0.0 – 4.4%.

--- SENTENCE 235 ---
A number of general trends were noted between the survey years.

--- SENTENCE 236 ---
Coral cover varied, with some transects showing an increase from 2004, while others showed a decrease.

--- SENTENCE 237 ---
Changes in percent cover of coral are roughly inversely correlated with changes in percent cover of macroalgae (Fig. 5).

--- SENTENCE 238 ---
Macroalgal cover generally increased, as did invertebrate cover (Table 7).

--- SENTENCE 239 ---
Overall, there was a marked decrease in the percent cover of non-living surfaces (Table 7).

--- SENTENCE 240 ---
In a 1985 survey conducts by Birkeland et al (1987), a weak trend was seen with macroalgae and particularly CCA, decreasing in percent cover with depth (Figs. 7, 8).

--- SENTENCE 241 ---
This

trend is not evident in the subsequent surveys in 1995, 2004 and 2007.

--- SENTENCE 242 ---
However, Figures 7 and 8 show that the abundance of algae has generally decreased over time, since 1985, when Fagatele was still recovering from the major crown-of thorns outbreak in 1978 (Birkeland 1982).

### Corals in Pago Pago Harbor –2007

--- SENTENCE 319 ---
A total of 401 coral colonies belonging to 30 species were recorded in the surveys at the two sites in Pago Pago Harbor in 2007.

--- SENTENCE 320 ---
The most common coral was *Montipora grisea* (80 colonies) closely followed by *Pavona varians* (78 colonies).

--- SENTENCE 321 ---
Only five other species were abundant (represented by >20 colonies); *Pocillopora damicornis* (53 colonies), *Psammocora profundacella* (40 colonies), *Psammocora contigua* (32 colonies), *Pavona divaricata* (29 colonies), and *Pocillopora verrucosa* (26 colonies).

--- SENTENCE 322 ---
All other species (n = 24) were rare (<11 colonies) (Table 1).

--- SENTENCE 323 ---
Average cover in Pago Pago Harbor was only 19.4%, and only two species contributed substantially to the coral cover in the harbor.

--- SENTENCE 324 ---
*Diploastrea heliopora* represented 14.1% cover and *Pavona varians* represented 4.8% cover in the area surveyed.

--- SENTENCE 325 ---
All other species combined represented just 0.5% cover.

--- SENTENCE 326 ---
Average coral cover was highest at 6 m at Rainmaker (21.7%), and lowest at 3m at Aua (15.5%).

--- SENTENCE 327 ---
Coral density in Pago Pago harbor was also low.

### *Benthic Cover in Pago Pago Harbor*

Coral cover ranged from 11.4% at Rainmaker to 32.2% at Aua, while macroalgae cover ranged from 45.8% at Rainmaker to 63.6% at Aua (Table 11).

--- SENTENCE 357 ---
The Rainmaker site is located at the back of Pago Pago Harbor, where circulation generally appears more restricted compared with the Aua site.

--- SENTENCE 358 ---
Turbidity is high at the Rainmaker site with corresponding low light levels.

--- SENTENCE 359 ---
Sediment is easily disturbed and remains suspended in water column for a long time (> 2 hours).

--- SENTENCE 360 ---
These conditions generally favor more shade-loving species, such as some species of red algae, and discourage organisms with higher light requirements, such as coral and many species of algae.

--- SENTENCE 361 ---
The low coral and algal cover and high non-living cover at the Rainmaker site may, in part, result from the high turbidity at the site.

--- SENTENCE 362 ---
While algal cover increases from Rainmaker to Aua, it is important to note that the increase is largely in percent cover of CCA, which increase from 22% at Rainmaker to 54% at Aua and turf algae decrease from approximately 20% at Rainmaker to 6% at Aua.

--- SENTENCE 363 ---
A decrease in percent cover of turf algae often indicates a change to a healthier oligotrophic environment and a concomitant increase in grazer biomass.

### *The Aua Transect*

Long- term monitoring shows that coral density has changed dramatically over time along the Aua transect (Fig. 11).

--- SENTENCE 379 ---
In 1917, corals were recorded along the entire reef flat, with the highest density recorded on the outer reef flat and crest as were evident in Mayor's (1924) photographs.

--- SENTENCE 380 ---
The excavation of a burrow pit in the 1950s removed nearly all corals from the inner 91 m since that time.

--- SENTENCE 381 ---
Density was lowest in 1995 and 1998, but there has been a significant recovery of coral communities on the reef crest and outer reef flat in during the past 15 years.

--- SENTENCE 382 ---
The corals were few in 1995-1998, but starting with a massive recruitment of *Acropora nana* in 1999, density has remained high on the outer part of the transect with solid substrata, but low on the rubble sections of the middle part of the transect and essentially non existent in the burrow pit.

!

--- SENTENCE 383 ---
Density of corals from 1917 to 2007 from the shore to the reef crest.

--- SENTENCE 384 ---
Along with increased coral population density, the living coral cover has also returned (Fig. 12), but the coral species diversity is now slightly lower (Fig. 13).

--- SENTENCE 385 ---
There used to be a variety of *Acropora* species on the reef crest, but Acropora nana now dominates nearly exclusively.

--- SENTENCE 386 ---
In 1917, there was high coral cover on the outer reef flat, mostly *Porites cylindrica* and *Pocillopora* spp.

--- SENTENCE 387 ---
Now the reef flat is mostly rubble with only a few colonies of *Acropora formosa* starting to be established.

--- SENTENCE 388 ---
The *Acropora formosa* colonies on the rubble are quite healthy, as are the other species living on widely separated large immobile blocks among the rubble.

--- SENTENCE 389 ---
The coral colonies are healthy as long as they are not toppled over because of being attached to unstable substrata.

--- SENTENCE 390 ---
The *Acropora formosa* are able to gain stability by lodging of their relatively extensive branches.

--- SENTENCE 391 ---
Pavona and Millepora sometimes form coraliths, or unattached free-rolling fragments of substrata with living coral on all sides.

### **DISCUSSION**

### **Fagatele Bay National Marine Sanctuary**

### *Coral Communities in Fagatele Bay*

The main conclusion from our survey of the corals in Fagatele Bay is that they are still resilient and doing quite well.

--- SENTENCE 453 ---
"Resilience" is the ability to recover from damage.

--- SENTENCE 454 ---
The corals of American Samoa have been subjected to a major crown-of-thorns outbreak (1978), hurricanes (1981, 1987, 1990, 1991, 2004, 2005), bleaching from extraordinarily warm water (1994, 2002, 2003), and extreme low tides (1998, 2005), but results from our survey indicate that the coral communities are back to normal.

--- SENTENCE 455 ---
The abundance and population density of corals (14.5 colonies per m2 ) and the living coral cover (64%) are typical of very healthy reefs.

--- SENTENCE 456 ---
ReefBase considers reefs with 30% living coral cover as relatively healthy.

--- SENTENCE 457 ---
In diverse ecosystems, most species are rare, and yet 94 species were common enough to be encountered within our transect areas.

--- SENTENCE 458 ---
Obviously, in Fagatele Bay the common species are not dominating the reef community to the exclusion of less common species.

--- SENTENCE 459 ---
The 94 species on our transects indicate a truly diverse coral community.

--- SENTENCE 460 ---
The distribution of relative abundances matched the 2004 survey of Fagatele Bay, which indicates the system to be stable.

--- SENTENCE 461 ---
A total of 164 species of corals are known from Fagatele Bay from previous surveys, but this latest survey found yet another four species not previously recorded: *Acropora formosa, Montipora hispida*, *Fungia concinna* and *Fungia klunzingeri*, bringing the total number of species in Fagatele Bay to 168.

--- SENTENCE 462 ---
The number of species in Fagatele Bay is not yet decreasing.

--- SENTENCE 463 ---
The size distribution of coral colonies in Fagatele Bay also indicates a healthy situation with a few large colonies and an abundance of smaller colonies, indicating a healthy recruitment.

--- SENTENCE 464 ---
However, some of the smaller colonies are of species that do not usually grow large, e.g., *Porites* sp. 2 and *Galaxea fascicularis*.

--- SENTENCE 465 ---
The numerical pattern of relative abundance of species, the relative abundance of coral species representing the different families of corals, and the population densities of corals along the depth distribution are all fairly constant between 2004 and 2007, indicating that the population has reached a temporary equilibrium as a relatively healthy coral community.

--- SENTENCE 466 ---
The proportion of corals in the smallest size category (< 5 cm diameter) has decreased and the proportion in the higher size classes have increased since 1995, showing that the coral community has grown in size distribution over the past decade.

--- SENTENCE 467 ---
This basic resilience of the coral reefs in Fagatele Bay influenced by multiple disturbance factors is in stark contrast to the coral reefs of the western Atlantic, where the rate of reef degradation has been continuous since the late 1970s (Gardner et al. 2003, Lang 2003).

--- SENTENCE 468 ---
It is possible that the resilience of American Samoan reefs in general is in part because of the distance from continents.

--- SENTENCE 469 ---
All the reefs in the western Atlantic are closely bordered by North and South American continents.

--- SENTENCE 470 ---
From the north, the Mississippi River has dumped an average of 1.6 million metric tons of fixed nitrogen and 100,000 metric tons of phosphate per year into the western Atlantic and from the south the Orinoco has dumped comparable amounts.

--- SENTENCE 471 ---
Likewise, the western Atlantic gets hundreds of tons of microbe-infected dust from North African soil (Garrison et al. 2003).

--- SENTENCE 472 ---
Although atmospheric CO2 concentration has increased globally, much more has been taken up in the North Atlantic and very little is being taken up in the Pacific near American Samoa because of differences in regional physical oceanography (Sabine et al. 2004).

### *Corals in Pago Pago Harbor*

The pipe discharging the wastewater outflow from the two main tuna canneries was constructed to extend out to the entrance of Pago Pago harbor in 1992.

--- SENTENCE 473 ---
Since that time, the coral communities have definitely been improving at the Aua Transect and at the Rainmaker (now Sadies By the Sea) Hotel.

--- SENTENCE 474 ---
There has been successful recruitment and growth of some colonies of *Acropora hyacinthus* at both sites.

--- SENTENCE 475 ---
The coral cover typical of a fully normal and healthy reef community has returned to wherever there are solid substrata, i.e., on the reef crest and on large coral blocks on the reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 476 ---
The dominant species is *Acropora nana* because of a major massive recruitment.

--- SENTENCE 477 ---
*Pocillopora eydouxi, P. verrucosa, P. damicornis, Pavona divaricata, Porites cylindrica* and some large mound *Porites* are fairly common on the solid substrata.

--- SENTENCE 478 ---
On the inner reef flat that is still blanketed by rubble, the coral cover remains small even though the few corals that are there appear in fine health.

--- SENTENCE 479 ---
On the rubble, there are currently a few *Acropora formosa* colonies braced in the sand, a few coraliths of *Montipora* spp.

--- SENTENCE 480 ---
and *Millepora* spp., occasional *Stylaraea punctata* attached to rubble, and a number of species growing attached to large stable rocks.

--- SENTENCE 481 ---
Alfred Mayor of the Carnegie Institute in Washington DC established a transect for a quantitative study of the corals across a reef flat from the shore to the reef crest in 1917.

--- SENTENCE 482 ---
The transect was well within the protected waters of Pago Pago harbor, near the village of Aua, and was covered by branching corals, with *Porites cylindrica* dominant from about 15 m from shore to 160 m and with *Acropora* spp.

--- SENTENCE 483 ---
dominant on the outer 100 m (Mayor 1924; Green et al. 1997).

--- SENTENCE 484 ---
Substantial urban development began in Pago Pago after World War II.

--- SENTENCE 485 ---
In the 1950s, two tuna canneries were constructed and began operation, a fuel dump at Aua contributed pollutants to the seawater, and the inner 90 m of Mayor's Aua transect was dredged for road construction materials, contributing a plume of sedimentation over the transect for several years.

--- SENTENCE 486 ---
The canneries poured large quantities of wastewater into the harbor continuously and this lower water quality was a chronic problem for about four decades.

--- SENTENCE 487 ---
Art Dahl and Austin Lamberts (1977) resurveyed the Aua transect in 1973 and recorded that the reef flat between the solid pavement of the reef crest and the excavated pit near shore was converted from a community of branching corals to a blanket of loose rubble.

--- SENTENCE 488 ---
In 1992, the canneries extended the wastewater outflow pipes to the outer harbor, beyond the Aua transect, where water flow is stronger.

--- SENTENCE 489 ---
After 1992, the corals showed successful recruitment and growth.

--- SENTENCE 490 ---
The living coral cover and species richness all increased substantially wherever there was solid substratum, to about the levels recorded by Mayor in 1917.

--- SENTENCE 491 ---
The population density of corals on solid substrata is even higher than in 1917, although the living coral cover is comparable, so the average colony size is probably smaller now.

--- SENTENCE 492 ---
On the inner reef flat that is still blanketed by rubble, the coral cover remains small even though the few corals that are there appear in fine health.

--- SENTENCE 493 ---
On the rubble, there are currently a few *Acropora formosa*

--- SENTENCE 494 ---
(= *A. muricata*) colonies braced in the sand, a few coraliths of *Montipora* spp.

--- SENTENCE 495 ---
and *Millepora* spp., occasional *Stylaraea punctata* attached to rubble, and a number of species growing attached to large stable rocks.

--- SENTENCE 496 ---
The coral reef communities in American Samoa are still generally resilient and will recover readily from acute disturbances such as hurricanes, bleaching events, and predation by *Acanthaster planci*.

--- SENTENCE 497 ---
The record of 90 years on Mayor's Aua transect has shown that chronic disturbances such as a continuous input of pollution might keep the corals from recovering for decades, but the corals can promptly return if the chronic disturbance is ended, so long as there is solid substratum available.

--- SENTENCE 498 ---
However, where the substratum is loose rubble, recovery will take decades until the substratum can be solidified or until the corals can eventually form a self-reinforcing aggregation.

--- SENTENCE 499 ---
### *Fish communities*

.

- Could only do limited assessment because only 1/6th of survey has been completed.

--- SENTENCE 500 ---
A more detailed analysis will be provided when all of the surveys have been completed.
- Changes in fish communities over time is partly due to changes in coral communities as a result of habitat destruction by cots, hurricanes etc.

--- SENTENCE 501 ---
This mostly affects small specialized species that are closely associated with *Acropora* and *Pocillopora* corals (eg. *Plectroglyphidon dicki* – see Green 2002).

--- SENTENCE 502 ---
- Changes in density (particularly peaks in abundance in 1985 and 2001) were primarily due to surgeonfishes (see Fig 10, Green et al 2005).

--- SENTENCE 503 ---
These pulses are associated with mass recruitment events of *C. striatus* (Birkeland et al 1987; Green et al 2005).
- Coral reef fish communities in FB and elsewhere around Tutuila are still showing signs of overfishing.

--- SENTENCE 504 ---
Several large species that are characteristic of unfished reefs in the Indo-Pacific remain conspicuous by their absence or small size.

--- SENTENCE 505 ---
They include species such as maori wrasse (*Chelinus undulatus*), sharks, and larger species of serranids and scarids, all of which are known to be particularly vulnerable to fishing (Green 2002).

--- SENTENCE 506 ---
- The particularly low abundance and species richness in 1988 is believed to be due partly to a difference in methodology that year, since surveys were completed while other divers were in the vicinity (this has been avoided in other years: see Birkeland et al 2003).

--- SENTENCE 507 ---
The apparent decline in fish abundance in Fagatele Bay and other sites around Tutuila since 2001 may be partly due to seasonal variation in abundance, since surveys were conducted in September 2004 and November 2007 rather than during the recruitment season in March, as was the case in previous surveys.

--- SENTENCE 508 ---
The surveys were moved to late summer to avoid this recruitment season, which makes fish counting more difficult because of the large number of ephemeral juveniles at that time (particularly *C. striatus*).

--- SENTENCE 509 ---
The addition of size estimates to the methods in the last few surveys will allow for the interpretation of this confounding effect using the size structure of the fish populations in future.
- Surveys should be moved back to March better weather then.

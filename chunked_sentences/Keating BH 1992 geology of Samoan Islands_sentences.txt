## THE GEOLOGY OF THE SAMOAN ISLANDS

Barbara H. Keating Hawaii Institute of Geophysics, University of Hawaii, Honolulu, HI 96822

## **ABSTRACT**

The Samoan chain consists of high volcanic islands, atolls, and submerged reef banks near the southwest margin of the Pacific plate.

--- SENTENCE 9 ---
The chain is unusual, particularly when compared with the Hawaiian chain, because the islands are volcanically active on both the eastern and western ends of the chain, the islands are larger westward, the easternmost edifice is an atoll not an active seamount, and the chain consists dominantly of alkali rather than tholeiitic lavas.

--- SENTENCE 10 ---
While geological studies of the Samoan group are limited, the existing results are consistent with a hot spot origin similar to Hawaii, complicated by continued reactivation of volcanism on Savaii.

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
The continuing volcanism on Savaii is believed to be the result of deformation of the margin due to lithospheric dilation, as the plate bends where it approaches the Tonga Trench subduction zone.

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
The dominance of alkalic volcanism in this island chain has recently been associated with a geochemical heterogeneity in the underlying mantle.

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
## INTRODUCTION

The Samoan islands are a chain of high volcanic islands in the southwest Pacific Ocean.

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
The chain consists of three high volcanic islands (Tutuila, American Samoa; Upolu and Savaii, Western Samoa) and numerous islets (Fig. 1).

--- SENTENCE 15 ---
Reef banks and seamounts continue westward from the chain.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
constituting the Northern Melanesian Borderland (Brocher, 1985).

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
The first geologic studies of the islands were conducted from sailing vessels exploring the south Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
Several early geologic studies reported volcanic eruptions (1902-1911).

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
Early geologic mapping took place in the 1940's and 1950's but little more was done until the 1980's.

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
The islands of the chain, other than Rose Atoll, are young (less than a few million years in age).

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
Previous workers have compared these islands with the Hawaiian islands because the geologic relations, ages, and orientation of the islands and submerged seamounts are similar (Fig. 2).

--- SENTENCE 22 ---
The purpose of this chapter is to summarize the current state of our understanding of the origin and evolution of the Samoan island chain, and bring together the geologic information from diverse publications into a single summary.

## **BACKGROUND**

## Volcanic Heritage

The word Samoa comes from the words sa ia moa in the native Samoan language.

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
The legendary name is derived from the volcanic origin of the land itself.

--- SENTENCE 46 ---
According to Samoan legend, "the rocks cried to the earth, and the earth became pregnant.

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
Salevao, the god of rocks, observed motion in the moa, or center of the earth.

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
The child was born and named sa ia Moa, from the place where it was seen moving.

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
Salevao said he would become loose stones, and that everything which grew would be sa ia Moa or sacred to Moa.

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
Hence the rocks and earth were called sa ia Moa or as it is abbreviated, SAMOA," according to Turner (1979).

#### Discovery and Early Exploration

Jacob Roggeveen is considered to be the modern discoverer of the Samoa islands in 1722.

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
The islands were inhabited by natives at the time of the first encounter with Europeans.

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
Roggeveen called them the "Baumann Islands" after a captain in his squadron of ships.

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
In 1768, L. Bougainville visited the islands, naming them the "Isles des Navigateurs" after observing the frequent use of canoes by the natives.

--- SENTENCE 54 ---
Captain James Cook heard of these islands while in Tonga in 1773 and recorded their names but did not visit.

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
In 1789, a visit by La Perouse to Tutuila proved eventful.

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
His second-in-command, Captain de Langle, died along with many crew members in a scene very similar to that in which Captain James Cook lost his life in the Hawaiian Islands.

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
After the death of his crew members, La Perouse gave the island the name "Massacre Island"—now Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
The bay on the north shore of Tutuila is still known as Massacre Bay (near Aasu).

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
The historic event is of interest since the geologic features there played an important part in the events which transpired at the bay.

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
On December 12, Captain de Langle and 61 men in two longboats and two pinnaces went to a village in a cove near their anchorage to collect water.

--- SENTENCE 61 ---
Instead of a spacious and convenient cove, the men found a coral-filled cove with a

twisting, narrow entry channel.

--- SENTENCE 62 ---
The captain had observed the bay at high tide and was not aware of the tidal range within these islands.

--- SENTENCE 63 ---
The water collecting went well, but within an hour almost fifteen hundred natives had assembled, leading to great confusion.

--- SENTENCE 64 ---
The confusion was compounded when de Langle began giving gifts to natives he thought were chiefs.

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
Conflict resulted and de Langle ordered his men back to the boats.

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
The men were stoned, de Langle responded by firing his musket in the air, and this provoked the natives to a general attack.

--- SENTENCE 67 ---
The captain was knocked down, fell overboard, and was clubbed to death.

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
Many sailors abandoned some of the longboats and scrambled to the pinnaces.

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
Others steered the boats through the narrow passage for open water.

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
Unfortunately, the boats ran aground at the narrowest part of the channel.

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
The reef terrace formed by a higher sea level stand allowed their pursuers to close in again.

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
The volleys from the muskets scared the attackers away.

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
The boats cleared the reef and returned to the frigates.

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
La Perouse considered returning, but decided against it when he saw the coral reef platform which contributed to the loss of lives.

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
The Samoan islands were visited by Edwards in 1791, as part of the search by H.M.S. *Pandora* for participants in the mutiny on H.M.S. *Bounty*.

--- SENTENCE 76 ---
A book by the captain and physician on this voyage (Edwards and Hamilton, 1915) describes many visits to South Pacific islands.

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
Von Kotzebue visited the islands in 1824.

## REGIONAL SETTING

The Samoan islands consist of a series of high volcanic islands, atolls and submerged reef banks, and seamounts which form a linear chain in the southwest Pacific Ocean.

--- SENTENCE 78 ---
The chain trends in a southeast-northwest direction (Fig. 2) beginning near the international date line and extending westward, roughly 100 km north of the termination of the Tonga Trench, into a region known as the Northern Melanesian Borderland (Fig. 3, from Brocher, 1985).

--- SENTENCE 79 ---
The Northern Melanesian Borderland is a complex region within the southwest Pacific where island arcs like the Tongan and Lau Islands, mid-plate features like Peggy Ridge, and seamounts of hot spot origin like the Samoan group, occur near the convergent margin of the Pacific plate and the Fiji Plateau.

--- SENTENCE 84 ---

--- SENTENCE 88 ---

Farther to the west are the submerged seamounts and reef banks described by Brocher (1985).

--- SENTENCE 90 ---
Age dating of rocks dredged from these banks suggests these banks are a continuation of the Samoan seamount group.

## **GEOLOGIC EXPLORATION**

#### **Previous Studies**

The first geological explorations of Samoa were reported by Dana (1849) as part of the U.S. Exploring Expedition under Lt. Charles Wilkes.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
Dana spent a very limited time on Samoa; his visit to the islands was restricted to the time required by the expedition to generate a hydrographic map of the island.

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
Despite his short visit, the observations of Dana show an obvious insight into the geology of these islands.

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
As a result of Dana's visit to these islands and others, he published numerous manuscripts, including "Geology of the Pacific Area" (1846) in the Wilkes expedition volumes, "Corals and Coral Islands" (1872) and "Characteristics of Volcanoes" (1890).

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
Dana noted the youthful appearance of the western district of Samoa (Savaii), which contrasts with the greater age of the central portion of the chain (Tutuila).

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
He felt that these islands formed from two volcanic fissures, one of age equivalent to Tahiti (in the Society Islands) and Kauai (in the Hawaiian islands), and a second contemporaneous with the present reefs.

## GENERAL DESCRIPTION OF THE ISLANDS

Western Samoa

Kear and Wood (1959) reported on the geology and hydrology of Western Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 121 ---
They recognize a general structure on Upolu of deeply eroded and dissected volcanic terrains (assigned a Pliocene or early Pleistocene age), largely buried by late Pleistocene and Recent lavas.

--- SENTENCE 122 ---
They assign the name Fagaloa volcanics to the oldest of these rocks, largely on the basis of their weathered appearance, consisting of a'a and pahoehoe flows along with associated dykes, tuffs and cone deposits.

--- SENTENCE 123 ---
They point out that these rocks characteristically form steep-sided high mountains with erosional slopes of 25–50°.

--- SENTENCE 124 ---
Of considerable interest is their observation that the original dips on the exposed surfaces suggest that these volcanics were extruded from vents that are oriented approximately parallel to those of the younger lavas.

--- SENTENCE 125 ---
Rocks from each of the younger mapped units uncomformably overlie and fill valleys eroded into Fagaloa volcanics.

--- SENTENCE 126 ---
Most of these units are olivine-rich basalts which strongly resemble one another petrologically.

--- SENTENCE 127 ---
The features used to differentiate between the rock units include (Kear and Wood, 1959):

- Salani Volcanics deep soil and weathering; evidence of pre-Mulifanua canyon cutting.
- Mulifanua Volcanics the existence of wide barrier reefs existing offshore of these outcrops; only shallow stream channels.

--- SENTENCE 128 ---
- Lefaga Volcanics lack of dissection; only narrow fringing reefs present offshore of these outcrops.

--- SENTENCE 129 ---
- PuaPua Volcanics thin soils; lavas flow offshore and form rocky (or "ironbound") coasts; ubiquitous aa and pahoehoe structures form broad domes.
- Aopo Volcanics cones erupted in last 200 years, fresh porphyritic pahoehoe flows and a'a flows common only around cones which fill older valleys and spill out over coasts to fill lagoons and cover the barrier reef.

--- SENTENCE 130 ---
Kear and Wood (1959) note that the volcanic cones are plentiful on Upolu and Savaii and that the degree of weathering, dissection, and decay of the cones varies, reflecting their Salani to Aopo ages.

--- SENTENCE 131 ---
The largest cones occur between 600 and 900 m above sea level on Upolu, and up to 1800 m on Savaii.

--- SENTENCE 132 ---
The cones vary widely in form, some having minor amounts of ejecta and giving rise to major lava floods, while others form simple single cones of cinders and

scoria.

--- SENTENCE 133 ---
The younger cones are generally black with veneers of glassy cinder.

--- SENTENCE 134 ---
As the rocks weather, they tend to turn reddish in color, and the finer-grained materials are altered to clays, forming a reddish clay soil littered with blocks of scoria.

--- SENTENCE 135 ---
Savaii

The island of Savaii is by far the largest island in the Samoan chain.

--- SENTENCE 136 ---
Using the definitions and mapping unit of Kear and Wood (1959), the oldest rocks observed on the island are situated on either side of the Vaipouli River on the north shore of Savaii (Fig. 4).

--- SENTENCE 137 ---
This unit displays considerable relief and deep weathering.

--- SENTENCE 138 ---
Paleomagnetic studies of Savaii (Keating, 1985a) indicate that these rocks are characterized by normal polarity magnetization.

--- SENTENCE 139 ---
Based upon the magnetic reversal time scale, we believe these rocks are likely to correspond to the Gauss Normal Chron and therefore are 2.5 million years old or older.

--- SENTENCE 140 ---
Radiometric dating of these rocks has been undertaken by Ian McDougall.

--- SENTENCE 141 ---
The results of these studies when published will much more accurately define the ages of these rocks.

Asau Bay

Asau Bay is located on the northwestern coast of Savaii.

--- SENTENCE 185 ---
The eastern margin of the bay (Utuloa Point) is bounded by PuaPua volcanics that are flat-lying pahoehoe lavas which filled the pre-existing lagoon and buried the reef.

--- SENTENCE 186 ---
The remainder of the bay is formed by Mulifanua lavas.

--- SENTENCE 187 ---
These lavas are highly vesicular nearly flat-lying basaltic lava flows.

--- SENTENCE 188 ---
Near the water the lava flows are very fresh in appearance, lacking substantial weathering.

--- SENTENCE 189 ---
A well-developed coral reef exists offshore from Matavai (or Utuloa Point, depending on the map used) to Fagasua.

There another PuaPua flow buries much of the lagoon (Fig.

#### Offshore Manganese Deposits

The land area of the Samoan islands is roughly 3,000 square kilometers while the offshore area within the Exclusive Economic Zone or EEZ (the area within 200 nautical miles of the islands) is about 150,000 square kilometers (Exon, 1982).

--- SENTENCE 379 ---
In order to evaluate potential manganese (Mn) nodule deposits, sampling programs have been conducted in the basins surrounding the Samoan islands.

--- SENTENCE 380 ---
Manganese nodules were found to be rare, and to be of low ecomonic grades.

--- SENTENCE 381 ---
Exon (1982) ascribes the rarity to the high sedimentation rates caused by volcanic input of debris, which is detrimental to Mn nodule formation.

--- SENTENCE 382 ---
Exon also concludes that the low grades of nickel and copper observed reflect the low production of plankton generally associated with deposits.

--- SENTENCE 383 ---
Because the seafloor is well above the calcite compensation depth, calcareous plankton are not dissolved

and do not release metals to the seafloor where Mn concentration takes place.

--- SENTENCE 384 ---
Manheim and Lane-Bostwick (1989) point out the importance of the chemical character of the water mass.

--- SENTENCE 385 ---
They have found that cobalt distribution within Mn crusts reflect regional highs and lows in the distribution of hydrothermal fluid discharge in the ocean.

--- SENTENCE 386 ---
Commonly, at moderate depths (1-2 km) Mn crusts form on the upper slopes of sea mountains.

--- SENTENCE 387 ---
In general, on relatively young seamounts (a few m. y.)

--- SENTENCE 388 ---
these crust formations are very thin and do not constitute economic resources.

--- SENTENCE 389 ---
Studies in the Hawaiian chain (McMurtry et al., 1986) show thin Mn crusts of little economic value compared to the thick Mn crusts present on Mesozoic seamounts within the Central Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 390 ---
Two seamounts of possible Mesozoic age are present within the EEZ of Samoa, Machias Seamount and Rose Atoll.

--- SENTENCE 391 ---
Machias Seamount is situated southwest of the Samoa Islands on the flexure arch of the Pacific plate near the Tonga trench.

--- SENTENCE 392 ---
Early work on the seamount included dredges of the shallow summit (Hawkins and Natland, 1975).

--- SENTENCE 393 ---
Cobbles, gravel, pahoehoe fragments, and coral were found in the dredges, indicating the crest of the seamount was formerly at sea level.

--- SENTENCE 394 ---

--- SENTENCE 395 ---

--- SENTENCE 396 ---

--- SENTENCE 397 ---

--- SENTENCE 398 ---

## GEOLOGIC MAPPING UNITS OF WESTERN SAMOA

Kear and Wood (1959) produced the original geologic maps of Western Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 471 ---
Their division of geologic mapping units was based upon geomorphology.

--- SENTENCE 472 ---
They identified six geologic units and assigned them ages based upon relative weathering of Holocene to Pliocene.

## Fagaloa Volcanics

The stratigraphically lowest, thus oldest, unit mapped is the Fagaloa volcanics.

--- SENTENCE 473 ---
This unit is deeply weathered.

--- SENTENCE 474 ---
The mapped units include "two somewhat different landforms."

--- SENTENCE 475 ---
Kear and Wood (1959; p. 36) report that, "one landform, possibly the older, has no part of the original cone form remaining and forms steep high mountains with slopes up to 50°, rising as inliers above the gentle slopes of the later lavas.

--- SENTENCE 476 ---
It includes dykes, which form bold vertical cliffs and steep narrow ridges, often several hundred feet high.

--- SENTENCE 477 ---
The other landform, though steep locally, includes ridges that descend steeply seawards and could be the surviving parts of the original cone surfaces.

--- SENTENCE 478 ---
Some of the gently sloping ridges have narrow flat tops on which the soil is strongly leached, acidic, and now supports little more than wiry grass.

--- SENTENCE 479 ---
The steep offshore slope has prevented the formation of coral reefs over much of the Fagaloa coastline, and where a reef is present, it is fringing.

--- SENTENCE 480 ---
"

#### Salani Volcanics

The Fagaloa volcanics are overlain by Salani volcanics.

--- SENTENCE 481 ---
Valleys and gorges cut deeply into this volcanic unit.

--- SENTENCE 482 ---
This morphological characteristic was used to map the unit aerially.

--- SENTENCE 483 ---
Often the Salani valleys are filled by younger lava flows.

--- SENTENCE 484 ---
Salani volcanics consist of low angle lava flows that most frequently can be traced to the cone or crater from which they originated.

--- SENTENCE 485 ---
The cones themselves are usually weathered to the point that they are breached or contain sufficient decomposed material to seal the bottom of the crater and form crater lakes or swamps.

--- SENTENCE 486 ---
The surfaces of flows are often deeply weathered, and soil is often more than 10 cm thick.

--- SENTENCE 487 ---
On the distal flows, over 30 cm of soil has formed.

--- SENTENCE 488 ---
Relict boulders are often observed to show onion skin weathering.

--- SENTENCE 489 ---
The gorges formed by the rivers on Savaii and Upolu cut deeply into Salani rocks.

--- SENTENCE 490 ---
Often these gorges are filled by younger lava flows.

--- SENTENCE 491 ---
Valleys are common in rocks of the Salani formation and are a distinctive mapping feature.

--- SENTENCE 492 ---
In many places, these valleys contain permanent rivers.

--- SENTENCE 493 ---
Elsewhere, the valleys can be dry much of the year.

--- SENTENCE 494 ---
Typically, a barrier reef exists offshore of Salani outcrops.

--- SENTENCE 495 ---
However, along the eastern part of the south coast of Upolu, cliffs are found at the coast and the reef is close to shore.

--- SENTENCE 496 ---
Near Vavau on Upolu, blowholes and sinkholes are present, probably resulting from the collapse of blowholes.

--- SENTENCE 497 ---
The Salani rocks are fine grained gray to black porphyritic basalt which grade upward to vesicular basalt with a rubbly a'a surface.

--- SENTENCE 498 ---
The thickness of flows generally average 1-2 m. The more weathered outcrops and presence of large green olivine phenocrysts were used in the field mapping to distinguish these rocks from the lithologically similar but younger Mulifanua rocks (Kear and Wood, 1959).

--- SENTENCE 499 ---
Rocks of the Salani volcanics include picrite basalts, olivine dolerite, and basalts showing late-stage deuteric alteration and zeolitization.

--- SENTENCE 500 ---
Olivine basalts constitute more than 50% of the Salani volcanics sampled (Brothers, in Kear and Wood, 1959).

--- SENTENCE 501 ---
The Salani volcanics are differentiated from the Fagaloa volcanics by their less-weathered appearance, less mature landform (partial cones and craters), the absence of dykes, less common occurrence of large olivine nodules and phenocrysts, absence of andesites and trachytes, and the presence of a well developed reef offshore.

--- SENTENCE 502 ---
These lavas fill valleys formed in Fagaloa rocks, and top Fagaloa ridges and terrain.

--- SENTENCE 503 ---
A well-developed soil horizon separates the Fagaloa and Salani volcanics at the Alaoa power station on Upolu.

--- SENTENCE 504 ---
This deep soil horizon and the deep gorges cut within the Fagaloa are thought to reflect a substantial erosional interval.

--- SENTENCE 505 ---
Barrier reefs are generally present offshore of Salani volcanics.

--- SENTENCE 506 ---
#### Mulifanua Volcanics

Areas mapped as the Mulifanua unit are characterized by dry shallow valleys and gullies, where flowing water is rare.

--- SENTENCE 507 ---
The weathering in this unit is "moderate."

--- SENTENCE 508 ---
The flows descend from "well-formed" cones, and are "almost uneroded."

--- SENTENCE 509 ---
Boulders on the surface of the flows are angular and onion-skin weathering has not developed.

--- SENTENCE 510 ---
In distal portions of the flows, weathering can reach 30 cm.

--- SENTENCE 511 ---
These flows sometimes occur in valleys cut into pre-existing rocks.

#### Lefaga Volcanics

The Lefaga volcanics are the next stratigraphically higher unit.

--- SENTENCE 512 ---
According to Kear and Wood (1959), "The surface expression is similar to that of Mulifanua rocks, except that a'a at the ground surface is more common, the lavas appear to have flowed out into the lagoonal area, and the reef is relatively close inshore.

--- SENTENCE 513 ---
Onion-skin weathering occurs locally, due possibly to the high feldspar content.

--- SENTENCE 514 ---
"

#### PuaPua Volcanics

The PuaPua volcanics consist of young basalt flows and perfectly preserved cinder cones.

--- SENTENCE 515 ---
The rocks have very little weathering and the soil is thin.

--- SENTENCE 516 ---
The ropy pahoehoe flows cover pre-existing flows, fill gorges, and flow over pre-existing erosion scarps.

--- SENTENCE 517 ---
The flows have low dips and form relatively even surfaces.

--- SENTENCE 518 ---
Extensive swamps have developed

in low depressions.

--- SENTENCE 519 ---
Locally, where slopes are steep, the flows consist of a'a flows.

--- SENTENCE 520 ---
Reefs are not developed off shore of PuaPua basalts.

--- SENTENCE 521 ---
Instead, the basalts fill lagoonal areas and cover pre-existing reef.

--- SENTENCE 522 ---
## Aopu Volcanics

The final unit defined and mapped by Kear and Woods is the Aopo volcanics.

--- SENTENCE 523 ---
These are "ropy, vesicular, porphyritic (feldspar and olivine) basalts, with but little a'a.

--- SENTENCE 524 ---
Where the a'a flows occur, it is blocky, loose and appears to have been transported on the surface of the flows and to have accumulated along the margins."

--- SENTENCE 525 ---
These are the historical lavas.

### Hydrology-Western Samoa

Kear and Wood (1959) attempted to establish a framework for understanding the ground-water gradients in Western Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 527 ---
They attempted to map similar volcanic units, on the basis of the degree of erosion, in order to use the known depth of the water table (established by drilling) in a limited number of sites, and extrapolate away to areas where the water table was unknown.

--- SENTENCE 528 ---
They suggested very low ground water gradients "should be suspected in the younger volcanics that cover the majority of the Territory."

--- SENTENCE 529 ---
They suggested that wells be drilled to sea level to obtain water, because "apart from areas of the oldest rocks (Fagaloa or older Salani), where the streams are virtually permanent, the water table will be close to the surface only near the coast, or close to "buried hills" of older rocks".

--- SENTENCE 530 ---
The Fagaloa volcanics are the oldest rocks exposed in Western Samoa and are severely weathered.

--- SENTENCE 531 ---
Kear and Wood (1959) state that the ground water "would generally be shallower, and available in smaller quantities than from younger less weathered formations..." Where Fagaloa volcanics have been buried by younger lava flows, the water trapped above these older volcanics would provide a shallow water source.

--- SENTENCE 532 ---
The Salani volcanics are typified by large rivers which carry water to the sea.

--- SENTENCE 533 ---
Significant losses of water occur along stream courses due to seepage.

--- SENTENCE 534 ---
Inland springs occur where these rocks are covered by thin lavas of younger rocks.

--- SENTENCE 535 ---
The Mulifanua rocks absorb water readily and rarely is surface water present.

--- SENTENCE 536 ---
Kear and Wood (1959) note that the scoriaceous nature of parts of the flows provides abundant water.

--- SENTENCE 537 ---
Wells however must be drilled to sea level to obtain water.

--- SENTENCE 538 ---
Water obtained from the Lefaga, PuaPua and Aopu volcanics is characterized by high iron content.

--- SENTENCE 539 ---
In general, the areas covered by these rocks have the poorest water supplies in Samoa except where the units are only a thin

cover which overlies older rocks.

--- SENTENCE 540 ---
On the island of Apolima, a surface stream emits water from the Vini Tuffs.

--- SENTENCE 541 ---
The stream never dries.

--- SENTENCE 542 ---
On other islands, where the Vini Tuff is present, a non-brackish water supply is uncertain (Kear and Wood, 1959).

## GEOLOGIC MAPPING OF AMERICAN SAMOA

In Western Samoa hydrologists used physiographic features to map the geology.

--- SENTENCE 770 ---
The strategy used was to map units using physiographic development in order to establish units of roughly equal age.

--- SENTENCE 771 ---
In American Samoa Tutuila was mapped by a volcanologist, Harold Stearns, who coincidentally was a specialist in groundwater resources.

--- SENTENCE 772 ---
Stearns carefully observed the geology of the American Samoan islands, identified the intrusive centers, calderas, and extracaldera lava flows.

--- SENTENCE 773 ---
The relationships he observed allowed him to map volcanic centers.

--- SENTENCE 774 ---
Often onlapping relationships allowed Stearns to establish relative ages for volcanism on an island.

--- SENTENCE 775 ---
It was only after radiometric dating was attempted decades later that some absolute age relationships have been established.

--- SENTENCE 776 ---
Stearns (1944) concluded that Tutuila is built from five volcanic centers (Fig. 20).

--- SENTENCE 777 ---
The volcanic centers are aligned on two or possibly three rifts, trending northeast-southwest.

--- SENTENCE 778 ---
The majority of the lavas are basic lavas, capped by limited trachyte, alluvium, coral reefs, and beach sands.

--- SENTENCE 779 ---
Stearns suggests that the bulk of the volcanoes appear to be Pliocene, based on the weathered and eroded conditions.

--- SENTENCE 780 ---
Fresh tuff cones and lava flows on the southwest side of the island, and on Aunuu Island are assigned Recent ages.

## Masefau Dike Complex

On the north coast of Tutuila near Masefau village is a complex of hundreds of basaltic dikes ranging from a few centimeters to 2 meters in width.

--- SENTENCE 783 ---
The dike rocks are vesicular and platy; some are amygdaloidal.

--- SENTENCE 784 ---
The dike complex strikes N 70° E and dips slightly southward.

--- SENTENCE 785 ---
These rocks are interpreted to be the oldest rocks exposed on the surface and were part of the deep structure of a volcanic rift zone.

--- SENTENCE 786 ---
The north side of the dike complex is exposed at Afono Bay, in a cliff on the north side of a promontory opposite Bartlett Island (now called Nuusetoga Island).

--- SENTENCE 787 ---
Stearns (1944) suggests that the cliff may be an eroded fault scarp or a sea cliff.

--- SENTENCE 788 ---
Resting against the face of the cliff is talus breccia overlain by 3-23 m of vitric basaltic pumice and cinders on which as basalt cascaded over the cliff.

--- SENTENCE 789 ---
Stearns suggests the north side of the dike complex may be the eroded caldera wall of a volcano older than the Pago volcano, whose vent lies north along the present coast; alternately, it may be an eroded early rift zone horst block of the Pago volcano, later buried by flows.

--- SENTENCE 790 ---
Based upon analogies with dike complexes elsewhere, Stearns suggests that the complex was apparently formed not more than 610 m nor less than 92 m below the surface of a rift zone and that 300 m or more of lavas which formerly overlaid the dike complex have been removed by erosion.

--- SENTENCE 791 ---
The Masefau dike complex cuts thin basaltic flows dipping at moderate angles (from 10 to 20° NW).

--- SENTENCE 792 ---
These lavas are in large part shattered and brecciated by faults.

--- SENTENCE 793 ---
Stearns suggests that much of the rock in the rift zone shatters slowly, during repeated intrusion of the dikes, and that the shattered brecciated material settled downward into the underlying magma chamber as breccia.

The Pago volcanic series includes extra-caldera lavas, dikes, plugs, cinder cones, vitric and lithic tuffs, and breccias as well as intra-caldera lavas, dikes, plugs, cones, tuffs and breccias.

The Pago volcano caldera was situated at the center of Tutuila Island.

--- SENTENCE 812 ---
Stearns (1944) estimates that the caldera extended 9.6 km in length and 4.8 km in width.

## Intra-Caldera Volcanic Units

Within the Pago caldera, lava flows formed thick ponded lava sequences to the north, east, and west.

--- SENTENCE 813 ---
Pahoehoe flows are scarce.

--- SENTENCE 814 ---
The lavas are generally aphanitic and olivine poor.

--- SENTENCE 815 ---
Dikes are observed, ranging from 1 to 15 m wide.

--- SENTENCE 816 ---
The tuff and breccia member is the explosive debris that accumulated during the excavation of the inter-caldera

The deposits contain lithic, crystal lithic, and lithicric debris.

--- SENTENCE 817 ---
The beds range in texture from fine-grained, minated, silty tuffs through lapilli tuffs to coarse blocky eds of breccia 6 m thick with blocks of ejecta 1.5 m wide.

--- SENTENCE 818 ---
Thick deposits of fire-fountain debris are interbedded with thic beds of cataclysmic explosions.

--- SENTENCE 819 ---
Pisolitic beds are present.

--- SENTENCE 820 ---
A few beds of carbonaceous plant remains are occasionally found, which could be used to determine radiocarbon ages, the ages are less than roughly 40,000 years.

--- SENTENCE 821 ---
Beds containing pumice fragments are also preserved.

--- SENTENCE 822 ---
Other beds contain both basaltic pumice and dense fragments of trachyte, indicating that the trachyte eruptions were followed by basaltic eruptions.

--- SENTENCE 823 ---
The beds are nearly horizontal near the source.

--- SENTENCE 824 ---
Where they have steep dips (reaching 32°) they interfinger and merge with talus breccia, suggesting they accumulated concurrently with the talus at the foot of the caldera wall.

--- SENTENCE 825 ---
An unconformity exists between the Pago tuffs and the pre-caldera lavas.

--- SENTENCE 826 ---
The unconformity is well exposed in a small stream channel at the head of Pago Pago valley, where the caldera rim is seen.

--- SENTENCE 827 ---
Thin bedded olivine basalts are uncomformably overlain by beds of talus breccia and tuffs.

--- SENTENCE 828 ---
Two dikes cut the lavas but do not cut the tuffs.

--- SENTENCE 829 ---
Faulting is present but the lack of deformation of the tuffs suggests that the faulting motion pre-dates the accumulation of tuffs.

--- SENTENCE 830 ---
The stream's channel, which is a series of cascades, cuts down through the exhumed caldera wall.

--- SENTENCE 831 ---
Another unconformity can be seen on the north side of Taumata Peak.

--- SENTENCE 832 ---
There, the massive columnar jointed ponded lava forms a peak resting on talus breccias, thin bedded lithic tuffs, and basaltic pumice and cinder beds of the Pago Tuff Member.

--- SENTENCE 833 ---
The intra-caldera lavas on the northeast side of the peak rest on red baked vitric tuff.

--- SENTENCE 834 ---
The tuffs of the Pago volcanic series east of Taumata Peak are underlain and overlain by massive ponded lavas.

--- SENTENCE 835 ---
A dike complex crops out in a valley on the northeast side of Tau Peak.

--- SENTENCE 836 ---
The dikes are terminated by massive lavas, suggesting an unconformity or caldera wall between the caldera-filling lavas and the dike complex.

A small spur of dense rock is present on the road just west of Aua.

--- SENTENCE 838 ---
Stearns suggests it is a remnant of caldera lavas.

### The Pago Caldera

Stearns suggests the Pago caldera was 4.8 km wide and 9.6 km long.

--- SENTENCE 840 ---
The geological evidence indicates the caldera formed by collapse following the building of a cone.

--- SENTENCE 841 ---
A water development tunnel near Pago Pago Bay cuts a major fault.

--- SENTENCE 842 ---
Along the fault a "gouge and splinter" zone is observed 30 cm wide.

--- SENTENCE 843 ---
There, 6.3 m of friction breccia is seen which contain faceted blocks up to 60 cm across and smaller fragments which have been ground to balls by friction.

--- SENTENCE 844 ---
The lavas inside and out of the caldera differ.

--- SENTENCE 845 ---
Stearns suggests at least 480 m of collapse took place.

--- SENTENCE 846 ---
Projections of the original height of the mountain, using the dip of extra-caldera lavas, indicate the mountain would rise 1200 m above present sea level, if the cone had the same slope at the summit.

--- SENTENCE 847 ---
The southern rim of the volcano was much lower than the northern rim.

--- SENTENCE 848 ---
Stearns suggests the caldera may have been horse-shoe shaped with no rim on the south.

--- SENTENCE 849 ---
Stearns suggests, "by analogy with other similar basaltic volcanoes the collapse probably proceeded with the growth of the upper part of the cone, the caldera widening and deepening progressively even though lavas were erupted simultaneously on the floor.

--- SENTENCE 850 ---
Subsidence finally stopped as the volcano approached old age and the lavas began to differentiate.

--- SENTENCE 851 ---
Filling gained on subsidence, and at the cessation of activity the caldera was filled to the brim in the northwestern sector, overtopped and buried by perhaps as much as 150 m of lava in the southern and southeastern sectors, but lacked about 180 m of being filled in the northeastern sector ...."

#### Taputapu Volcano

The Taputapu volcanics form the western end of Tutuila Island.

--- SENTENCE 904 ---
The volcano is roughly 10 km long and 5 km wide, and 450 m above sea level.

--- SENTENCE 905 ---
The flows are dominantly olivine basalts from 2–15 m thick.

--- SENTENCE 906 ---
Locally, they are capped by thicker flows of porphyritic and nonporphyritic olivine poor basalts.

--- SENTENCE 907 ---
The flows dip at angles of 5 to 10 degrees.

--- SENTENCE 908 ---
The lavas where extruded from a rift zone extending N 75° E. Beds of red vitric tuff and cinder are common between flows a few centimeters to a couple meters thick, indicating cinder cones were very common along the rift zone.

--- SENTENCE 909 ---
Exposures of the dipping beds of the Taputapu volcanics in the streams flowing into Massacre Bay led Stearns to separate the Taputapu volcanics from the Pago volcanics.

--- SENTENCE 910 ---
An unconformity was not found between the two volcanoes.

--- SENTENCE 911 ---
Based upon the relatively small erosion and dissection of the Taputapu lavas compared to the Pago volcanics, Stearns concluded the Taputapu volcanics overlap the Pago lavas, and are younger.

#### Leone Volcano

The Leone volcanics are the least weathered volcanics on the island and appear very young.

--- SENTENCE 914 ---
They form a large peninsula on the south side of the island (referred to in some publications as the Tafuna-Leone plain) and are associated with several cinder and ash cones.

--- SENTENCE 915 ---
The top of the pahoehoe lavas on the Leone peninsula at Steps Point are only about 2 m above sea level.

--- SENTENCE 916 ---
Two thick tuff units were identified by Stearns but could not be mapped due to the thick vegetation.

--- SENTENCE 917 ---
Stearns (1944) suggests a crack, 6.4 km long, opened from Olotele Peak across the present fringing reef and the submerged 72 m shelf in Recent times.

--- SENTENCE 918 ---
Eruptions began at the upper end of the crack with fire fountains.

--- SENTENCE 919 ---
Where the crack reached the sea, gas-charged lava rose through the water and produced violent explosions.

--- SENTENCE 920 ---
The ocean water repeatedly entered the vent producing many explosions, with sufficient intensity to hurl material 1.5 km in the air, according to Stearns.

--- SENTENCE 921 ---
The strong trade winds blew much of this material northwestward across the island and partly filled the bay and valley at Leone (P. Eyre, personal communications, 1990).

--- SENTENCE 922 ---
Fragments of drowned coral reef were involved in the eruptions (Daly, 1924).

#### Relative Ages

The Samoan Islands are much like the Hawaiian islands.

--- SENTENCE 923 ---
Thomson (1921) remarked on similarities and differences between the Samoan and Hawaiian islands which are worth reiterating.

--- SENTENCE 924 ---
First, like Hawaii, with the exception of coral reefs along the coast, the rocks of the Samoan island chain are volcanic.

--- SENTENCE 925 ---
Second, there does seem to be a progressive shifting of volcanic centers in Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 926 ---
In Hawaii, the shifting of volcanic activity is from northwest to southeast.

--- SENTENCE 927 ---
Thomson (1921) points out that Savaii has experienced three eruptions in historic times and is comparable in form and state of erosion to the island of Hawaii.

--- SENTENCE 928 ---
Upolu appears more dissected and is comparable to Maui.

--- SENTENCE 929 ---
Tutuila, he points out, is still more dissected and is comparable to Oahu.

--- SENTENCE 930 ---
Recent radiometric dating by Natland and Turner (1985) has yielded a date near the base of the volcanic pile on Tutuila island of 1.5 Ma.

--- SENTENCE 931 ---
Thus, recent results confirm Thomson's geologic interpretation.

--- SENTENCE 932 ---
Dana (1849) pointed out that the character of the rocks and general topography of the central district of Upolu were similar to that of Tahiti and Kauai and thus suggest a similar age.

--- SENTENCE 933 ---

## Paleomagnetism

--- SENTENCE 970 ---

## Dredged Rocks

Hawkins and Natland (1975) examined the major element chemistry and trace element geochemistry of rocks dredged from the flanks of Savaii, Upolu, and Tutuila as well as nearby seamounts (including Machias) within the Samoan seamount chain.

--- SENTENCE 1036 ---
The post-erosional lavas sampled are strongly undersaturated with silica.

--- SENTENCE 1037 ---
In order to explain the Si and Al-poor, and Ca, Ti, Mg, Fe-rich basanites and olivine nephelinites, the authors suggest that magma generation took place close to the probable base of the lithosphere.

--- SENTENCE 1038 ---
Magma generation was due to moderately extensive melting (10-15%) of an essentially anhydrous and alkali-poor mantle at depths of about 85 km.

--- SENTENCE 1039 ---
Hawkins and Natland suggest a hot spot mechanism is uncertain and a more likely explanation is a combination of viscous shear melting and lithosphere dilation due to the deformation of the Pacific plate at the nearby Tonga Trench subduction

Natland (1980) suggests that at a magmatic lineage can be identified on Machias Seamount producing trachytes and phonolites.

--- SENTENCE 1040 ---

--- SENTENCE 1041 ---
All Samoan lavas were found to have an undepleted radiogenic mantle source.

--- SENTENCE 1042 ---

--- SENTENCE 1043 ---

--- SENTENCE 1271 ---
In the early 1800's the descriptions of atolls as great rings of coral around calm lagoons, within the deep Pacific captured the interest of many.

--- SENTENCE 1272 ---
Charles Lyell (who was mentor to Charles Darwin) suggested the atolls were the crests of submarine volcanoes overgrown by coral (Darwin, 1842).

--- SENTENCE 1273 ---
Darwin conceived the idea that volcanoes grow from the sea floor, form high volcanic islands, then die, subsiding

into the sea.

--- SENTENCE 1274 ---
Corals grow on the shorelines rimming the volcanic islands; as the volcanoes subside, the corals grow upward, leaving a gap between the barrier reef and the central volcanic island.

--- SENTENCE 1275 ---
As subsidence continues and the volcano subsides below sea level, the coral atoll remains.

--- SENTENCE 1276 ---

--- SENTENCE 1277 ---

### Tectonic setting

Studies of the bathymetric trends of the linear seamount and island chains in the Pacific (e.g., Clague and Jarrard, 1973; Jarrard and Clague, 1977; Epp, 1978) have yielded a great deal of information regarding the tectonic history of the Pacific plate.

--- SENTENCE 1371 ---
Linear seamount chains in the Pacific are numerous and varied.

--- SENTENCE 1372 ---
The Hawaiian, Emperor, and Line Islands seamount chains are the best studied.

--- SENTENCE 1373 ---
West of these three seamount and island chains, however, the bathymetry becomes extremely complex, and the trends are so numerous that even the linear bathymetric trends become less apparent.

--- SENTENCE 1374 ---
Analyses of the bathymetric trends show that most of the Pacific seamount and island chains lie on parallel small circles about Pacific plate hot spot poles.

--- SENTENCE 1375 ---
The Hawaiian Islands and Emperor Seamounts have been associated with a melting anomaly (e.g., Morgan, 1972; Shaw and Jackson, 1973) referred to as a hot spot.

--- SENTENCE 1376 ---
Very limited radiometric, petrographic or paleomagnetic results are available from the western and southern Pacific seamount chains, particularly those that are old and submerged.

--- SENTENCE 1377 ---
Due to the lack of dating of the western Pacific seamount chains it is difficult to determine if these chains display simple age progression patterns like the Hawaiian-Emperor chain, or if they were necessarily produced by passage of the Pacific plate over relatively fixed melting anomalies, or hotspots, in the mantle.

--- SENTENCE 1378 ---
The Line Islands chain, for example, has proven to be a composite of two or more major volcanic episodes (Jarrard and Clague, 1977; Jackson, 1976;

--- SENTENCE 1379 ---
Saito and Ozima, 1977; Jackson and Schlanger, 1976; Schlanger et al., 1984; Keating, this volume).

--- SENTENCE 1380 ---
Clague and Jarrard (1973) and subsequent workers have proposed that the Samoan Islands are a hot spot trace.

--- SENTENCE 1381 ---
Hawkins and Natland (1975) and Natland (1980) suggest that plate deformation associated with subduction in the nearby Tonga Trench has influenced, perhaps even caused, Samoan volcanism.

--- SENTENCE 1382 ---
It has been suggested that the deformation has determined the location and orientation of Samoan shield volcanoes, and contributes to the unusual volume of post-erosional volcanism at the western end of the chain.

--- SENTENCE 1383 ---
This subduction-related deformation has determined the orientation of the posterosional volcanic rift zone, and has deformed the sea floor around the Samoan islands.

--- SENTENCE 1384 ---
The results of new studies provide basic observations that constrain the origin, age, and evolution of the Samoan Islands and Melanesian borderland seamounts (Brocher, 1985; Duncan, 1985; Natland and Turner, 1985; Sinton et al., 1985; Keating, 1985).

--- SENTENCE 1385 ---
These authors suggest that the results of the individual studies are consistent with a hot spot origin for the Samoan islands.

--- SENTENCE 1386 ---
In examining the data collectively, sparsity of data becomes an important concern.

--- SENTENCE 1387 ---
At the present time, the paleomagnetic, radiometric, and geochemical data remain very limited.

--- SENTENCE 1388 ---
The paleomagnetic data, for example, can be correlated with a hot spot model for the origin of the Samoan islands.

--- SENTENCE 1389 ---
But at the same time, the available data can also be used to support a model for progressive volcanism toward Savaii, which is quite inconsistent with a hot spot model.

--- SENTENCE 1390 ---
Likewise, the existing radiometric dates on the islands are limited; they allow a positive correlation with a hot spot model in only two islands and two seamounts west of the Samoan chain.

--- SENTENCE 1391 ---
Thus, although the data are "broadly consistent with a hot spot mode of origin for the volcanoes," as stated by McDougall (personal communications, 1984), it is important that variations of the hot spot mode of origin be examined in order to explain the anomalous patterns of volcanism observed in the Samoan Islands.

Duncan (1985) shows that recent (0.8 Ma) volcanic activity has occurred on Wallis Island (roughly 180 nm west of Savaii; Fig. 36).

--- SENTENCE 1393 ---

--- SENTENCE 1394 ---
Could these three islands be related?

--- SENTENCE 1395 ---
The three form a linear trend that cuts across the main trend of the Samoan group.

--- SENTENCE 1396 ---
Assigning a deformational history to this recent activity, as suggested by Natland, resolves many of the anomalous features of the volcanic propagation in the Samoan chain.

--- SENTENCE 1397 ---

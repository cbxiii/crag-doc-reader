### The Site

Fagatele Bay is a 163-acre embayment on the southwest coast of Tutuila (Figure 2), American Samoa's largest and most populated island.

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
It has long been recognized as a resource of high ecological value by various federal, territorial, and private groups.

--- SENTENCE 15 ---
Its pristine character has been virtually unchanged for many years because of the steep cliffs (rising vertically more than 200 feet) that rim the bay.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
Because of the difficult overland access, the bay has remained virtually isolated, and free from most human impacts.

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
The cliffs, however, did not prevent the partial devastation of the bay's coral reefs from natural causes.

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
During late 1978 and early 1979, the starfish destroyed more than half of the coral reefs fringing Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
Despite the devastating impact it had upon the live coral resource, this incident presented a unique opportunity to study and document the recovery of such ecosystems.

# Traditional Culture and Fagatele Bay

The traditional Samoan lifestyle, known as fa'a Samoa, places great importance on the dignity and achievements of the group rather than on an individual's achievements.

--- SENTENCE 24 ---
The traditional communal lifestyle revolves around the aiga (ah-eeng'-a), or extended family.

--- SENTENCE 25 ---
The aiga is headed by a matai (mah-tie'), or village chief, who takes responsibility for the welfare of all his or her aiga.

--- SENTENCE 26 ---
The matai manages the communal economy, protects and distributes family lands, and represents the family in councils.

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
American Samoa's system of land tenure is based on communal lands held by aigas.

--- SENTENCE 28 ---
The basic claim of each aiga is recognized and respected by all aigas.

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
Land transfers among aigas rarely occur.

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
Land alienation laws, aimed at preserving this Samoan system, have existed since the first U.S. Navy administration in 1900 (see box on page 22).

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
The traditional Samoan village was supported by a self-sustained local economy requiring a minimum of trade and specialization.

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
Land and marine tenure allowed for farming and harvesting specific areas.

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
Access to both land and water areas adjacent to villages was controlled by the matai.

--- SENTENCE 34 ---
While no written history of Fagatele Bay's traditional use patterns exists, oral accounts and archaeological work revealed two village sites along the margin of the bay.

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
The villages' uses of the bay may have included salt harvesting and subsistence fishing, with tenure boundaries comprising the entire bay.

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
Today, primary usage of the bay includes sport and subsistence fishing by villagers who live above the bay.

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
They control land access to the bay via their communal land tenure system.

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
Gleaninggathering primarily shellfish, such as the giant clam Tridacna, and algae (Dictyota, Laurencia, and Ulva) from the nearshore reef areas—and pole and line fishing are the major subsistence activities, while rod and reel fishing are the dominant sportfishing activities.

--- SENTENCE 39 ---
The most common species caught include butterfly fish (Chaeotodon spp.), surgeonfish (Acanthurus spp.), goatfish (Parapeneus), and snappers (Lutjanus).

--- SENTENCE 40 ---
Most of these activities, occurring along the nearshore areas of the bay, presently are conducted by less than 20 people from the nearby villages.

# **Cooperative Planning Effort**

Recognizing the impact on the bay's resources resulting from the starfish infestation, the Pacific-wide need for coral reef management-related research, and the need to protect the site from additional human-induced stresses, a proposal was submitted in March 1982 by the American Samoa Government (ASG) to the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) nominating Fagatele Bay as a candidate for marine sanctuary designation.

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
In addition to describing the scientific justifications for sanctuary designation, the proposal also explained the role of culture and tradition and its importance to daily life in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
It was apparent that recognizing the existence of village regulations (non-codified) based on their traditional lifestyle, and protecting these traditions along with the bay's resources were vital to the sanctuary designation process.

# Sanctuary Designation

April 1986, Fagatele Bay was formally designated der the pre-1984 regulations) the nation's newest

and southernmost National Marine Sanctuary.

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
The designation of Fagatele Bay as a sanctuary established the basis for cooperative management of the area by the American Samoan Government and NOAA.

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
The management plan includes regulations that delineate the Sanctuary's boundaries, and prohibits such activities as spearfishing, taking or damaging natural resources (including the Crown-of-Thorns starfish); the use of trawls, seines, trammel nets or any fixed nets; disturbance to the benthic community; the discharge of any materials or substances into sanctuary waters; removing or damaging cultural resources; and the taking of any sea turtles.

--- SENTENCE 54 ---
Traditional activities, such as gleaning and subsistence fishing, are specifically allowed.

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
The plan achieves a number of goals.

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
The resource protection regulations reflect the area's historical use, while at the same time addressing coral reef management needs and ensuring adequate protection of Fagatele Bay's resources.

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
NOAA and the territorial government also recognized the need to provide technical training to local Samoans to provide further assurance that local participation in the sanctuary's day-to-day management will not be ignored.

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
Thus, another major emphasis of the sanctuary at Fagatele Bay is to provide a mechanism to assist in the training of local personnel in resource management techniques.

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
With active involvement of the sanctuary manager, Samoans will participate in workshops and training

# American Samoa

American Samoa is the only U.S. Territory 🔪 south of the equator.

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
It consists of seven islands that possess a total land area of 76 square miles and a combined population of approximately 32,000 people.

--- SENTENCE 61 ---
Approximately 90 percent of the population resides on American Samoa's largest island, 54 squaremile Tutuila (Too-too-ee'-la), while the remaining 10 percent live on nearby Aunu'u (Ow-noo'-oo) and the smaller eastern islands of Ofu (oh'-foo), Olosega (Oh-low-seng'-uh), and Ta'u (Tah-oo').

--- SENTENCE 62 ---
Rose Atoll, an uninhabited area approximately 160 miles east of Tutuila, is a National Wildlife Refuge.

--- SENTENCE 63 ---
Swains Island, a privately-owned coral atoll, lies approximately 225 miles to the north.

--- SENTENCE 64 ---
The Dutch were the first Europeans to contact the Samoan people in the late 1700s.

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
From 1840 to 1899, Germany, Great Britain, and the United States had a tripartite agreement to oversee Samoa's commerce.

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
Their efforts to unify the islands were supplanted by their individual attempts to establish naval stations there.

--- SENTENCE 67 ---
For example, in 1870, the United States launched a scientific expedition to the islands of Samoa led by Lt. Charles Wilkes.

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
From the information provided by this expedition, the United States drafted a treaty with the Samoans in 1872 that would have granted its exclusive use of Pago Pago Harbor, had it been ratified.

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
The three powers nearly went to war over their exclusionary actions, but instead agreed to split the islands in 1899: the United States assumed jurisdiction over Samoa's eastern islands, Germany took control over the western islands, and Great Britain withdrew.

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
The German islands of Western Samoa were larger and more fertile than the mountainous eastern islands.

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
During World War I, New Zealand landed troops and took over the German colony.

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
The islands were then placed under New Zealand's direction, until Western Samoa gained its full independence in 1962.

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
The eastern islands, which included Pago Pago Harbor, were awarded to the United States.

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
In early 1900, American Samoa became a U.S. Territory; and on February 19, 1900, the U.S. Navy was assigned responsibility for administering the islands by President William McKinley.

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
During World War II, the U.S. Navy used Pago Pago Harbor as a refueling station for their Pacific and south Pacific operations.

--- SENTENCE 76 ---
They also created a landing strip near the village of Leone that the U.S. Marine Corps jointly used.

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
In 1951, President Harry Truman transferred administration of the islands to the U.S. Department of the Interior, where it remains today.

--- SENTENCE 78 ---
The Territorial government is an Americanstyled system with three branches.

--- SENTENCE 79 ---
The Executive Branch is headed by an elected governor.

--- SENTENCE 80 ---
A bicameral Legislature, the Fono, has law-making authority under the Territorial Constitution.

--- SENTENCE 81 ---
Members of the House of Representatives are elected by adult suffrage for two-year terms.

--- SENTENCE 82 ---
The House includes residents of all social strata.

--- SENTENCE 83 ---
Senators are registered chiefs who are selected by County Councils for four-year terms.

--- SENTENCE 84 ---
The Judicial Branch includes a High Court and five District Courts.

# Sanctuary Education Program

Sanctuary designation will also enhance American Samoa's public education.

--- SENTENCE 90 ---
For many years, American Samoa's history was passed on in the oral tradition by village chiefs.

--- SENTENCE 91 ---
Today, government officials who determine policies and standards use very different channels to communicate with people.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
The sanctuary education program is responsive to both the traditional and modern communication processes.

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
Relying on a combination of local support, local experts, and non-island educators and scientists, the education program will not only convey the findings of scientists to the general public, but also will include the history of traditional rights in Samoa and other Pacific islands, and outline their roles in 20th Century conservation efforts.

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
Implementation of the education program is scheduled for this year.

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
The program is divided into two stages, and will use the University of Hawaii's Curriculum and Development Group; and American Samoa's Department of Education, Community College, Department of Recreation, Development Planning Office, and Office of Marine Resources to facilitate its execution.

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
Stage I (1988 and 1989) will focus on identifying the sanctuary to the public and disseminating that information through the development of a marine science curricula in local schools, public outreach programs, and traveling exhibits.

--- SENTENCE 97 ---
Stage II (1990–1993) will expand on Stage I to include areas outside the sanctuary, emphasizing the cultural and historical aspects of the bay and similar areas throughout the Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 98 ---
The cultural and natural history program will use legends, stories, and traditional conservation practices from Samoa and other Pacific islands to show the essential links between Samoans and the sea's resources.

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
It also will establish bonds with other national marine sanctuaries, as well as other marine protected areas throughout the Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 100 ---
Centering around the desire to develop a strong environmental ethic, the sanctuary's educational program forms a solid foundation for individual and community actions that will promote environmental awareness on the whole island.

--- SENTENCE 101 ---
The involvement of village matai will maintain the traditional lines of oral communication.

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
In combination with on-site and off-site programs, involvement of the matai will also allow for greater acceptance of the program by the Samoan people.

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
Thus, the sanctuary will serve not only as an environmental education laboratory, but also as a demonstration of how traditional and modern conservation practices may complement each other.

--- SENTENCE 104 ---
In this manner, traditional rights and practices can re-emerge as tools for future resource conservation efforts.

--- SENTENCE 105 ---
The role of enforcement officials will include educating violators of Sanctuary regulations of the reasons for protecting Fagatele Bay's resources, and

how they can contribute to leaving a living legacy future generations.

--- SENTENCE 106 ---
Through this, public support for American Samoa's National Marine Sanctuary can enhanced.

--- SENTENCE 107 ---
With education and public support, the need for strict enforcement efforts ideally will be minimal.

# Pacific Marine Resource Management

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
Although the Crown-of-Thorns starfish infestation provided the impetus for sanctuary designation of Fagatele Bay, it also pinpointed the need to effectively manage coral reef resources throughout the Samoan Archipelago and the Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
If left undisturbed, these fragile ecosystems can recover.

--- SENTENCE 111 ---
However, on the land-poor, volcanic South Pacific islands, increasing population and land-use pressure on the limited flat land have led to increased pressure on all coastal resources.

--- SENTENCE 112 ---
The filling-in of reefs and mangroves to extend the available flat land is a common practice in several island areas.

--- SENTENCE 113 ---
Since many of these practices are either condoned or ignored by the local governments, sound management of coastal resources requires monly a complete understanding of the coral reef ecosystem, but also complete support by the local population for research and management of these areas.

--- SENTENCE 114 ---
The approach to these issues will necessarily vary from island to island, depending on the degree of "westernization" of the culture.

--- SENTENCE 115 ---
But, through a combination of research, education, and enforcement programs tailored to the needs of the various island cultures, many of the most important coral reef management issues, such as the Crown-of Thorns, can be addressed effectively.

--- SENTENCE 116 ---
In keeping with a central goal of other conservation programs in the Pacific, the creation of other marine protected areas throughout the Pacific will help to preserve environments and traditions characteristic of other islands and regions.

--- SENTENCE 117 ---
In this respect, it is believed that Fagatele Bay will eventually evolve to serve as a model for other Pacific island nations where coral reef management environmental awareness, and the maintenance of traditional culture are, or soon will become, important issues.

--- SENTENCE 118 ---
## Uniquely Samoan

While coral reef management and environmental awareness are relatively new to American Samoa, continued cooperation from both Federal and Territorial governments will enhance the success of the sanctuary program.

--- SENTENCE 119 ---
Through the Fagatele Bay National Marine Sanctuary, the legacy left for future generations of American Samoans will improve the quality of life, while maintaining a cultural heritage and identity that is uniquely Samoan.

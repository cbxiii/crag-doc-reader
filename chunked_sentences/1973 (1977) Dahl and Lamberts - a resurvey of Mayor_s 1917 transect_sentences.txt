# Environmental Impact on a Samoan Coral Reef: A Resurvey of Mayor's 1917 Transect<sup>1</sup>

ARTHUR L. DAHL<sup>2</sup> AND AUSTIN E. LAMBERTS<sup>3</sup>

ABSTRACT:

--- SENTENCE 2 ---
Coral reef sites in Pago Pago Harbor, American Samoa, for which descriptions and quantitative data were obtained by Alfred G. Mayor and the Carnegie Institution of Washington expeditions of 1917–1920, were resurveyed in 1973.

--- SENTENCE 3 ---
Some sites were destroyed and others damaged in the intervening half century, but it was possible to relocate the major quantitative transect at Aua.

--- SENTENCE 4 ---
A reduction in total numbers of corals, a change in the relative proportions of different genera, and a probable reduction in the average size of individual colonies are recorded.

--- SENTENCE 5 ---
Elsewhere in the harbor, more drastic effects on the reefs were noted.

--- SENTENCE 6 ---
Both human and natural impacts may be responsible for the observed changes; it is suggested that the Aua reef may now be recovering from earlier damaging events.

--- SENTENCE 7 ---
A MAJOR DIFFICULTY in determining the environmental impact of urbanization and human activity stems from lack of adequate baseline data from which to measure change.

--- SENTENCE 8 ---
The lack is particularly acute in the case of complex tropical ecosystems such as coral reefs.

--- SENTENCE 9 ---
Even where there are early studies, they are generally insufficiently documented to permit meaningful comparisons with present conditions.

--- SENTENCE 10 ---
The extensive studies by Mayor (1924a, b, c, d) are an exception.

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
These studies, conducted in American Samoa between 1917 and 1920, centered in an area altered by subsequent development and provide a baseline for studies of the long-term impact of human activities on a coral reef.

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
We report on a recent resurvey of some of Mayor's sites.

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
The Samoan Islands are an eroded volcanic archipelago in the mid-South Pacific (14° S, 170° W), of which the eastern islands constitute the Territory of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
Tutuila, largest island in the territory, consists

of a 32 km long volcanic crest reaching heights of 654 meters but never more than 9.6 km wide.

--- SENTENCE 15 ---
The island runs SW-NE, and is partly encircled by about 55 km of narrow fringing reefs, predominantly on the south side exposed to the full sweep of the southeast tradewinds.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
The large deep-water Pago Pago Harbor opening to the southeast nearly bisects the island, and contains a fifth of the reef front (Figure 1).

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
The Polynesian population apparently never exceeded 5000, subsisting in part on seafood gathered from the reefs and on fish caught along the margins of the bays.

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
Pago Pago Harbor became a United States coaling station in 1872, and the territory was ceded to the United States in 1900.

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
Between 1942 and 1945, the military dredged several inshore areas for landfill, there was an increase in harbor traffic, and shipping converted from coal to oil.

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
In 1950, with the transfer of administration to the Department of the Interior, development of Tutuila accelerated.

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
Tuna canneries were established on the north shore of the harbor in 1956, and dredging operations were expanded in 1960.

--- SENTENCE 22 ---
During the past half century, the population of Tutuila increased to about 30,000, with extensive urban development along the harbor shores.

## RESURVEY METHODS

The Carnegie Institution program of 1917- 1920 remains the only extensive study of the ecology of Samoan reefs.

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
This program included taxonomic studies of various groups, such as corals (Hoffmeister 1925), soft corals (Cary 1931), terrestrial and marine plants (Setchell 1924), geological and ecological studies including reef coring (Bramlette 1926, Cary 1931) and the wide-ranging experiments and observations of Mayor himself *(l924a, b,* c, d).

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
Central to this program were a series of line transects across the reefs in Pago Pago Harbor.

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
Mayor defined his Aua transect as running 39.5° W (magnetic) from a large tree on the beach to a conspicuous coral block on the outer edge of the reef (Mayor 1924a).

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
In the intervening 57 years, road construction has altered the shoreline, eliminating both beach and tree.

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
To relocate the Aua line, one of us (Dahl), matched Mayor's map (Mayor 1924a) with recent maps and the actual shore contour, obtained a new starting point, and aligned the line with respect to the mountain contours opposite to correspond Mayor's photograph (Mayor 1924a, plate 41B).

--- SENTENCE 46 ---
The other (Lamberts) independently positioned a line based on recent charts, Mayor's photographs, and a bearing recalculated from 1917 to 1974.

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
The two lines,

established without consultations, were within 1 meter of each other, and passed within 1 meter of a large, well-cemented block, 1 meter above the general contour of the reef edge.

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
Remeasuring the line inward from the reef edge indicated that the margin of the present road fill roughly corresponded to Mayor's starting point.

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
The remeasured line was marked with iron rods at 65.6-meter (200-ft) intervals, and Mayor's quantitative survey method was repeated with squares 7.32 meters (24 ft) on a side marked out at the same distances used in 1917.

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
A team of five members with face masks and underwater slates, each concentrating on a particular group of corals or other organisms, counted genera and species within each square.

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
A voucher collection of corals was later compared directly with Mayor's specimens at the National Museum of Natural History, Smithsonian Institution.

## RESULTS

The dredging of a nearshore lagoon 3 meters deep and over 40 meters wide altered Mayor's first four measured squares too greatly to include them in this study.

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
This lagoon bottom consisted of calcareous sand and mud with occasional outcroppings of coral rock to which heads of *Porites lutea* Vaughan up to 35 cm in diameter were attached, as well as scattered clumps of *Acroporaformosa* (Dana) larger than any on the reef flat.

The remaining contours and the surface of the Aua reef seem not to have changed appreciably in the intervening years.

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
Water currents pass obliquely from the harbor entrance across the Aua line.

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
Surface waters generally sweep to the head of the bay where the currents are dissipated.

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
The waters within the confines of the upper harbor are turbid and dark and a plume of discoloration can often be observed in the cannery area.

Coral growth on the reef opposite the village of Aua remains vigorous, but along the north harbor shore it becomes sparse.

### DISCUSSION

Measurements from charts indicate that approximately 10 km of reef front once existed in Pago Pago Harbor between Point Distress and Breakers Point.

--- SENTENCE 132 ---
In 1917 when Mayor visited Samoa, a wharf was already present, but from his lack of discussion it is inferred the reefs in the inner harbor were flourishing.

--- SENTENCE 133 ---
Mayor dredged living deep-water corals in the upper harbor and chose Lepua for coral growth studies (Mayor 1924d).

--- SENTENCE 134 ---
This site lies in the middle of a 2-mile stretch of reef which now shows considerable decrease in coral abundance compared with the Aua reef.

--- SENTENCE 135 ---
Also, 2 km of reef in the innermost part of the harbor showed no living coral in 1973.

--- SENTENCE 136 ---
The combination of sedimentation and eutrophication has caused destruction of many coral reefs in Kaneohe Bay, Oahu, Hawaii (Banner and Bailey 1970, Caperon

et al. 1971, Smith et al. 1973), and physical conditions in Pago Pago Harbor are in many ways comparable to those found in Kaneohe Bay.

--- SENTENCE 137 ---
Dredging activities in the upper harbor have been continuous for several years with attendant water turbidity and silt.

--- SENTENCE 138 ---
Johannes (1972, personal communication) and Pillai and Gopinadha (1971) implicate siltation as the most significant factor in reef destruction.

--- SENTENCE 139 ---
In Pago Pago Harbor some of the sediment formed by dredging is carried by currents to areas outside the harbor, but this circulation does not normally affect the Aua line.

--- SENTENCE 140 ---
Wastes from the canneries are, for the most part, washings and other organic fish residues which appear to contribute to progressive eutrophication of the bay.

--- SENTENCE 141 ---
The decrease in total number of coral heads and change in species proportion on the Aua transect since 1917 cannot readily be attributed to any specific natural fluctuation or human disturbance, although it may be related to the processes that have killed the corals of the inner harbor and generally impoverished the reefs ofthe north harborshore.

--- SENTENCE 142 ---
The changesin both numbers and proportions relative to distance on the transect suggest that major alterations in community structure have taken place.

--- SENTENCE 143 ---
While *Acropora* continues to be a major contributor to the structure of the reef, *Psammocora* has been reduced by two-thirds.

--- SENTENCE 144 ---
In the midzone, *Pocillopora*, especially *P. damicornis* and *P. brevicornis,* have increased fivefold, occupying a zone once dominated by *Porites.

--- SENTENCE 145 ---
*

Unfortunately, Mayor did not record the sizes of the colonies counted, although his photographs suggest considerable numbers of large colonies.

--- SENTENCE 146 ---
Comparison of coral sizes at Aua with those of other reefs on Tutuila shows that young corals now predominate at Aua.

--- SENTENCE 147 ---
*Porites lutea* frequently forms colonies 1 meter or more in diameter on Tutuila reefs, but at Aua the heads observed were as small as 3 cm.

--- SENTENCE 148 ---
In other reef moats on Tutuila, it is common to find patches of *Acropora formosa* many square meters in extent and 1 meter or more in height.

--- SENTENCE 149 ---
The lower parts ofthese thickets are often dead, and the upper parts killed in a sharply demarcated line, presumably corresponding to extreme low

tide level.

--- SENTENCE 150 ---
No such large patches were observed at Aua.

--- SENTENCE 151 ---
The many small colonies and lack of large ones suggests a reef subjected to intermittent severe stress, or one gradually recovering from an earlier traumatic event (for example, the freshwater inundation of 1969).

--- SENTENCE 152 ---
What is known of species tolerances in corals also supports the view that the Aua reef is gradually recovering from earlier stress.

--- SENTENCE 153 ---
Acropora requires pure, clean water and is intolerant to siltation.

--- SENTENCE 154 ---
The presence of some mature A. formosa in the dredged area and at the reef edge suggests impure water has not been a recent problem.

--- SENTENCE 155 ---
Pocillopora damicornis is most tolerant of adverse conditions and is often found near shore where there is silt and fluctuating water temperature.

--- SENTENCE 156 ---
This species and P. brevicornis are good colonizers of denuded areas because the planulae are released frequently and in large numbers.

--- SENTENCE 157 ---
A fivefold increase in numbers of *Pocillopora* is thus not unexpected.

--- SENTENCE 158 ---
One species greatly reduced in numbers at Aua was *Porites andrewsi*, and on the Lepua reef it was not found at all.

--- SENTENCE 159 ---
On other reefs on Tutuila it is often found in conjunction with *P. lutea* though there are inshore areas outside the harbor where one species was present in large numbers and the other absent.

--- SENTENCE 160 ---
Our recount of the number of black holothurians, *Stichopus chloronotus* Brandt, showed triple the number found in 1917, possibly suggesting an increase in organic material in the sediments.

--- SENTENCE 161 ---
The Aua transect in 1973 showed healthy recent coral growth with species diversity consistent with that found on other sections of the Tutuila reefs.

--- SENTENCE 162 ---
However, the total number of colonies was less than that in 1917, species proportions are considerably altered, and the number of small colonies is high.

--- SENTENCE 163 ---
Whether the reef will eventually return to the state recorded over 50 years ago by Mayor, will further decline under the impact of natural or man-induced stress, or will achieve some new equilibrium state can only be determined by future surveys.

--- SENTENCE 164 ---
Fortunately, the foresight and skill of the Carnegie Institution studies will make such a determination possible.

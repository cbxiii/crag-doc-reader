## **DEVELOPING AN INITIAL WATERSHED CLASSIFICATION FOR AMERICAN SAMOA**

!

--- SENTENCE 8 ---
Guy T. DiDonato

Report from the American Samoa Environmental Protection Agency PO Box PPA Pago Pago, AS 96799 2004

# **ABSTRACT**

The American Samoa archipelago is composed of five main islands: Tutuila, Aunu'u, Ta'u, Olosega, and Ofu.

--- SENTENCE 9 ---
Tutuila is the largest island (53 mi 2 ) with the most people (55,414 people as of 2000).

--- SENTENCE 10 ---
Population growth and its attendant pressures on natural habitats and resources has highlighted the need for aquatic resource monitoring in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
Of special concern are coral reef habitats, reef fish assemblages, and surface water ecosystems.

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
A watershed classification is a useful framework upon which to develop agency monitoring programs, especially stream and nearshore marine monitoring programs, as a classification scheme can provide *a priori* expectations concerning the condition of adjacent aquatic habitats.

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
US Census data from 2000 were used to calculate population density in Territorial watersheds, and density was used as a surrogate for human disturbance within each watershed.

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
From population density data, four watershed classes were defined: pristine (<100 individuals/mi<sup>2</sup> ), minimal (>100 but less than 500 individuals/mi<sup>2</sup> ), intermediate (>500 but less than 1000 individuals/mi<sup>2</sup> ), and extensive (>1000 individuals/mi<sup>2</sup> ).

--- SENTENCE 15 ---
Watershed classification is the first step to establishing an integrated monitoring program for Territorial waters.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
For instance, ASEPA developed a stream monitoring program based on this classification scheme.

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
Preliminary results from that stream monitoring are consistent with the expected changes in stream condition across watershed class.

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
Whether other aquatic habitats (e.g., beaches) are also consistent with this scheme is unknown at this time.

#### **INTRODUCTION**

The American Samoa archipelago, of which Tutuila is the largest island (53 mi<sup>2</sup> ), lies roughly 14 degrees south of the Equator between longitudes 169 and 173 west.

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
Other major islands in the archipelago are Aunu'u, Ta'u, Olosega, and Ofu.

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
With 55,414 people, Tutuila is the most densely populated island (average density=1045 individuals/mi<sup>2</sup> ).

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
Population growth and its attendant pressures on natural habitats and resources have highlighted the need for aquatic resource monitoring in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 22 ---
Of special concern are coral reef habitats, reef fish assemblages, and surface water ecosystems.

--- SENTENCE 23 ---
With respect to stream ecosystems, several studies have been commissioned to determine the current status and potential impact of population on freshwater resources (e.g., M&E Pacific 1979).

--- SENTENCE 24 ---
Studies have concluded that freshwater resources are still in good condition, although concerns for recharge of underground aquifers suggest that groundwater resources could suffer from land use changes and pollution impacts in the near future.

--- SENTENCE 25 ---
Furthermore, increased development may directly impact the stream ecosystems themselves; many streams, especially in the populated areas, are extensively modified.

--- SENTENCE 26 ---
Many more will continue to change as land use and resource extraction patterns continue to support the island's burgeoning population.

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
TheAmericanSamoaEnvironmental Protection Agency (ASEPA) is responsible for tracking the condition of local aquatic resources, including stream, beach, and nearshore marine habitats.

--- SENTENCE 28 ---
The ASEPA has recently adopted a watershed approach to monitoring and assessing these ecosystems (Pedersen 2000).

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
Concurrent with many watershed-level programs, the ASEPA will monitor stream ecosystems to assess the overall condition of these habitats in the Territory.

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
A watershed classification is a useful framework upon which to develop agency monitoring

programs, especially stream and nearshore marine monitoring.

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
Since most,if not all, of the pollutants or human impacts on these ecosystems are coming from the surrounding watershed, a classification scheme can provide *a priori* expectations for the condition of adjacent aquatic habitats.

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
### **METHODS**

Watersheds are often classified using data concerning surrounding land use types.

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
For instance, the percentage of total area that is covered with asphalt is correlated to stream condition, i.e., the level of nutrient enhancement, the integrity of the biological community, riparian modifications, etc. (Karr and Chu 1999).

--- SENTENCE 34 ---
Other variables include percentage of land in agricultural production and percent forested land.

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
Many of these variables are calculated from GIS software platforms.

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
GIS assessments and land use mapping are beginning in earnest on Tutuila; however, these efforts are not yet mature enough to assess all 41 watersheds of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
Instead, US Census data from 2000 were used to approximate the level of human disturbance within local watersheds.

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
Data were downloaded from the Census 2000 website (www.census2000.gov).

--- SENTENCE 39 ---
Watershed populations were calculated by summing village population totals within each watershed (following Pedersen 2000).

--- SENTENCE 40 ---
After current populations were determined, a cumulative distribution function (CDF) was plotted to examine the distribution of population data.

--- SENTENCE 41 ---
Watershed size varies by over an order-ofmagnitude (0.40 to 14.2 mi<sup>2</sup> ), so population estimates were normalized to watershed size.

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
Population density was plotted with a CDF.

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
Watersheds were classified based on the distribution of population densities.

#### **RESULTS**

Watershed-level data are summarized in Table 1.

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
Population varied from 0 (e.g, Aoloau Sisifo, Fagatuitui, Tau Saute) to 17,256 (Tafuna Plain).

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
Population numbers in two watersheds could not be directly determined.

--- SENTENCE 46 ---
The upper region of Aoloau Sasae has some houses that are part of the village of Aoloau, but the exact number of residents is unknown.

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
Similarly, the area surrounding Fagatele and Larson Bays has some houses and evidently some residents that the census data do not enumerate.

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
The raw population data are continuously distributed up to the population of Lauli'i-Aumi (1,460 residents); the next lowest watershed population is Leone (6,600).

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
The CDF for population density (Figure 1) provides a better basis for classifying watersheds using population data.

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
The first 27% of the watersheds (11 out of 41) have population densities less than 100 individuals/mi<sup>2</sup> .

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
There is a large cluster of 22 watersheds that show a continuous distribution of population density.

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
Thereafter, there is another gap in the distribution, and the last 8 watersheds have high population densities.

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
The break in the distribution of the first group was arbitrarily set at 100 individuals/mi<sup>2</sup> .

--- SENTENCE 54 ---
Watersheds below that density point were considered **pristine**.

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
Aoloau Sasae and Fagatele-Larson were included in this grouping because of the very low (albeit unquantified) population in those watersheds.

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
The major break at around 1,000 individuals/mi<sup>2</sup> marked the transition into watersheds showing **extensive** modification.

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
The middle grouping was divided roughly in half, with a cutoff point established at 500 individuals/mi<sup>2</sup> .

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
This was an arbitrary decision, but, in lieu of other supporting data to more carefully distinguish among these watersheds, this decision was acceptable.

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
Thus, below the 500 individuals/mi<sup>2</sup> but greater than 100 individuals/mi<sup>2</sup> , watersheds were considered as showing **minimal** disturbance.

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
Above 500 but less than 1000 individuals/mi<sup>2</sup> , units were classified as showing **intermediate** disturbance.

--- SENTENCE 61 ---

density, and the resulting classification for each watershed are summarized in Table 1.

As an example, the results are plotted on a map for the island of Tutuila (Figure 2).

#### **DISCUSSION**

Watershed classification is the first step to establishing an integrated monitoring program for Territorial waters.

--- SENTENCE 64 ---
Using the classification levels as strata, for instance, ASEPA developed a stream monitoring program that *a priori* accounts for a significant level of variability (i.e., human disturbance) in these island streams .

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
Furthermore, this classification scheme provides an expectation for the degree of beach contamination (i.e., we expect more contaminated beaches from extensively modified watersheds than from pristine watersheds).

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
The distribution of population density (the response variable) has two visible breaks, which are used in this scheme as boundaries.

--- SENTENCE 67 ---
The first break, set arbitrarily at 100 individuals/mi<sup>2</sup> , marks the upper boundary of watersheds considered pristine.

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
Six of these watersheds (Fagatuitui, Aoloau Sisifo, Aunuu Sasae, Olosega Sasae, and Tau Saute) are uninhabited, while three have low resident populations (10 in Ofu Matu, 17 in Maloata, 39 in Fagamalo).

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
There is no direct data for two watersheds (Aoloau Sasae and Fagatele-Larson), but the small number of residential structures in these watersheds, if they were accurately censussed, would likely have a final population density below 100 individuals/mi<sup>2</sup> .

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
The second obvious break sets offthewatersheds where population density is highest (>1,000 individuals/mi<sup>2</sup> ).

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
These watersheds are primarily found in the Pago Pago Harbor area and the Tafuna-Leone Plain (Figure 2).

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
One other watershed (Alao) is on the far eastern end of Tutuila, and the density there is 1,015 individuals/mi<sup>2</sup> .

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
Aerial photography of this watershed, taken back in 1994, indicates some development, but certainly not in the same category as Tafuna Plain or Nuuuli Pala.

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
This may be a situation where the population density is high due to a very small watershed, and this watershed may be more appropriately placed in a different category.

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
However, at this time, Alao will stay classified as showing extensive disturbance.

--- SENTENCE 76 ---
Lastly, the group in the middle (between the two natural breaks in the distribution) is composed of 22 watersheds.

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
As there is not an obvious break in the distribution, a point was chosen to divide this into 2 roughly equal groups.

--- SENTENCE 78 ---
At 500 individuals/mi<sup>2</sup> , 13 watersheds are below and 9 are above this arbitrary point.

--- SENTENCE 79 ---
There might not be a big difference between Poloa (483 individuals/mi<sup>2</sup> ) and Onenoa (510 individuals/mi<sup>2</sup> ), but these watersheds are classified into two different groups (minimal and intermediate, respectively).

--- SENTENCE 80 ---
One concern is that, since some watersheds are so small, this might skew the estimates of population density (e.g., Alao).

--- SENTENCE 81 ---
For instance, Amanave, with a population of 287, is in the lower third of the watershed population distribution.

--- SENTENCE 82 ---
However, its density is in the upper third.

--- SENTENCE 83 ---
This suggests that an artifact of the density calculation could cause misclassification of watersheds if the correspondence between population size and density ranks was not very strong.

--- SENTENCE 84 ---
This was tested by rank-transforming both population and density for each watershed.

--- SENTENCE 85 ---
While some, like Amanave, are different, there is a significant positive relationship (*D*=0.828, p<0.001), suggesting that the classification scheme based on density is robust.

--- SENTENCE 86 ---
Lastly, a map of Tutuila watersheds coded by classification level (Figure 2) shows that the extensively disturbed areas on the island are concentrated between the Harbor and the Tafuna Plain.

--- SENTENCE 87 ---
The impact of recent development on these areas is obvious from aerial photographs.

--- SENTENCE 88 ---
The pristine watersheds are predominantly located on the northern side of the island, while minimal and

intermediate disturbance is scattered across both eastern and western watersheds.

#### Utility in Aquatic Monitoring

There are 163 streams on Tutuila, 141 of which are considered perennial (Burger and Maciolek 1981).

--- SENTENCE 89 ---
Most of the streams are short (median length of all streams=1 km), and stream drainage areas are typically small.

--- SENTENCE 90 ---
Specifically, 122/141 (87%) of the perennial stream basins are less than 1 km<sup>2</sup> .

--- SENTENCE 91 ---
The rugged, steep terrain of Tutuila means that most of the streams have steep gradients: the median grade of perennial streams on Tutuila is 21.8%.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
While stream water quality and biotic communities are essential indicators of watershed health, very little systematic research has occurred on the stream ecosystems of Tutuila (Cook 2004).

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
There are extensive data records on stream flows at various permanent gauging stations around Tutuila (Wong 1996).

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
In addition, selected sites have been sampled for physico-chemical (e.g., nutrients, pH, temperature, dissolved oxygen) and biological (e.g., faunal lists and qualitative abundances) variables.

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
However, much of this sampling was done on a one-time basis.

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
There has been no effort to survey the streams systematically and consistently.

--- SENTENCE 97 ---
Systematic monitoring of Tutuila streams will provide data necessary to evaluate environmental condition and detect degradation in freshwater systems.

--- SENTENCE 98 ---
In order to begin a comprehensive stream monitoring program, the ASEPA adopted a probabilistic approach based on subsampling the population of Tutuila streams after they had been classified at the watershed level as described above.

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
After 1 year of monitoring, stream data generally agree with the *a priori* expectations.

--- SENTENCE 100 ---
For many of the parameters measured, there was a statistically significant increase (or decrease, in some cases) across streams from the 4 watershed classes.

--- SENTENCE 101 ---
The details of this analysis are outlined in "ASEPA Stream Monitoring: Results from Year 1 and

Preliminary Interpretation" (DiDonato 2004).

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
This watershed approach has also been used as a backbone for other monitoring activities within the agency.

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
For example, local coral reef monitoring done in collaboration with colleagues from the Commonwealth of Northern Mariana Islands (CNMI) was based on selecting sites from across the expected gradient of disturbance.

--- SENTENCE 104 ---
Initial results from that research show that some measures of coral reef health at the reef crest are in fact inversely correlated with adjacent human population levels (Houk et al. in press).

--- SENTENCE 105 ---
Furthermore, watershed and adjacent coral reef flat monitoring done in conjunction with US Fish and Wildlife Service personnel was also based on the watershed classification scheme.

--- SENTENCE 106 ---
Initial results from this survey work will be reported soon.

--- SENTENCE 107 ---
In conclusion, the watershed classification scheme provides an organizational framework for agency monitoring efforts.

--- SENTENCE 108 ---
Initial results with stream monitoring and coral reef crest monitoring suggest that the condition of these natural systems is related to watershed population density.

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
However, further study is necessary to determine if these initial trends continue and if these relationships are true for other systems (e.g. nearshore beaches).

## GROWTH-RATE OF SAMOAN CORALS.

--- SENTENCE 2 ---
By Alfred Goldsborough Mayor.

--- SENTENCE 3 ---
By far the most accurate and extensive observations upon the growth-rate of corals are those of Vaughan, published in Year Book No. 14, Carnegie Institution of Washington (pp. 221–231).

--- SENTENCE 4 ---
Vaughan studied the corals of Tortugas, Florida, and of Andros Island, Bahamas.

--- SENTENCE 5 ---
He reared the planulæ of several species and weighed and measured other corals with their bases embedded in concrete attached to tiles, which were staked out with iron stakes upon the reef-flats, and he also observed the growth-rate of corals growing naturally upon the reefs.

--- SENTENCE 6 ---
All of the forms studied by Vaughan were shallow-water species, such as constitute the reefs of the Florida-Bahama region.

--- SENTENCE 7 ---
He found that, on the average, corals of the genus Acropora1 made an annual growth of 54 mm.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
Thus a reef composed of the massive *Porites* of the Pacific might grow upward three times as fast as one composed of the Atlantic Orbicella annularis.

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
Vaughan estimates that a reef composed of Orbicella annularis might attain a thickness of 150 feet in between 6,500 and 7,600 years, and according to my estimate such a reef composed of the massive *Porites* of Samoa might develop in 2,700 years; but neither of these corals grows at depths of 150 feet, so such a reef would have to start on talus, or on a structure built up by the very slow-building corals which grow at depths greater than 20 fathoms.

In June 1890, Saville-Kent measured and photographed certain corals growing off Vivien Point, Thursday Island, Australia, and some of these were remeasured by me in November 1913.

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
The most reliable study of growth-rate of Pacific corals which has hitherto been published is that of J. Stanley Gardiner, Fauna and Geography of the Maldive and Laccadive Archipelagoes (Cambridge University Press, vol. 1, pp.

--- SENTENCE 34 ---
Gardiner observed the size attained by young coral stocks which had grown in an artificial channel through the reef at Hululu, North Male Atoll, Maldives, which had been cleared of all coral 3 years previous to his study.

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
He weighed, measured, ascertained the volume, specific gravity, and area in square centimeters covered by these corals, and presents his results in the form of a table and concludes that the average upward growth of these corals was 77 mm.

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
in not more than 3 years, and that if broken down into a sheet they would form a layer of limestone 24.2 mm.

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
Thus these corals made an average upward growth of 25.6 mm. per annum.

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
This is nearly in accord with the 24 mm. which is my calculated general average increase in height for the corals of Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 39 ---
As Gardiner says, this growth might give rise to a reef about 14.5 fathoms in thickness in 1,000 years; while according to my estimate a reef 13.5 fathoms in thickness might be formed in this time.

--- SENTENCE 40 ---
This slight difference has no meaning, due to the large factor of error in such estimates, but the apparent agreement in our results would indicate that the modern reefs now constituting the atolls and barriers of the Pacific could readily have grown upward to sea-level from the floors of submerged platforms since the close of the last glacial epoch.

--- SENTENCE 41 ---
The growth-rate of corals of Cocos-Keeling Atoll has been studied by F. Wood Jones and reported in his well-known book on *Coral and Atolls*, 1912, and also in a pamphlet upon the *Rate of Growth of the Reef-Building Corals*, published in London in 1908.

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
Wood Jones finds that corals do not grow steadily, but by fits and starts, with no apparent reason for this activity or for its absence.

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
This is amply confirmed by my studies in Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
For example, a massive *Porites* in Pago Pago Harbor

grew 37 mm.

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
in diameter in 1917–18, and then made no growth the following year; but in 1919-20 it again started to enlarge and amost made up its loss by growing 54 mm.

--- SENTENCE 46 ---
in diameter in this last year.

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
Wood Jones fastened copper bands to the stems of branching corals, the band being placed at a distance of 10 centimeters from the distal extremity.

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
He then observed the growth made in 100 days and states that the average growth was 27.4 mm.

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
in the 100 days, but unfortunately he tells nothing of what species of branching corals he studied, except that they were chiefly "Madrepores" (Acropora).

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
In stating the growth-rate of massive forms he falls into error, for he states that such corals add 1/37 of their original circumference to their growth in 100 days, which amounts to practically one-tenth the circumference (or the diameter) in a year.

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
Thus, according to Wood Jones a massive Porites 100 mm.

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
in diameter would become 110 mm.

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
in diameter at the end of a year, gaining 10 mm.; while a head 500 mm.

--- SENTENCE 54 ---
in diameter would gain 50 mm.

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
in the same time; or, as Wood Jones puts it, "a paradox is arrived at, for the older a coral is the faster it grows."

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
This would indeed be a paradox were it true, but it is obviously untrue.

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
For example, 8 massive Porites heads studied by me in Samoa had an average initial diameter of 339 mm. and made an average gain in diameter of 37.5 mm. per year; whereas 9 larger Porites heads having an average original diameter of 1,620 mm. made an average gain in diameter of 28 mm. per annum.

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
In fact, the observations of Vaughan, as well as those made by me, indicate that young corals grow more rapidly than old ones and that in most and perhaps all species, after a certain size has been attained, growth practically ceases.

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
Moreover, I find from studies made on *Porites clavaria* at Tortugas, Florida, that if a large coral which has ceased to grow, or is growing only slowly, be broken apart and its branches planted in concrete, these isolated branches continue to grow at the slow rate of the old stock and do not gain in growth-rate by being separated.

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
The same is true if we girdle branches of old coral stocks of *Porites* by encircling them with a lethal mixture of corrosive sublimate and iodine, for then the tips of such physiologically isolated branches do not regain the growth-rate of young stocks, but still retain the reduced rate characteristic of senile corals.

--- SENTENCE 61 ---
In fact, this "experiment" is often made in nature, for it is a common thing to find the hard part of a stem of Acropora or Porites girdled by sea-weed, millepora, or lithothamnium, and yet such a branch does not gain in growth-rate through being isolated from the stock.

--- SENTENCE 62 ---
But to return to the observations of Wood Jones, he states that his results are practically in accord with those of H. B. Guppy (1889, Scottish Geographical Magazine, vol. 5, pp.

--- SENTENCE 63 ---
573-575), who concludes that in the Cocos-Keeling Atoll massive forms of *Porites* grow upward between 12.7 and 19 mm. per year, while the branching *Porites* (palmata?) grows about 30 mm., and Acropora and certain forms of Montipora at least 100 to 125 mm. per year; and we may say that these figures would also apply quite well to the growth-rate of corals in the shallows of the reef-flats of Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 64 ---
Sluiter (1889, Naturkundig Tijdschrift Nederland Indie, Bd. 29, p. 375) found that a young reef, which grew up after the explosion at the Black Cliff of Krakatoa, had grown to a thickness of 200 mm.

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
in not more than 5 years; but by contrast

## EXPERIMENTS AND OBSERVATIONS.

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
Seven sets of experiments were carried out upon the growth-rate of corals between 1917 and 1920.

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
One set of observations (table 2) was made upon photographed, weighed, and measured corals which were collected from the Aua reef-flat on the eastern side of Pago Pago Harbor.

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
Figures 1 to 49, plates 2–14, illustrate these corals.

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
After being marked by attaching numbered brass tags to these corals by means of copper wire, some of them were fastened to iron stakes driven into the reef-flat in quiet water 400 feet from shore, in a region wherein Porites flourishes.

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
Other colonies had their basal stocks embedded in concrete 700 feet from shore in a region wherein Acropora is dominant and the water is agitated by the surge from the breakers.

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
In this way 50 corals were treated and 38 of these survived and were found in July 1918.

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
Observations upon corals growing naturally and undisturbed upon the reef-flats of Pago Pago showed that these weighed, photographed, and planted corals appeared to grow at a normal rate, due probably to the fact that the planted corals were removed from the water only long enough to photograph and weigh them, and were at once replaced upon the reef-flat.

--- SENTENCE 76 ---
Another set of observations, referred to in table 3 and figures 50 to 52, plate 15, were made upon 22 coral-heads growing naturally upon the reef-flats of Pago Pago Harbor.

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
These were first measured in April 1917 and again in July 1918.

--- SENTENCE 78 ---
A third series consisted of 20 corals which were collected by means of a diving-hood at depths of 2 to 3 fathoms off the seaward edge of the Aua Reef in a region moderately agitated by breakers.

--- SENTENCE 79 ---
A dense clustering of Acropora occurs in this place, at least three-quarters of the surface of the submarine slope being covered thickly with these corals.

--- SENTENCE 80 ---
The corals were removed from the water only long enough to number, weigh, photograph, and embed their basal parts in concrete on tiles which were then replaced at a depth of 2.5 fathoms, close to the place where they were collected.

--- SENTENCE 81 ---
These corals were numbered 32 D to 51 D, and the results of this experiment are recorded in table 4 and in figures 32 D to 51 D, plates 19-26.

--- SENTENCE 82 ---
Another set of observations was made upon 7 specimens of Acropora (Tylopora) leptocyathus Brook which were growing naturally on the breaker-washed edge of a

reef-patch off Aua Village, Pago Pago Harbor.

An idea of the appearance and association of these corals may be gained from plate IV, fig.

--- SENTENCE 87 ---
In order to test the relative growth-rates of corals in the highly agitated shallow water of the upper surface of the reef-flat, and in deep, quiet water off the seaward edge of the reef, 24 corals of medium size from the upper surface of the Aua reef-flat were sawed into approximately equal halves.

--- SENTENCE 88 ---
One-half marked S was embedded in concrete on the upper surface of a reef-patch off Aua, Pago Pago Harbor, in pure, highly agitated shallow water about 8 inches deep at lowest tides.

--- SENTENCE 89 ---

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
These sunken corals were in quiet water away from the surge of breakers and in a region in which we could detect no current by means of an Ekman meter.

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
The bottom was covered with fine silt which clung like dust to every object and was at once fatal to all the Acropora.

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
As none of these corals was in its natural environment, the water being either too agitated or too quiet, their growth-rates are not considered, but they are regarded merely as examples of the effects of agitation, or of stagnant water and silt, upon the growth-form of corals.

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
The results are recorded in table 8 and are illustrated for the corals in pure, agitated, shallow water by figures 1 s to 24 s, and for the halves grown in deep, quiet, silted water by figures 1 D to 24 D, except that illustrations of the Acropora and others which died without growing are not shown.

--- SENTENCE 97 ---
In general, the corals in agitated water produced short, thick-set stems, while those in the deep, quiet water developed long, slender stems.

--- SENTENCE 98 ---
F. Wood Jones, in his Coral and Atolls, 1912, has well described these effects for Pocillopora and branched Porites.

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
but our results show that Psammocora, Pavona, and even Acropora may also respond in a similar manner to these influences.

--- SENTENCE 100 ---
Vaughan observed a similar response in an Agaricea grown in agitated water at Tortugas, Florida.

--- SENTENCE 101 ---
In order to determine the growth-rate of corals as affecting the development of the reefs, we must consider corals which die or do not grow as well as those which flourish, and take the average of the entire set of observations.

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
Some corals, such as the massive *Porites* and other huge heads, certainly live a century or more, but most of the species, such as the numerous *Acropora*, *Pocillopora*, and branched *Porites*, are short-lived and probably attain full growth in less than 10 years.

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
Indeed, Vaughan showed that in *Favia fragum* of the Atlantic the growth is rapid during the first year, after which it declines, and in *Mæandra areolata* 

۲,

there is a decided falling off in growth-rate after the coral has attained a length of about 60 mm.

--- SENTENCE 104 ---
Not one of the Acroporas, Pocilloporas, and branched Porites measured by Saville Kent in 1890 off Vivien Point, Thursday Island, Australia, could be found by me in 1913, and in Samoa it was evident that Acroporas, which had attained to large size and perfection of condition, generally the next year showed signs of deterioration and were more or less covered with sea-weed, millepores, bryozoa, or lithothamnium, and had made little growth.

--- SENTENCE 105 ---
Thus, if we confine our measurements to small heads, we will probably get a rapid growth-rate, whereas large senile heads will give much less.

--- SENTENCE 106 ---
The endeavor was therefore made to select specimens of average size over the Samoan reefs.

--- SENTENCE 107 ---
If a coral gains 9 mm. in diameter in 9 months it may be expected to attain 12 mm.

--- SENTENCE 108 ---
in 12 months, the dimensions following a linear ratio proportionate to the time; but if we wished to calculate the weight at the end of the year the problem would be more complex.

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
In hemispherical or oval massive heads, such as Porites lutea, Symphyllia, or Goniastrea, the weight may be expected to increase as the cube of the diameter.

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
Thus if D be the diameter at the beginning of the year and if d be the increase in diameter due to a year's growth, then if  $D^2$  represent the weight at the beginning of the year,  $(D+d)^2$  will represent the weight at the end of the year.

--- SENTENCE 111 ---
Often, however, these corals grow in very shallow water and are thus killed on top at low tide-level, so that they can grow only laterally as a ring of living polypites constituting the vertical outer margin of a cylinder, and in this case the weight gained per annum must be simply proportional to the relative diameters of the cylinder at the beginning and at the end of the year.

--- SENTENCE 112 ---
In most of the branching forms, such as *Porites andrewsi*, and the numerous species of *Acropora* and *Pocillopora*, the branches radiate outward from a basal center, and as the coral grows new branches develop from the sides of the old ones, so that the tips of the branches always remain approximately at a constant distance apart.

--- SENTENCE 113 ---
In these cases one would expect the weight to increase as the square of the average linear dimension.

--- SENTENCE 114 ---
Thus, if a coral of weight (w) has an average dimension of (d), where  $d = \frac{(\text{length} + \text{breadth} + \text{height})}{3}$ , and if this coral grows so that it attains a weight w and an average dimension of D, then

$$W = \frac{D^2 w}{d^2}$$

--- SENTENCE 115 ---
This formula would be strictly true if the branches all arose from the base and not at various levels from the sides of older branches, and if they were cylindrical and not tapering conically from base to tip.

--- SENTENCE 116 ---
It seems, however, that these two factors about offset each other; for taking the average gain in weight in 15 months made by 6 branched Acropora, 4 Porites, 4 Pocillopora, and 1 Pavona foliosa of Samoa it was found that the observed average weight at the end of the 15 months was 1.09 times the expected average weight as calculated by the formula given above, the calculated and the observed gain in weight thus being practically identical.

Making use of a white board and a pencil tied to a string it was possible to take elaborate notes upon the reef conditions while one walked around among the coral-heads at depths down to about 6 fathoms.

--- SENTENCE 215 ---

--- SENTENCE 217 ---

--- SENTENCE 218 ---
Beneath this overhanging edge there is a precipice or steep slope of from about 1 to 6 fathoms, at the base of which Acropora clusters thickly.

--- SENTENCE 219 ---

--- SENTENCE 220 ---

--- SENTENCE 221 ---

--- SENTENCE 222 ---

--- SENTENCE 223 ---

About 502,200 heads of *Porites* grow over 72 per cent of the area of the upper surface of the reef-flat and contribute (due to their growth) 246,000 pounds of limestone per annum.

--- SENTENCE 402 ---
347,500 Acropora distributed over 56 per cent of the reef-flat contribute annuly 465,400 pounds of limestone.

--- SENTENCE 403 ---
57,600 heads of *Pocillopora* scattered over the entire reef-flat contribute 30,300 pounds of limestone.

--- SENTENCE 404 ---
55,900 *Psammocora* distributed over 30 per cent of the reef-flat contribute each year 11,000 pounds of limestone.

--- SENTENCE 405 ---
15,500 Pavona found over 35 per cent of the reef-flat contribute each year about 11,600 pounds of limestone.

--- SENTENCE 406 ---
Thus the growth of these corals appears to add about 764,300 pounds of limestone each year to the upper surface of the reef-flat, this limestone being estimated as dried and free from animal matter; and as these genera contribute 95 per cent of the entire coral-heads of the reef-flat, it is probable that about 800,000 pounds of limestone are added to the reef-flat each year by the growth of the corals over its surface, or about 2.8 pounds per square yard; or, taking the specific gravity of limestone as 1.8, this would be equivalent to a layer of coral about 8 mm.

--- SENTENCE 408 ---
The corals of Samoa are more effective as reef builders than are those of the Atlantic according to Vaughan.

--- SENTENCE 409 ---
The massive Porites of Samoa which takes the place of Orbicella annularis of the Atlantic grows upward about 17 mm. per annum instead of 6 to 7 mm., as does the Atlantic Orbicella.

--- SENTENCE 410 ---
Moreover, one never sees in the Atlantic anything like the dense clustering of vigorous stocks of Acropora, such as characterize the seaward slopes of the Samoan reefs.

--- SENTENCE 411 ---
A far greater proportion of the ground is covered by corals in Samoa than on the Atlantic reefs, and while the growth-rate of Samoan Acropora is only slightly greater than that of the Atlantic species according to Vaughan, yet the far greater abundance of this genus over the Samoan reefs causes it to be more effective as a reef builder than are the Atlantic Acropora.

--- SENTENCE 412 ---
In middle Oligocene times the Atlantic reefs were probably comparable with those of the modern Pacific, but the Atlantic reefs have been impoverished by the dying out of many forms which still survive in the Pacific.

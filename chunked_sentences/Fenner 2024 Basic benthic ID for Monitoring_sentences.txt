### Introduction

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
This is a basic guide to identifying life found in benthic monitoring surveys in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
It will cover the major groups such as algae, sessile invertebrates, relatives of corals, soft corals, hard corals, indicator species, and various kinds of damage including bleaching, predation, and diseases.

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
Identification will only be of the most common or important species, non-corals will only to be to genus and in some cases with no scientific name.

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
There will be references to works that have many more species and much more advanced identification, so things can be identified further.

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
But there will be some general info on the importance of different groups, and some basic clues for identification, including the feel of things as well as their looks.

--- SENTENCE 61 ---
In general, it is my feeling that in monitoring it is good to record as much detail as possible, to record to as fine a category as possible.

--- SENTENCE 62 ---
You can always lump things together later if you want to.

--- SENTENCE 63 ---
If you have a video or photo record of your transect then indeed you can go back and record more detail later if you want to.

--- SENTENCE 64 ---
But if you do something like point intercept like I do, then if you don't record it when you first record the data, you don't have it and can't go back and do it in greater detail later.

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
So that is an advantage of a video or photo method.

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
On the other hand, if I ID things under water, I can do it with greater detail than I can do from photos or video.

--- SENTENCE 67 ---
Things that are clear under water are sometimes blurry or off color in video or photos.

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
I also like to look at the reef while I am collecting monitoring data, since I can often notice interesting or important things that may not be in my transects.

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
An important part of monitoring is keeping an eye open for anything unusual, as well as just learning about the reef and trying to understand it better.

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
Two of the most important groups on the bottom on coral reefs are corals and algae.

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
We often put a lot more emphasis on corals than algae, but algae are actually often just as important as corals, and in some cases are just as pretty.

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
I will begin with algae.

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
### ALGAE

 Algae can be grouped by taxonomic groups or by functional groups.

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
We will do some of both, grouping them by taxonomy but discussing the functional groups as well.

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
Monitoring categories are often by functional group.

--- SENTENCE 76 ---
Functional groups include turf (filamentous algae), macroalgae, coralline algae, and cyanobacteria.

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
The taxonomic groups are identified by color, green, brown, red, yellow, and blue-green (=cyanobacteria).

--- SENTENCE 78 ---
Algae are natural, there is quite a bit of it on reefs that are far from people, so having quite a bit is not necessarily a bad thing.

--- SENTENCE 79 ---
But some types bloom when there are too many nutrients or not enough herbivores, so it is good to monitor those.

--- SENTENCE 80 ---
I'll be pointing out which ones those are.

--- SENTENCE 81 ---
Other types of algae produce calcium and are major contributors to building reefs, in some places they can contribute even more than corals.

--- SENTENCE 82 ---
So they are good things to have on a reef, and I'll be pointing out which ones are big contributors.

#### Green algae (Chlorophyta)

 Green algae are virtually always green, and algae in the other groups are not green, so color is a pretty good ID tool for groups.

--- SENTENCE 83 ---
Some green algae are "filamentous" or "turf."

--- SENTENCE 84 ---
Filamentous is made of fine filaments made of strings of single cells.

--- SENTENCE 85 ---
Turf is a mixture of filamentous algae species but may also include some species that are like thin wires.

--- SENTENCE 86 ---
In some systems, turf is considered anything less than about 2 cm (1 inch) tall.

--- SENTENCE 87 ---
But there are filamentous species that make somewhat larger clumps.

--- SENTENCE 88 ---
Those larger species are relatively rare, so the problem of how to record them isn't important most of the time.

--- SENTENCE 89 ---
Turf algae is important on reefs as fish food.

--- SENTENCE 90 ---
It grows fast but doesn't have toxic chemicals or lots of cellulose, so it is nutritious.

--- SENTENCE 91 ---
Most herbivorous fish concentrate on turf algae, and it actually grows faster when it is cropped by herbivores.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
A reef that has little turf will generally have fewer fish than a reef with turf, so turf is good for fish.

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
The filaments are too small to ID to even genus, and they are usually mixed assemblages and

can have filamentous red or brown species as well as green.

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
It can also be called filamentous algae or algal lawn.

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
Virtually every surface on a reef has something growing on it, and newly killed coral starts to turn green from turf in a few days.

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
Rock that is not pearly white has some turf growing on it.

--- SENTENCE 97 ---
Fish can keep it cropped close and hard to see except for the color.

!

--- SENTENCE 98 ---
[](_page_5_Picture_1.jpeg)

Green turf growing where a damselfish has bitten off coral polyps.

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
The brown fuzzy algae is also turf.

!

--- SENTENCE 100 ---
[](_page_6_Picture_0.jpeg)

Turf, which is another term for filamentous algae.

--- SENTENCE 101 ---
Every surface on a reef that doesn't have something else growing on it quickly grows turf.

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
Any place where coral is newly dead or the reef is broken will be white immediately after the coral dies or the reef is broken.

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
Very quickly, within a few days, the white surface will turn green with filamentous algae, also called turf.

--- SENTENCE 104 ---
Anything that isn't white has something growing on it, which is virtually all surfaces on reefs (bare volcanic basalt would be black).

--- SENTENCE 105 ---
Turf is commonly green fuzz, though it can be brown.

--- SENTENCE 106 ---
Turf or filamentous algae does not have distinct "leaves" which are called thallus in macroalgae.

--- SENTENCE 107 ---
Turf grows extremely fast, and is not defended with chemicals, calcium, or cellulose.

--- SENTENCE 108 ---
So it is excellent fish food, and many fish eat it.

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
When a fish scrapes the turf off, the base of the filament is left attached to the rock, and quickly grows back.

!

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
[](_page_7_Picture_0.jpeg)

Chorodesmis, a filamentous green alga that forms distinctive clumps.

--- SENTENCE 111 ---
It contains toxic chemicals.

--- SENTENCE 112 ---
Caulerpa is a type of macroalgae and a fun genus, not very common, but fairly easy to ID to genus and even species.

--- SENTENCE 113 ---
They are amazing in that a whole plant is all one cell, so it has no divisions by cell walls, but it has many nuclei.

--- SENTENCE 114 ---
Each round ball is a "thallus."

!

--- SENTENCE 115 ---
[](_page_8_Picture_1.jpeg)

Caulerpa racemosa

 Halimeda is one of the most important genera of macroalgae.

--- SENTENCE 116 ---
Each plant is one cell with many nuclei.

--- SENTENCE 117 ---
It has plates that are usually in strings.

--- SENTENCE 118 ---
Each plate produces calcium inside.

--- SENTENCE 119 ---
When the plate dies the calcium is then in the form of a porous flake.

--- SENTENCE 120 ---
Halimeda is abundant some places, so it produces lots of calcium, and some sands are made of all these little flakes or chips that are from Halimeda.

--- SENTENCE 121 ---
So it is a major reef and sand builder, and makes an important contribution to reefs.

--- SENTENCE 122 ---
There are several other kinds of algae that contribute calcium, which we will point out as we go.

--- SENTENCE 123 ---
Halimeda is pretty common on some lower reef slopes, occasionally it can be the dominant form of life.

--- SENTENCE 124 ---
Halimeda is an example of what are called "macroalgae" or "fleshy algae" or "frondose algae."

--- SENTENCE 125 ---
Algae can pretty much be divided up into "turf" (filamentous algae) on the one hand, and "macroalgae" on the other.

--- SENTENCE 126 ---
They can also be divided into "calcifying" and "noncalcifying" based on whether they produce calcium, though the ones that do produce it vary in how much they produce.

--- SENTENCE 127 ---
Halimeda is one of the ones that produces a lot.

--- SENTENCE 128 ---
Each green segment is called a "thallus."

--- SENTENCE 129 ---
By the way, when you see it, often the segment at the end of a string is light green.

--- SENTENCE 130 ---
That is a new segment.

--- SENTENCE 131 ---
They can put on a new segment in only one night!

--- SENTENCE 132 ---
They fill it with cytoplasm from the rest of the segments.

--- SENTENCE 133 ---
The segments produce calcium probably partly to make themselves less attractive to eat.

--- SENTENCE 134 ---
In a sense the difference between turf and macroalgae is between fast-growing undefended and heavily browsed turf on the one hand, and on the other hand macroalgae that is slow-growing and heavily defended.

--- SENTENCE 135 ---
Macroalgae defends itself with calcium, and/or tough cellulose (as appealing to eat as wood), and/or nasty chemicals.

--- SENTENCE 136 ---
Most fish don't like to eat macroalgae, they prefer turf.

--- SENTENCE 137 ---
A reef that has more turf can feed more fish than one that has little turf.

!

--- SENTENCE 138 ---
[](_page_10_Picture_0.jpeg)

Halimeda

 Boodlea composita is a green alga formed of fibers in a tangled mass.

--- SENTENCE 139 ---
The fibers are a bit hard, so if you crush it in your hand it will feel crunchy.

--- SENTENCE 140 ---
It seems to respond to nutrients, coming and going near land.

--- SENTENCE 141 ---
The coming and going may be in response to pulses of nutrients coming out of streams.

--- SENTENCE 142 ---
An alga like Boodlea composita that is stimulated by nutrients, can be a bioindicator of nutrients, much easier to use to detect nutrients than chemical analysis, since you just look for it instead of having to pay for fancy chemistry.

--- SENTENCE 143 ---
Also, the algae sum up the average nutrients, while if you take water samples for chemistry, most of the nutrients probably come out as pulses which you will miss unless you take a sample at just the right time during a pulse runoff rain event.

!

--- SENTENCE 144 ---
Boodlea composita

 Dictyosphaeria is a green alga that makes small plants that are usually tightly attached to the substrate and may be tucked between other hard things.

--- SENTENCE 145 ---
It is relatively solid, with no filaments or leaf-like structures.

--- SENTENCE 146 ---
Algae in this genus bloomed in Kaneohe Bay, Oahu, when the effluent from sewage plants was still piped into the bay, and it became less common when they extended the pipes outside the bay so sewage effluent no longer was released in the bay.

--- SENTENCE 147 ---
So it could be an indicator for nutrients.

--- SENTENCE 148 ---
It is not very obvious here.

!

--- SENTENCE 149 ---
This is Microdictyon, which is common on Rose and Swains, but rare or absent from Tutuila.

#### Brown algae (Phaeophyta)

 Brown algae have several important genera for reefs.

--- SENTENCE 150 ---
One is Dictyota, which forms small flattened strips that divide sideways.

--- SENTENCE 151 ---
It forms clumps, and can grow over corals.

--- SENTENCE 152 ---
It is one of the species that grows when a reef makes a phase shift from coral to algae, and it is likely stimulated by nutrients.

--- SENTENCE 153 ---
It is chemically defended with nasty chemicals, so fish don't like to eat it.

--- SENTENCE 154 ---
It may come and go in an area, which may be natural or follow nutrient inputs coming and going.

--- SENTENCE 155 ---
In 2008, it suddenly was growing all over the slopes including corals at Vatia.

--- SENTENCE 156 ---
It had been decreasing and then the tsunami took the rest away.

--- SENTENCE 157 ---
But it is a species to watch.

--- SENTENCE 158 ---
It doesn't produce calcium.

!

--- SENTENCE 159 ---
[](_page_14_Picture_2.jpeg)

Dictyota

 Padina is a brown algae that produces thin flat curled surfaces.

--- SENTENCE 160 ---
It is like Dictyota in that it is one of the species that can bloom in response to nutrients, and can increase when a reef shifts from coral to algae.

--- SENTENCE 161 ---
It is also said to be readily eaten by herbivorous fish, so when it is present it is an indicator that there are likely not enough herbivorous fish in that area.

--- SENTENCE 162 ---
For several years there was a lot of it along the shore of Coconut Pt.

--- SENTENCE 163 ---
It produces small amounts of calcium, so while it is flexible, it feels crunchy if you crush it in your hand.

!

--- SENTENCE 164 ---
[](_page_15_Picture_1.jpeg)

Padina

 Sargassum is a brown algae that is so rare here I have never seen it here.

--- SENTENCE 165 ---
It is reported from rocky intertidal.

--- SENTENCE 166 ---
It is illustrated in the guide to the seaweeds of American Samoa by Posa Skelton.

--- SENTENCE 167 ---
That guide is on pdf, is free, and I'd be glad to provide it to anyone.

--- SENTENCE 168 ---
It has more species than presented here.

--- SENTENCE 169 ---
Sargassum is one of the species that can take over when a reef changes phase from coral to algae, and it probably responds to nutrients.

--- SENTENCE 170 ---
The reef flat on the south side of the big island in Fiji is covered with it, a mass of it.

--- SENTENCE 171 ---
They have a lot of people in villages, piggeries, hotels releasing sewage, so lots of nutrients, also with lots of people they have fished the reefs pretty heavily.

--- SENTENCE 172 ---
There is no way to know whether nutrients cause the algae bloom or lack of herbivorous fish, or both.

--- SENTENCE 173 ---
I always say assume it is both, and work on both, because that way you won't pick the wrong one.

--- SENTENCE 174 ---
If you have a place where it looks like nutrients are probably to blame, then work on that, if there is a place where herbivorous fish are mostly gone, work on that.

--- SENTENCE 175 ---
By the way, herbivores are said to have "top down" control on algae, and nutrients have "bottom up" control of algae, and there is scientific evidence of both having effects on algae, though more studies report control by herbivores than by nutrients.

--- SENTENCE 176 ---
Worst is if you have both nutrients and lack herbivores.

--- SENTENCE 177 ---
The fact that our reef flat has almost no macroalgae most places is a stark contrast to the one in Fiji which is a mass of macroalgae, all bad types.

!

--- SENTENCE 178 ---
[](_page_16_Picture_1.jpeg)

Sargassum collected by Kelley Anderson Tagarino from a tide pool.

--- SENTENCE 179 ---
Turbinaria is a brown algae that consists of a clump of very tough spiky units connected by a thin stem.

--- SENTENCE 180 ---
It is known as one of the species that can take over in a phase shift from corals to algae, and is abundant along with Sargassum on the reef flats on the south side of Viti Levu, Fiji.

--- SENTENCE 181 ---
I did not see it on reef flats until recently, and now there appear to be some, with maybe 3-6 feet between them so they are not very obvious, but I did not see them on reef flats before.

--- SENTENCE 182 ---
This is one to watch.

!

#### Golden algae

--- SENTENCE 184 ---
This you won't find in the books, and there are only a few kinds known.

--- SENTENCE 185 ---
One type known from reefs is called "Golden Noodle" because it forms yellow noodle or tube-like extensions.

--- SENTENCE 186 ---
It is very soft and fragile, and will fall apart in your hands.

--- SENTENCE 187 ---
It is made of mucus with separate cells embedded in it.

--- SENTENCE 188 ---
It has only relatively recently been discovered.

--- SENTENCE 189 ---
It grows on rubble in shallow areas protected from waves, and comes and goes.

--- SENTENCE 190 ---
It may respond to nutrients.

!

Golden noodle algae, Chrysocystis fragilis

--- SENTENCE 192 ---
Sometimes you can see a thin thread-like stem in the middle of part of it, as in the picture below.

--- SENTENCE 193 ---
It doesn't seem to have a common name.

#### Red algae (Rhodophyta)

 Red algae usually are red colored, but some may be dark or brownish.

--- SENTENCE 195 ---
Green algae, brown algae and golden algae can be reliably placed in their groups based on color.

--- SENTENCE 196 ---
Most red algae are red or pink, but not all.

--- SENTENCE 197 ---
Probably the most important red algae are coralline algae.

--- SENTENCE 198 ---
The most common kind of them is encrusting, and it looks like purple or pink paint on rock.

--- SENTENCE 199 ---
It is very much a live plant, but it produces hard calcium underneath it, and is an important contributor to reef building.

--- SENTENCE 200 ---
Although most are purple, pink, or red, a few can be tan, brown, or yellow.

--- SENTENCE 201 ---
Encrusting coralline algae are relatively smooth, hard, and not slimy.

--- SENTENCE 202 ---
Corals always are spiny and feel rough like sandpaper to the touch (unless they are very fleshy, in which case they are slimy).

--- SENTENCE 203 ---
They are never ever smooth and not slimy.

--- SENTENCE 204 ---
Coralline algae needs to have a clean surface.

--- SENTENCE 205 ---
They don't grow well in sediment, so they are rare in areas with lots of sediment, and they don't grow well with other algae growing on them, so there is less of it when turf or macroalgae is common.

--- SENTENCE 206 ---
Actually, if you look under turf or macroalgae, you may find some of it, so that is not a totally strict requirement.

--- SENTENCE 207 ---
But it is a good indicator of low sediment.

--- SENTENCE 208 ---
It also grows well in water motion, probably because that helps clean sediment off of it.

--- SENTENCE 209 ---

#### Diatoms

 Diatoms are single cells that form a pillbox shaped shell around them made of silica glass.

--- SENTENCE 289 ---
Most are phytoplankton, floating around in the water.

--- SENTENCE 290 ---
But a few live stuck to objects.

--- SENTENCE 291 ---
Rarely, a coral may have a yellowish area, which may be diatoms living on the surface of the coral.

--- SENTENCE 292 ---
The following photo may be an example.

!

--- SENTENCE 293 ---
[](_page_43_Picture_2.jpeg)

Fire coral, Millepora, with yellow on the right that may be a coating of diatoms.

--- SENTENCE 294 ---
This completes the algae proper, including the macroalgae.

--- SENTENCE 295 ---
It is good to keep in mind that all algae compete with corals for space.

--- SENTENCE 296 ---
Macroalgae can overgrow and shade coral, making it hard for coral.

--- SENTENCE 297 ---
Macroalgae also usually have nasty chemicals in them, and some release some of those chemicals.

--- SENTENCE 298 ---
If they are close to corals, those chemicals can damage the coral.

--- SENTENCE 299 ---
Macroalgae can also mechanically damage coral, when waves move them back and forth, they may drag hard parts across the coral surfaces, abrading the tissue.

--- SENTENCE 300 ---
And lastly, it has also been discovered that macroalgae can harbor coral diseases, and if they abrade the coral tissue can pass the disease on to the coral.

--- SENTENCE 301 ---
Large mature corals are best able to defend themselves, but the newly settled coral recruits and juveniles are the most vulnerable to these things.

--- SENTENCE 302 ---
So the first effect of too much macroalgae may be to shut down coral recruitment, and after that to start killing adult corals.

--- SENTENCE 303 ---
In a healthy reef, the competition between algae and coral is not a terrible thing, and corals are very good at defending themselves.

--- SENTENCE 304 ---
But if the reef is not healthy, with too many nutrients, too few herbivores, or sick coral from too high temperatures or too much disease, then the corals can loose the battle and the algae can take over.

#### Cyanobacteria

 Cyanobacteria can photosynthesize like algae, but they are actually not algae, even though people used to call them "blue-green algae."

--- SENTENCE 305 ---
They are actually a kind of bacteria.

--- SENTENCE 306 ---
The cells usually grow in strings, and they also secrete mucus, which helps hold strings of cells to each other.

--- SENTENCE 307 ---
The result is very soft, stringy masses, which are slimy and often fall apart at the touch.

--- SENTENCE 308 ---
Some of them can fix nitrogen that can be used as nutrients as well.

--- SENTENCE 309 ---
They are said to be very sensitive to nutrients, so they can be indicators of nutrient runoff.

--- SENTENCE 310 ---
They are a variety of colors, such as grey, black, yellow, orange, and red.

--- SENTENCE 311 ---
Most we see are stringy, but some make more solid gelatinous blobs.

--- SENTENCE 312 ---
Sometimes they are seen to have oxygen bubbles in the mucus, and often the sway back and forth with the wave surge.

--- SENTENCE 313 ---
On Rose Atoll, a sunken longliner was surrounded by a large area of black cyanobacteria.

--- SENTENCE 314 ---
Apparently the iron stimulated their growth.

--- SENTENCE 315 ---
Iron is a micronutrient, and sea water is usually deficient in it, so adding iron can stimulate the growth of plankton or in this case, cyanobacteria.

--- SENTENCE 316 ---
Now that the longliner has been removed, the cyanobacteria there are decreasing, though some remains.

--- SENTENCE 317 ---
There are also a variety of other wrecks

--- SENTENCE 320 ---

#### Sponges: Porifera

 Most macroinvertebrates in American Samoa seem to be relatively small, and/or rare, or hidden.

--- SENTENCE 349 ---
There are a few that are fairly common, including a couple of sponges.

--- SENTENCE 350 ---
The diversity of sponges seems pretty good, but most are small and uncommon to rare.

--- SENTENCE 351 ---
The most common sponge on reef slopes is an orange lump, which is in genus Stylissa.

--- SENTENCE 352 ---
Sponges are filter feeders which have two sets of holes, small holes to take water in, and larger holes to put it back out.

--- SENTENCE 353 ---
The small holes are usually too small to see.

--- SENTENCE 354 ---
The larger holes usually are irregular in size and placement, and there are often quite a few.

--- SENTENCE 355 ---
Sponges are among the oldest and simplest of all animals, and have a construction quite different from all other organisms.

--- SENTENCE 356 ---
They have no nerves or muscle, so they are incapable of moving.

--- SENTENCE 357 ---
If you touch it, the holes will not contract.

--- SENTENCE 358 ---
These things distinguish it from sea squirts, which will be the next group we look at.

--- SENTENCE 359 ---
Sponge taxonomy is based mainly on the fine details of the little glass spicules embedded in their tissue, as well as the protein fibers that make the body leathery and tough.

--- SENTENCE 360 ---
Sponge taxonomy is even more difficult than coral taxonomy, so often we don't know what the name of a species is.

--- SENTENCE 361 ---
However, when they are alive, many are quite distinctive and not difficult to distinguish.

--- SENTENCE 362 ---
Sponges are usually flexible and somewhat leathery.

--- SENTENCE 363 ---
Because sponges are filter feeders, their abundance and size can be an indicator of plankton abundance, and thus of nutrients.

--- SENTENCE 364 ---
However, their populations may be influenced or even controlled by fish that feed on them and other animals that can feed on them, such as some nudibranchs.

--- SENTENCE 365 ---
Many sponges are full of toxic chemicals, so only a relatively small number of fish and nudibranchs can eat them.

--- SENTENCE 366 ---
Some angelfish can eat sponges, and hawksbill turtles eat sponges.

--- SENTENCE 367 ---
Although sponges are small and uncommon most places here, there is an area with abundant sponges, some of which are large, reported by Paul Brown from deep water (200+ feet) in the harbor.

--- SENTENCE 368 ---
This makes sense because the inner harbor often has green water from phytoplankton, and another bioindicator, small oysters just below the water line, are a good bioindicator of plankton in the harbor since they are abundant in the harbor but rare outside.

--- SENTENCE 369 ---
One group of sponges that have been recommended as a bioindicator species are the boring sponges.

--- SENTENCE 370 ---
These are sponges that bore into reef rock and coral skeletons.

--- SENTENCE 371 ---
Some species are visible as small spots of sponge about 1/8 inch diameter sticking out of holes in reef rock.

--- SENTENCE 372 ---
Some of the spots of sponge are openings that release water.

--- SENTENCE 373 ---
When they bore into reef rock or coral skeletons, they etch out and remove tiny chips of calcium, which then come flying out the water that comes out the sponge opening.

--- SENTENCE 374 ---
In reefs with lots of boring sponges (like in Florida), the reefs can be blanketed with a dusting of very fine white calcium dust which is these chips.

--- SENTENCE 375 ---
There is one species that is well known from the Great Barrier Reef that is coal black and produces irregular smooth patches on

reef rock.

--- SENTENCE 376 ---
We have black patches among the coralline algae, but if you touch them they are rock hard, not the leathery feel of a sponge, because they are algae not sponge.

--- SENTENCE 377 ---
I have never ever seen a single boring sponge here.

--- SENTENCE 378 ---
However, they may be hiding in the skeletons of coral, especially dead coral.

--- SENTENCE 379 ---
I plan to search for them.

--- SENTENCE 380 ---
But the low number of visible ones indicates low levels of plankton and thus nutrients in the water.

--- SENTENCE 381 ---
There is also no sign of the dusting of white chips from boring sponges.

--- SENTENCE 382 ---
The bright orange sponge Stylissa massa is probably our most common sponge.

--- SENTENCE 383 ---
It lives on reef slopes.

#### Sea squirts

 Sea squirts can also be called tunicates or ascidians.

--- SENTENCE 523 ---
Unlike sponges, they are complex animals with a nervous system, muscles, a heart and circulatory system, etc.

--- SENTENCE 524 ---
Some are solitary individuals, and some form colonies of individuals.

--- SENTENCE 525 ---
Solitary species have exactly two holes in an individual, one on the top and one on the side.

--- SENTENCE 526 ---
Water goes in one opening and out the other, and inside there is a filter basket that filters out food, so they are filter feeders like sponges.

--- SENTENCE 527 ---
If you touch them, the holes quickly close, which is very different from sponges.

--- SENTENCE 528 ---
Their shapes are not irregular, and the hole sizes are not irregular.

--- SENTENCE 529 ---
In colonial species there are usually two sizes of openings, large openings where water comes out, and small openings where water goes in.

--- SENTENCE 530 ---
There are many small openings for each large opening.

--- SENTENCE 531 ---
Each individual in the colony has its own small opening, but a group of them share the large opening.

--- SENTENCE 532 ---
The sizes of the holes are uniform, and often they are arranged spatially in a very regular pattern.

--- SENTENCE 533 ---
Colonial species have a larva that looks like a tadpole.

--- SENTENCE 534 ---
The tail has a nerve cord and a notochord (which elasmobranches have, and other vertebrates like us have briefly early in our development).

--- SENTENCE 535 ---
The head end has a mouth, a filter basket, and gill slits.

--- SENTENCE 536 ---
So they are thought to have a common ancestor with vertebrates, and are placed with vertebrates in Phylum Chordata.

--- SENTENCE 537 ---
The common ancestor was a very very long time ago (perhaps 500 million years or more).

--- SENTENCE 538 ---
We have a black species which is solitary, but I think I've only seen it in the harbor.

--- SENTENCE 539 ---
Because sea squirts are filter feeders, they can indicate plankton and thus nutrients.

--- SENTENCE 540 ---
Like sponges, they are often filled with lots of toxic substances and thus only a few things can eat them.

--- SENTENCE 541 ---
Some species have symbiotic algae and so don't need plankton and so their abundance may not indicate plankton.

--- SENTENCE 542 ---
Those species are likely to be thin and encrusting and found only in the light.

--- SENTENCE 543 ---
Diplosoma similis may be such a species.

#### Snails (gastropods)

--- SENTENCE 619 ---
Snails usually have coiled shells.

--- SENTENCE 620 ---
Here is a picture of a cone shell.

--- SENTENCE 621 ---
Cone shells have cone-shaped shells.

--- SENTENCE 622 ---
They have modified the normal snail tongue, which is a ribbon with teeth on it.

--- SENTENCE 623 ---
They enlarged a few teeth and coiled them, making tubes with sharp points, which are used as harpoons and syringe needles, to impale their prey and inject venom into them.

--- SENTENCE 624 ---
Most cone shells eat worms, a few eat other molluscs and a few eat fish.

--- SENTENCE 625 ---
Snails are slow-moving, so they have a major problem with fish.

--- SENTENCE 626 ---
That is, if they stick a fish with their harpoon-tooth, the fish will dart off, and they will never find it and never get to eat it.

--- SENTENCE 627 ---
So, they have to stop the fish before it can escape.

--- SENTENCE 628 ---
They do that with super-fast acting, super-powerful chemicals in their venom.

--- SENTENCE 629 ---
The chemicals are proteins, and they are super specific to nerve cells.

--- SENTENCE 630 ---
A few of them can kill humans, so unless you know which ones are dangerous, it is best never to pick one up.

--- SENTENCE 631 ---
Unless you see it is occupied by a hermit crab, then it is safe.

--- SENTENCE 632 ---
There are about 600 species of cone shells, all in one genus, Conus, the largest single genus of animals.

 There is one very unusual kind of snail that is common in corals here.

--- SENTENCE 660 ---
These are snails that do not coil their shell, but live in the shell inside of corals, with the shell opening at the surface of the coral.

--- SENTENCE 661 ---

--- SENTENCE 662 ---

--- SENTENCE 667 ---

--- SENTENCE 668 ---
They are fairly common in Porites some places, but are also in other corals.

#### Palolo worms (Palolo viridis)

--- SENTENCE 697 ---
Palolo worms are famous worms that have a mass spawning once a year and are avidly caught and eaten in the Samoas.

--- SENTENCE 698 ---
They live in holes in the reef and are not seen except during spawning.

--- SENTENCE 699 ---
During the year, they grow extra segments on their tail end, which are called the "epitoke".

--- SENTENCE 700 ---
The extra segments are filled with either eggs or sperm.

--- SENTENCE 701 ---
Each segment has a pair of paddles so it can swim.

--- SENTENCE 702 ---
It also has an eye so it can swim upwards towards light.

--- SENTENCE 703 ---
They spawn each year in October or November.

--- SENTENCE 704 ---
They spawn seven days after the full moon.

--- SENTENCE 705 ---
If that date is close to the end of October or the beginning of November, they will all spawn on one night.

--- SENTENCE 706 ---
If that day is in the middle of October and November, spawning will be split between those two nights.

--- SENTENCE 707 ---
They spawn at about 1 am on Tutuila, and at other times on other islands.

--- SENTENCE 708 ---
When they spawn, the worm backs up enough that the reproductive section that is their tail section is out of the burrow.

--- SENTENCE 709 ---
The tail end breaks off and swims upward and the rest of the worm goes back down the hole.

--- SENTENCE 710 ---
There are separate sexes, one is tan and the other light blue.

--- SENTENCE 711 ---
They swim in circles and then all of a sudden one of them explodes, releasing all their eggs or sperm and that sets the others off and they all explode.

--- SENTENCE 712 ---
People try to catch them before they explode.

--- SENTENCE 713 ---
They can be eaten raw or cooked, often with scrambled eggs.

--- SENTENCE 714 ---
They are a delicacy.

--- SENTENCE 715 ---
The spawning timing depends on several biological clocks, one is the time of year, another the time in the lunar cycle, and the third is the day-night cycle.

--- SENTENCE 716 ---
The spawning is also well known in Tonga, Fiji, Vanuatu, and Indonesia, but most famous in the Samoas.

#### Crustaceans

 Crustaceans are arthropods, like insects, spiders and scorpions.

--- SENTENCE 720 ---
Crustaceans like other arthropods have a shell on the outside which is made of chitin, and in crustaceans can be strengthened with calcium on the inside.

--- SENTENCE 721 ---
In order to grow, they must grow a new shell inside the old, and while the new shell is still soft and flexible, crawl out of the old shell and hide until the new shell hardens.

--- SENTENCE 722 ---
Crustaceans living on coral reefs include lobsters, crabs, shrimp, and barnacles.

--- SENTENCE 723 ---

 Feather stars have a small central disc with many arms with very regular small side branches, much like a feather.

--- SENTENCE 774 ---
As filter feeders they could be indicators of plankton and thus nutrients.

--- SENTENCE 775 ---

Indicators of plankton and thus nutrients:

Filter feeders which do not have symbiotic algae

Sponges (uncommon and small), especially boring sponges (not seen)

--- SENTENCE 821 ---
Sea squirts (uncommon and small)

Clams-

--- SENTENCE 822 ---

--- SENTENCE 824 ---
Basket stars (the author has not yet seen them here, but they are nocturnal)

Brightly colored seafans (rare)

Black corals (rare) Brightly colored soft corals (rare)

All of these are uncommon or rare here, except the oysters at the water line in the harbor.

--- SENTENCE 825 ---
Bioerosion is when living organisms erode away the calcium of the reef and/or corals.

--- SENTENCE 826 ---
If bioerosion is greater than the amount of calcium deposited by corals and algae, the reef can erode instead of build.

--- SENTENCE 827 ---
Bioerosion in natural, but if there is too much that can be a problem.

--- SENTENCE 828 ---
Some of the species that are filter feeders are also bioeroders.

--- SENTENCE 829 ---
There are two functional groups that can be distinguished, surface bioeroders and subsurface.

--- SENTENCE 830 ---
Boring sponges and urchins are the only two that have been reported to be problems in other places.

#### Water Clarity:

--- SENTENCE 832 ---
Water clarity is easily measured by visibility distance.

--- SENTENCE 833 ---
Water clarity is a pretty good indicator-

--- SENTENCE 834 ---
the clearer the water the better the conditions for reefs and the murkier the water the worse.

--- SENTENCE 835 ---
Sediment in water coming out of streams during and right after heavy rainfalls is very easy to see, and the darkness of the brown water and the extent of the area are clues to how much of a problem there is.

--- SENTENCE 836 ---
So silt or sediment directly affect visibility and the brown color is a good indicator of the cause, sediment runoff from land.

--- SENTENCE 837 ---
Nutrients can't be seen in water, they don't affect the visibility.

--- SENTENCE 838 ---
The main nutrients are nitrogen in the form of nitrates, and phosphorus in the form of phosphates, plus perhaps iron as a trace element.

--- SENTENCE 839 ---
If water has low visibility and the water is green, the cause is phytoplankton, and phytoplankton grow when nutrients are high enough.

--- SENTENCE 840 ---
They need both nitrates and phosphates, so it is possible to have a lot of one and none of the other and not have a plankton bloom.

--- SENTENCE 841 ---
Green water with low visibility is a very strong indicator of a plankton bloom which in turn is a strong indicator of high levels of nutrients in the water.

--- SENTENCE 842 ---
Green water with low visibility is virtually always found only near shore and almost always only in bays or places where the water exchange is limited.

--- SENTENCE 843 ---
In bays, the water doesn't get flushed, so nutrients that wash into the bay build up.

--- SENTENCE 844 ---
So there are two causes to green water in a bay, one is nutrient runoff from land, and the other is lack of flushing.

--- SENTENCE 845 ---
Lack of flushing is natural, it comes with the bay shape, and there isn't anything we can do about it.

--- SENTENCE 846 ---
Nutrient runoff comes from people (as well as smaller amounts that are natural).

--- SENTENCE 847 ---
But the same amount of nutrient runoff along an open coast will not produce a plankton bloom while it will in a bay with low flushing.

--- SENTENCE 848 ---
Bays are just more sensitive because of the lack of flushing.

#### Fishing:

--- SENTENCE 849 ---
Giant clams, octopus, and some sea cucumbers could be indicators of fishing pressure, but it is hard to know what the natural populations would be without fishing.

--- SENTENCE 850 ---
Octopus are very fast-growing and so populations can recover fast.

--- SENTENCE 851 ---
In places with commercial sea cucumber fisheries that export to Asia, sea cucumbers have often been quickly fished out and may not recover.

--- SENTENCE 852 ---
The relationship between boring clams and plankton has been documented, there is a positive correlation with the abundance of clams higher where plankton is more abundant.

--- SENTENCE 853 ---
There is even a way to monitor clams and other things that live on corals, as a way to measure that.

--- SENTENCE 854 ---
Keep in mind that no indicator is perfect, all indicator species can be influenced by things other than what they are said to indicate.

--- SENTENCE 855 ---
For instance, sponges might be uncommon here because the Pacific has lots of fish that eat them.

--- SENTENCE 856 ---
Mind you, a place like the Philippines or Indonesia has even more species that can eat sponges, and they have lots more than we have, some of which are large.

--- SENTENCE 857 ---
But they are archipelagos with lots of high islands, many larger islands, all with nutrient runoff.

--- SENTENCE 858 ---
It could be that macroinvertebrates are uncommon on Pacific Islands because there isn't enough plankton for their larvae to eat.

--- SENTENCE 859 ---
That itself would be an indicator.

--- SENTENCE 860 ---
But that wouldn't explain why most of the macroinvertebrates are small.

--- SENTENCE 861 ---
Plus, fish have larvae that eat plankton too, and we have lots of them.

### Cnidaria

 The Cnidaria includes not only the hard corals, but several related groups, some of which look a bit like hard corals.

--- SENTENCE 862 ---
We'll look at the different related groups before starting on the hard corals themselves.

#### Black Corals

 Black corals are rare here within safe diving depths.

--- SENTENCE 863 ---
The only ones I have seen are the whip-like forms, but there are probably more bushy forms in deeper water somewhere.

--- SENTENCE 864 ---
They form a single whip growing up from the bottom, in relatively low light, so down deeper on the slope.

--- SENTENCE 865 ---
They are often green, though can be lighter colors, or they may just look lighter in photos.

--- SENTENCE 866 ---
The whip has a central stiffening rod, which is surrounded by tissue, and the polyps are embedded in the tissue.

--- SENTENCE 867 ---
Small tentacles extend from the polyps embedded in the tissue.

--- SENTENCE 868 ---
Each polyp has exactly 6 tentacles.

--- SENTENCE 869 ---
The hard skeleton at the base of some bushy species are the ones carved into jewelry some places.

--- SENTENCE 870 ---
The scientific name for the group, Antipatharia, comes from an ancient belief that it could cure disease, but there is no evidence for that.

--- SENTENCE 871 ---
There are two genera of the whip like species, one has tentacles on only one side, the other on both sides.

--- SENTENCE 872 ---
The only similar thing here is the one gorgonian that forms branching colonies that are thicker rods, and has complete polyps extending, so each polyp has a column with a ring of tentacles at the top.

--- SENTENCE 873 ---
Black corals have no zooxanthellae, they are filter feeders and so could be an indicator of plankton and thus nutrients.

!

--- SENTENCE 874 ---
Some small gobies specialize in living on sea whips, and lay their eggs on them as well.

#### Zoanthids

 Zoanthids form colonies of small polyps which might look a bit like corals, but they have no hard skeleton.

--- SENTENCE 875 ---
Polyps are anywhere from maybe 1/8 inch up to about ½ inch in diameter, and have tentacles around the outer edge but not in the center of the polyp.

--- SENTENCE 876 ---
The polyps are all connected to each other in a colony, but you can't always see the connections.

--- SENTENCE 877 ---
There are three genera here which are easy to tell apart, but the species within those genera are either not worked out or not possible for us to distinguish.

--- SENTENCE 878 ---
They may seem similar to corallimorphs, but corallimorphs have larger polyps, 3 inches or larger in diameter, and their polyps have tentacles all over the oral disc of the polyp.

--- SENTENCE 879 ---
The zoanthids we see all have zooxanthellae, though some kinds live on the surfaces of sponges elsewhere and don't have zooxanthellae.

--- SENTENCE 880 ---
A fairly common zoanthid on reef flats is Palythoa.

--- SENTENCE 881 ---
Palythoa forms a thick cushion, with the polyps embedded in it, and not projecting.

--- SENTENCE 882 ---
So you see a thick cushion that is a yellow, brown, or cream color, and which has lots of rings on it.

--- SENTENCE 883 ---
Each ring is a ring of tentacles.

--- SENTENCE 884 ---
So the connection between polyps which is the cushion they are embedded in is obvious.

--- SENTENCE 885 ---
It is also on slopes but much rarer there.

--- SENTENCE 886 ---
It is highly toxic, though touching it shouldn't produce any problems, just don't eat it.

!

This green zoanthid forms green carpets over dead hard surfaces.

--- SENTENCE 889 ---
Sometimes it can be a rusty color.

--- SENTENCE 890 ---
Like any Cnidarian, they will retract when touched.

The polyps are small, around 1/8-1/4 inch diameter.

#### Corallimorphs

 Corallimorphs are polyps that are usually found in clusters.

--- SENTENCE 899 ---
They are actually separate polyps, though they are often close enough together that you can't tell whether they are connected like zoanthids, or not.

--- SENTENCE 900 ---
Their polyps are larger than zoanthids, with the smallest species having polyps about 2-3 inches diameter, and the largest species having polyps up to about 1 foot in diameter.

--- SENTENCE 901 ---
Corallimorph polyps have tentacles all over the oral disc (top) of their polyps.

--- SENTENCE 902 ---
The most common corallimorph in shallow water and on reef slopes is Rhodactis (matu-malu).

--- SENTENCE 903 ---
It has light brown polyps about 2-3 inches in diameter, and commonly covers dead rock, to which it is very tightly stuck.

--- SENTENCE 904 ---
It can, however, either sting and kill some corals, or grow right over and attach to living corals.

--- SENTENCE 905 ---
Some corals can sting it.

--- SENTENCE 906 ---
It can make small patches a few feet in diameter or much larger patches.

--- SENTENCE 907 ---
It is traditionally eaten, and has to be boiled first or it is toxic.

--- SENTENCE 908 ---
Commonly the rock it is on is removed with it and the rock with them on it boiled together.

--- SENTENCE 909 ---
In Palmyra, a sunken longliner has had a patch of it grow around it and expand to the point it now covers about one million square meters.

--- SENTENCE 910 ---
So it would appear that iron may stimulate it.

--- SENTENCE 911 ---
There is a fairly big patch in Tafeu bay on the north side, even though there are no people in that watershed.

--- SENTENCE 912 ---
Our basalt rock has some natural iron- some parts of it are orange from the iron, and in some places cracks in the rock have streaks of orange going down from them, where water containing iron drains.

--- SENTENCE 913 ---
So there is some natural iron going into the water.

--- SENTENCE 914 ---
The corallimorphs we see all have zooxanthellae, though in some places some solitary species come out at night and don't have zooxanthellae (they look like anemones).

!

There is also one small species of anemone which is not in many ID books (except the book on American Samoa, where it is misidentified as a corallimorph).

--- SENTENCE 936 ---
This species occurs in groups, and can be called a colonial anemone, though it is really in clonal groups of genetically identical separate individuals produced by fission, not a colony of connected polyps as in a coral.

--- SENTENCE 937 ---
Anyhow, this species, Gyractis sesere, is green and lives on the dead bases of branching corals, usually staghorns.

--- SENTENCE 938 ---

--- SENTENCE 939 ---

--- SENTENCE 940 ---

--- SENTENCE 941 ---
The polyps are small, less than 2 inches diameter (4 cm).

--- SENTENCE 942 ---
This species is widespread in the Indo-Pacific, and is a pest in saltwater aquaria.

--- SENTENCE 943 ---
It appears to have been described and named several times.

--- SENTENCE 944 ---

That will be a lot of work and a lengthy process.

--- SENTENCE 946 ---
The aquarium trade uses another name, genus Anemonia, but it is not in that genus.

### Octocorals: Gorgonians

 Gorgonians have a stiffening rod that is surrounded by tissue, and polyps extend from that tissue.

--- SENTENCE 971 ---
If the polyps are extended, you can see each has a column and a ring of tentacles at the end.

--- SENTENCE 972 ---
Each polyp always has exactly 8 tentacles, and each tentacle has side branches, but those are usually too small to see underwater.

--- SENTENCE 973 ---
The stiffening rods make it possible for gorgonians to often have the shape of whips, bushes, or sea fans.

--- SENTENCE 974 ---
They are uncommon here.

--- SENTENCE 975 ---
When the polyps are retracted the branches have a smooth surface, otherwise they are fuzzy with polyps.

--- SENTENCE 976 ---
Species with zooxanthellae are out in the sun and dull colored while species without zooxanthellae are in lower light and can be quite brightly colored.

--- SENTENCE 977 ---
Their tissue contains tiny granules or spines called sclerites which are made of calcium, and it is the shape of these that the taxonomy is based on.

--- SENTENCE 978 ---
Gorgonians and soft corals are two different shapes that used to be considered two different things, but there are some forms that have been discovered that are intermediates between them, so while they are two easily distinguishable shapes underwater and thus useful terms, they are no longer taxonomic terms.

--- SENTENCE 979 ---
Rumphella forms bushy colonies up to 2-3 feet tall.

--- SENTENCE 980 ---
They are light brown, and branches are about ¼-1/2 inch thick.

--- SENTENCE 981 ---
They are on the reef slope and are pretty rare here.

--- SENTENCE 982 ---
They commonly sway back and forth in the current.

--- SENTENCE 983 ---
When the polyps are retracted the branches are smooth.

#### Soft corals

 Soft corals form soft fleshy colonies usually attached to hard substrate, but which may have ridges, fingers, or branches extending from them.

--- SENTENCE 1009 ---
The colonies have polyps embedded in them that can extend or contract.

--- SENTENCE 1010 ---
The polyps have exactly eight tentacles, so they are placed in the Octocorals along with the gorgonians.

--- SENTENCE 1011 ---
The colonies do not make a rigid skeleton like hard corals, but they do produce calcium "sclerites" or little granules or spines in their tissue.

--- SENTENCE 1012 ---
In some colonies these granules or spines can be seen in the nearly clear tissue, and may stick out so they can also be felt as sharp points.

--- SENTENCE 1013 ---
In some species, especially in the genus Sinularia, the sclerites may be much more numerous near the base of the colony and be deposited underneath the soft coral, which the soft coral cements together as a hard round smooth base that it is attached to.

--- SENTENCE 1014 ---
A few make so many sclerites that the base becomes quite tall and may even be branching, reaching as much as 6 feet tall or more.

--- SENTENCE 1015 ---
This material is called spiculite and is quite hard, it serves much like the skeleton of a hard coral.

--- SENTENCE 1016 ---
There used to be a lot more soft corals in Pago Pago harbor than now, and one portion of the reef in the harbor is even made of this material that they left behind.

--- SENTENCE 1017 ---
The taxonomy is primarily based on the microscopic details of the sclerites, and while some identification to genera of the live animals is possible, it is rarely possible to identify them to species.

--- SENTENCE 1018 ---
Cladiella is a common soft coral on the outer reef slopes.

--- SENTENCE 1019 ---
It forms small colonies usually just a few inches in diameter and maybe an inch or two tall, which are made of stubby fingers.

--- SENTENCE 1020 ---
Colonies are usually brown, but if you touch them they quickly turn white.

--- SENTENCE 1021 ---
This is caused by the brown polyps pulling into the white tissue of the finger.

--- SENTENCE 1022 ---
Other kinds of soft coral do not change color so dramatically when you touch them.

--- SENTENCE 1023 ---
They have zooxanthellae.

#### Hard Corals

 Hard corals form hard skeletons, and usually the tissue is only a very thin layer on the outside of the hard skeleton.

--- SENTENCE 1060 ---
There are three major groups, blue coral, fire corals, and Scleractinia.

#### Blue Coral

 Blue coral is actually in the same group (Octocorals) as the soft corals and gorgonians, as it has exactly 8 tentacles.

--- SENTENCE 1061 ---
The tissue deposits some iron salts in the skeleton which turn it a dark blue.

--- SENTENCE 1062 ---
Living colonies are often a blue-grey, and if the polyps are extended a white fuzz may be visible.

--- SENTENCE 1063 ---
I have never seen it around Tutuila, but it is fairly common in the pools on Ofu, and is uncommon on the slopes of Ofu and Olosega.

--- SENTENCE 1064 ---
Olosega is the eastern most limit of its geographic range which extends west to the Red Sea.

--- SENTENCE 1065 ---
It has zooxanthellae.

!

--- SENTENCE 1066 ---

#### Fire Coral, Millepora

 Fire coral is actually in a separate class called "hydrozoa" which alternate between polyps and medusae (jellyfish).

--- SENTENCE 1067 ---
For fire corals, the polyps are what build the hard skeleton.

--- SENTENCE 1068 ---
The polyps are tiny.

--- SENTENCE 1069 ---
The word "Millepora" means thousands of pores, which refers to the tiny holes in the skeleton which the polyps sit in (one polyp in each tiny hole).

--- SENTENCE 1070 ---
If a fire coral is backlit (so sun behind it) sometimes you can see tiny white hairs extending from the coral.

--- SENTENCE 1071 ---
Those are the polyps.

--- SENTENCE 1072 ---
There are actually two kinds, one kind is short and fatter and has a mouth, and the other kind is longer and thinner and has no mouth but has more stingers.

--- SENTENCE 1073 ---
Fire corals are called that because they can produce a fiery sting on tender skin.

--- SENTENCE 1074 ---
You can touch them with the tips of your fingers where the skin is thick and feel nothing.

--- SENTENCE 1075 ---
Their stings are not dangerous and generally don't even produce welts or a rash, but they do get your attention.

--- SENTENCE 1076 ---
Millepora colonies have zooxanthellae.

--- SENTENCE 1077 ---
We have four species, at least one of which is fairly common.

--- SENTENCE 1078 ---
The most common fire coral in American Samoa produces an encrusting brown or yellow-brown base, from which paddles grow upward.

--- SENTENCE 1079 ---
The paddles can be a variety of shapes and sizes, usually similar on one colony but differing between colonies.

--- SENTENCE 1080 ---
They are fairly thick, up to about ½ inch thick.

--- SENTENCE 1081 ---
In some cases they are long enough to be curtainlike.

--- SENTENCE 1082 ---
The encrusting base is usually bumpy and the paddle sides may be as well.

--- SENTENCE 1083 ---
It can be common on outer reef flats and moderately common to uncommon on reef slopes.

#### Scleractinian Corals

 These are the corals that most people think of as "corals."

--- SENTENCE 1134 ---
They have six tentacles or multiples of six tentacles, so they are in the Hexacoral group, along with black corals, corallimorphs, zoanthids, and anemonies.

--- SENTENCE 1135 ---
Those that build reefs have zooxanthellae, and they form a hard, solid, calcium skeleton underneath the living tissue.

--- SENTENCE 1136 ---
There are about 831 species currently known around the world, and we have at least 250 species, perhaps as many as 400.

--- SENTENCE 1137 ---
Scleractinian corals have polyps, and most reef building species are colonies of many polyps.

--- SENTENCE 1138 ---
A few are single polyps, and referred to as "solitary" corals while most have many polyps and are referred to as "colonial."

--- SENTENCE 1139 ---
Actually, about half of the species in the world do not have zooxanthellae, and most of those live in the dark and only a few build larger structures you might call reefs.

--- SENTENCE 1140 ---
Polyps vary from less than 1/16th inch (1 mm) in diameter to about 30 cm in diameter.

--- SENTENCE 1141 ---
Mushroom corals are single polyps and some species can reach about 30 cm in diameter, they are the largest coral polyps.

--- SENTENCE 1142 ---
Species of scleractinia are usually not easy to distinguish but in recent years books have been published making it possible to distinguish many of them.

--- SENTENCE 1143 ---
Genera are easier to distinguish, although a few genera are hard to distinguish.

--- SENTENCE 1144 ---
Coral taxonomy is almost exclusively based on the skeleton, both the overall shape and the details under the microscope, and everything in between.

--- SENTENCE 1145 ---
But for identification, we can use anything that is helpful, including color.

--- SENTENCE 1146 ---
Color is often highly variable, but sometimes it is consistent and helpful.

--- SENTENCE 1147 ---
A polyp is a soft tissue structure with a mouth surrounded by tentacles and a hollow body filled with water.

--- SENTENCE 1148 ---
The body wall is very simple, with just 3 layers of cells.

--- SENTENCE 1149 ---
The body wall extends into the central cavity in the form of curtains called "mesenteries."

--- SENTENCE 1150 ---
Coral polyps sit in "polyp cups" called "corallites."

--- SENTENCE 1151 ---
Actually, the whole polyp cup structure is called a "corallite" but if you are talking about the inside that is called a "calice" (rhymes with "Alice.")

--- SENTENCE 1152 ---
A corallite has ridges extending into the central space, and those ridges are called "septa."

--- SENTENCE 1153 ---
Septa often continue on the outside of the corallite as "costae."

--- SENTENCE 1154 ---
The details of the shape of the corallites, the spaces between corallites, the septa, the costae, the granules and spines on the septa and costae, the number and sizes of septa and costae, and any spines between corallites are all taxonomic features along with colony shapes, used to distinguish species.

--- SENTENCE 1155 ---
In live corals, you can't see the skeleton directly, but because the tissue is usually thin, you can see some of the shapes of the corallites through the tissue.

--- SENTENCE 1156 ---
Those are all cues that can help in identifying live corals.

--- SENTENCE 1157 ---
I will provide identifications for some of the most common corals on our reefs.

--- SENTENCE 1158 ---
I'm working on a coral field guidebook that will provide identification for many more.

--- SENTENCE 1159 ---
But it will be most helpful to you, I believe, to begin with some of the most common.

--- SENTENCE 1160 ---
#### Lifeforms

 Lifeforms are colony shapes that are easier than genera and species to learn and often easy to identify.

--- SENTENCE 1161 ---
The following is the basic system.

--- SENTENCE 1162 ---
Massive- rounded, solid, usually a hemisphere, any size or weight (not the usual meaning of massive meaning heavy or large).

Branching- has branches

Encrusting- a thin layer on the hard substrate, almost like paint

Foliose- flattened or leaf-like.

--- SENTENCE 1163 ---
Some are near-horizontal plates, others form whorls, some form vertical plates or blades.

--- SENTENCE 1164 ---
Submassive: may look massive, but actually has polyps separated by deep cracks and the polyps are actually out at the ends of branches that are close together.

--- SENTENCE 1165 ---
Mushroom: looks like an upsidedown mushroom cap, round with radiating ridges that look like mushroom gills.

--- SENTENCE 1166 ---
the largest genus of corals, which dominates many reefs, and which are almost always branching.

--- SENTENCE 1167 ---
They have one polyp at the end of each branch, and different polyps on the sides of branches, unlike other corals.

--- SENTENCE 1168 ---
Acropora have some of their own lifeforms.

--- SENTENCE 1169 ---
Acropora branching

Acropora staghorn- branches that look rather like deer antlers.

--- SENTENCE 1170 ---
It is a type of Acropora branching, but there are many other branching shapes that are much more bushy.

--- SENTENCE 1171 ---
the colony grows in the form of a large flat surface, held up off the substrate by a single column or pedestal.

--- SENTENCE 1172 ---
Some have the pedestal in the center, others off to the side.

--- SENTENCE 1173 ---
Acropora digitate- colonies that have short branches which do not themselves branch (very much).

--- SENTENCE 1174 ---
Lifeforms are very easy to learn.

--- SENTENCE 1175 ---
The down side is that often you can see a colony that seems intermediate between two lifeforms.

--- SENTENCE 1176 ---
Also, lifeforms do not correspond to species.

--- SENTENCE 1177 ---
There are many species in each lifeform, and some species can have more than one lifeform.

--- SENTENCE 1178 ---
Lifeforms just help you get started identifying a species of coral.

--- SENTENCE 1179 ---
The other thing is that lifeforms may be functional groups to some extent.

--- SENTENCE 1180 ---
So for instance, branching corals are more easily broken than massive corals so they are more subject to hurricanes, but they also tend to grow faster, and corals in deep water may be foliose to gather more light, and lifeforms on the reef crest may be sturdy to withstand waves, and branching corals may bleach more easily than massive, and so on.

--- SENTENCE 1181 ---
So lifeforms can actually be very useful ecological information.

#### Common corals: back reef pools

Staghorns are species of Acropora that are shaped like deer antlers.

--- SENTENCE 1201 ---
Because they are Acropora, they have one polyp or corallite on the end of every branch.

 Acropora muricata is by far the most common staghorn in the backreef pools.

--- SENTENCE 1202 ---
It is tan to brown, with branches about a half inch thick, and it has a purple tip on the end of every branch.

--- SENTENCE 1203 ---

Other places that is not true.

--- SENTENCE 1205 ---

--- SENTENCE 1206 ---
It dominates the pools, along with finger coral, but it is not on the reef slopes at all.

#### Acropora hyacinthus

 Acropora hyacinthus is a table coral.

--- SENTENCE 1298 ---

--- SENTENCE 1299 ---
It is most common on upper reef slopes and can dominate some areas like Leone and the middle area of Fagatele Bay at about 10-15 feet deep.

--- SENTENCE 1300 ---
It is usually a brown or reddish brown, but some colonies have a slight greenish tint.

--- SENTENCE 1301 ---

# DAMAGE

 There are quite a variety of things that can damage coral reefs.

--- SENTENCE 1333 ---
Some of these, like predation and disease, can be quite hard to tell apart.

--- SENTENCE 1334 ---
Further, we don't know all of the different kinds of damage yet, so for instance we don't know which fish species are making some of the bite marks on corals, and we don't know the causes of most coral

diseases and we discover coral diseases we have not seen before fairly frequently.

--- SENTENCE 1335 ---
Four major kinds of damage we will cover here are mechanical damage, bleaching, predation, and disease.

#### Mechanical Damage

#### Breakage

 A variety of things can break coral, such as hurricanes, tsunamis, people, turtles, and fish.

--- SENTENCE 1336 ---
Newly broken corals usually have broken surfaces which are very white.

--- SENTENCE 1337 ---
The white is the bare coral skeleton.

!

--- SENTENCE 1338 ---
[](_page_244_Picture_4.jpeg)

Coral (Porites cylindrica) broken by a hurricane.

--- SENTENCE 1339 ---
The tips of branches are light colored but rounded, while the broken surfaces are flat and very white.

--- SENTENCE 1340 ---
The white broken surface actually has live tissue in tiny pores all through the skeleton.

--- SENTENCE 1341 ---
In several weeks to a few months these white broken surfaces will heal over and grow new rounded tips and resume healthy growth.

--- SENTENCE 1342 ---
Larger branches will have live tissue in pores in the outer part of the broken surface, but not on the interior of the branch, and typically you can see the different color in the center of the broken surface.

--- SENTENCE 1343 ---
The outer part with tissue will heal quickly, but the center part will grow algae.

--- SENTENCE 1344 ---
The live surface may then slowly grow over the dead area, if it isn't too large

!

--- SENTENCE 1345 ---
[](_page_245_Picture_0.jpeg)

Broken branches healing after the tsunami.

--- SENTENCE 1346 ---
Notice the branch at the top with algae (dark) growing in the center.

--- SENTENCE 1347 ---
Sea turtles break coral as they dig in finger coral or staghorns, apparently to find sponges to eat.

--- SENTENCE 1348 ---
Sponges may grow down on the dead bases of branches.

--- SENTENCE 1349 ---
Sponges are the favorite thing for Hawksbill turtles to eat, and most of our turtles are Hawksbills.

--- SENTENCE 1350 ---
They typically make a hole where they have broken the branches, and scatter the broken branches around the hole.

--- SENTENCE 1351 ---
Often I see turtles with their head in a hole in staghorns, hard at work.

!

--- SENTENCE 1352 ---

#### Low Tides

 Low tides kill coral on reef flats whenever there is an unusually low tide.

--- SENTENCE 1359 ---
The corals that stick up too far get exposed to air and the hot sun for too long and die.

--- SENTENCE 1360 ---
This is natural and is like mowing your grass.

--- SENTENCE 1361 ---
Since only the highest corals are killed and the lower corals continue to grow, deeper spots can get filled in while the high spots get mowed down.

--- SENTENCE 1362 ---
This results in the reef flat being very flat at about the low tide level.

!

--- SENTENCE 1363 ---
[](_page_249_Picture_2.jpeg)

Coral killed by low tide.

--- SENTENCE 1364 ---
You can see almost exactly how far down the water went (the water probably went a little lower, but the line is where the exposure was too much for the coral to survive).

--- SENTENCE 1365 ---
The dead coral was quickly colonized by turf or filamentous algae.

!

--- SENTENCE 1366 ---
[](_page_250_Picture_0.jpeg)

A coral a few days after one of the lowet tides.

--- SENTENCE 1367 ---
The yellow at top is new filamentous algae.

--- SENTENCE 1368 ---
The white below is stressed coral which survived but has not yet recovered.

--- SENTENCE 1369 ---
It has lost zooxanthellae.

--- SENTENCE 1370 ---
This can be called "bleaching" even though it was not caused by high temperatures, but rather by exposure to air.

--- SENTENCE 1371 ---
Bleaching is a response to a variety of stressors, including high temperatures, low temperatures, low salinity, and exposure to air.

--- SENTENCE 1372 ---
Coral that is killed by low tides is initially white and hard to tell from bleaching.

--- SENTENCE 1373 ---
Within a day or two it starts to get a very light yellow color, maybe from diatoms (a kind of single celled algae).

--- SENTENCE 1374 ---
If it is bright sun and you swish your hands past them and they are dead, the water motion will knock oxygen bubbles off of the algae and you will see a bunch of oxygen bubbles rise.

--- SENTENCE 1375 ---
Within 3-4 days of being killed the coral will be green with turf, and within a week or so they will be very dark green-brown or even black with dense turf.

!

### BLEACHING

 Corals expel their zooxanthellae single-cell algae if the water gets too hot for too long.

--- SENTENCE 1380 ---
Once the algae have been expelled, the tissue is clear, because all the color was in the zooxanthellae.

--- SENTENCE 1381 ---
Then you can see right through the thin clear tissue to the skeleton, which is white, and which reflects the bright light.

--- SENTENCE 1382 ---
So coral that have been too hot turn white.

--- SENTENCE 1383 ---
At that point they are still alive.

--- SENTENCE 1384 ---
If the temperature was just 1-2 degrees C above the normal summer maximum, the coral may survive and get its zooxanthellae and color back.

--- SENTENCE 1385 ---
If it is more than 2 degrees C above, then the coral may die.

--- SENTENCE 1386 ---
If temperatures are high, the coral can completely bleach and everything turns white.

!

--- SENTENCE 1387 ---
[](_page_253_Picture_2.jpeg)

Bleached staghorn at Amouli.

.

--- SENTENCE 1388 ---
A few corals have pigments in the coral cells, so that when they bleach they turn colors which are usually described as being neon colors that look a bit unreal.

--- SENTENCE 1389 ---
Typical colors are purple, pink, and sometimes yellow.

!

--- SENTENCE 1390 ---
[](_page_254_Picture_1.jpeg)

Bleached corals with neon colors.

--- SENTENCE 1391 ---
Notice the gradual fading from healthy brown to bleached white.

--- SENTENCE 1392 ---
Some years we have small amounts of bleaching on the reef slopes, with scattered corals that bleach.

--- SENTENCE 1393 ---
Some kinds of corals like Pocillopora and Montastrea curta may bleach while other corals don't bleach.

--- SENTENCE 1394 ---
This is a very mild bleaching event, and typically only parts of corals are bleached.

--- SENTENCE 1395 ---
Usually the upper parts of corals bleach first, the parts exposed to light.

--- SENTENCE 1396 ---
We know that intense light increases bleaching.

!

### PREDATION

--- SENTENCE 1407 ---
There are lots of predators on coral reefs, including predators that eat coral.

--- SENTENCE 1408 ---
Most do only minor damage to the coral, but a few can do a great deal of damage (Rotjan and Lewis, 2008).

#### Crown-of-thorns starfish

 Crown-of-thorns starfish eat coral by everting their stomach out their mouth so it is inside out, and putting the stomach on live coral and digesting the coral tissue.

--- SENTENCE 1409 ---
The starfish is soft and bendable and can bend around the tips of branching coral.

--- SENTENCE 1410 ---
On branching coral it eats the tips of branches but often can't reach the bases of branches.

--- SENTENCE 1411 ---
The dead area they leave is usually in patches about the size of the starfish or slightly smaller, which is the size the stomach can digest at one time.

--- SENTENCE 1412 ---
The starfish often hides in holes during the day and comes out at night to feed, but sometimes is out feeding during the day.

--- SENTENCE 1413 ---
It may not go far when it finishes eating one coral to go to the next coral it eats.

--- SENTENCE 1414 ---
So sometimes you can find a trail of white dead corals that it has left behind, and even figure out which direction it is going by which end of the trail has bright white coral (newly dead) and which has grey or green color (older dead coral with turf growing on it).

--- SENTENCE 1415 ---
When you see such a trail but don't see the starfish, looking in nearby holes may reveal the starfish that ate those corals.

!

--- SENTENCE 1416 ---
A crown-of-thorns starfish.

!

--- SENTENCE 1417 ---
[](_page_260_Picture_0.jpeg)

A crown-of-thorns starfish (Acanthaster cf.

--- SENTENCE 1418 ---
This species has long been called Acanthaster planci, but genetics show that Pacific and Indian Ocean populations are different species.

--- SENTENCE 1419 ---
Acanthaster planci is in the Indian Ocean and purple, and the Pacific species is Acanthaster cf.

--- SENTENCE 1420 ---
[](_page_261_Picture_0.jpeg)

A crown-of-thorns starfish with a trail of dead coral it has eaten behind it.

--- SENTENCE 1421 ---
Crown-of-thorns starfish are normally fairly rare, but occasionally there are millions of them in an "outbreak" which can eat and kill vast numbers of corals.

--- SENTENCE 1422 ---
Such an outbreak occurred here in 1978 and ate over 90% of all the corals.

--- SENTENCE 1423 ---
The primary cause is a plankton bloom right when the starfish spawn, which provides food for the larvae so huge numbers survive.

--- SENTENCE 1424 ---
One starfish can produce a million eggs.

--- SENTENCE 1425 ---
The plankton bloom may be produced by nutrients washed off of land by an unusually large rainstorm following a dry period when nutrients built up in the soil.

--- SENTENCE 1426 ---
The outbreaks around the Indo-Pacific were so bad in the 60's and 70's that some people thought they would destroy most Indo-Pacific reefs.

#### Drupella snails

 There are some small snails in the genus Drupella that also have had outbreaks some places like Japan and the Philippines.

--- SENTENCE 1441 ---
Drupella snails usually feed in a group, and can completely remove all the tissue from an area on a coral.

--- SENTENCE 1442 ---
Their small shells are bumpy and are usually covered with patches of coralline algae.

--- SENTENCE 1443 ---
They like to eat Acropora.

--- SENTENCE 1444 ---
They don't eat table corals very often, but when they do they are fairly easy to find, since they can't hide and they will be found on the border between the live and dead coral.

!

--- SENTENCE 1445 ---
[](_page_263_Picture_2.jpeg)

Most often, Drupella prefer to eat staghorns, maybe in part because they can hide better in the branches.

--- SENTENCE 1446 ---
They typically are hiding in a cluster down in the branches of the staghorn.

--- SENTENCE 1447 ---
They prefer to eat the coral down in the branches, and usually leave the branch tips alive.

--- SENTENCE 1448 ---
So the pattern is the opposite from crown-of-thorns.

--- SENTENCE 1449 ---
They are often hidden well enough down in the branches that you have to search to find them.

#### Parasitic snails

 Some snails feed on coral tissue, but take so little that the coral can heal as fast as the snail eats it.

--- SENTENCE 1452 ---
The result is that the snails are in effect parasites.

--- SENTENCE 1453 ---
Commonly they hide in cracks in massive corals, or deep between branches in branching corals like Pocillopora.

--- SENTENCE 1454 ---
They have smooth shells, and may be fairly hard to spot.

--- SENTENCE 1455 ---
They are usually cap-shaped like limpets.

#### Damselfish

 Some damselfish are algae farmers.

--- SENTENCE 1457 ---
They bite off the live polyps of the coral, let the turf or filamentous algae grow, and may even weed out undesirable species of algae.

--- SENTENCE 1458 ---
Because they bite off the coral polyps, they can be considered coral predators.

--- SENTENCE 1459 ---
Some, particularly a large grey species with a black spot at the rear base of their dorsal fin, prefer staghorns.

--- SENTENCE 1460 ---
They can be seen popping in and out from between the staghorn branches, and make popping sounds.

--- SENTENCE 1461 ---
Their dense turf gardens can be seen on the base of the staghorn branches.

--- SENTENCE 1462 ---
When they clear some more staghorn surface, it produces a bright white area between the dense turf and the live brown coral.

--- SENTENCE 1463 ---
Typically the boundary between the live brown coral and the bare white skeleton is very sharp.

!

--- SENTENCE 1464 ---
[](_page_267_Picture_0.jpeg)

Damage from a farmer damsel.

--- SENTENCE 1465 ---

--- SENTENCE 1466 ---
Their territory is typically all black compared to the purple coralline algae outside it.

--- SENTENCE 1467 ---

--- SENTENCE 1468 ---

# DISEASE

 Disease is defined in medicine as anything causing ill health.

--- SENTENCE 1518 ---
So it not only includes infectious diseases like colds and the flu and bacterial infections, it also includes diseases like diabetes, high blood pressure, heart attacks, strokes and cancer, which are disorders but are not infectious.

--- SENTENCE 1519 ---
Diseases affect all organisms, they are part of nature.

--- SENTENCE 1520 ---
For an ecosystem like a coral reef, we must expect some amount of disease to be present.

--- SENTENCE 1521 ---
That is natural and not a problem for us, but sometimes there are disease outbreaks and many organisms can die, and that is a problem.

--- SENTENCE 1522 ---
There are quite a few diseases of corals and coralline algae, and we discover new ones every once in a while.

--- SENTENCE 1523 ---
But for most diseases, we don't know the cause yet, and we aren't even sure which are separate diseases.

### Coralline algae diseases

#### CLOD

 CLOD stands for "Coralline Lethal Orange Disease."

--- SENTENCE 1524 ---
It is bright orange bacteria that kill and digest coralline algae.

--- SENTENCE 1525 ---
Soon after it was discovered, there seemed to be an outbreak of it in Fiji, with a lot of coralline algae killed.

--- SENTENCE 1526 ---
Since then there doesn't seem to have been much of a problem, and while we have it here and some places it is pretty common, the coralline algae grows back as fast as the CLOD can kill it, and there is a lot more coralline algae than CLOD (some places you have to search to find the CLOD).

--- SENTENCE 1527 ---
[](_page_280_Picture_3.jpeg)

CLOD forms a bright orange band on coralline algae, with live coralline algae on one side, and dead white coralline algae behind it.

--- SENTENCE 1528 ---
The bacteria must be able to move, here you can see places where it is streaming together off the dead areas toward the living areas.

--- SENTENCE 1529 ---
Remember that bacteria are amazingly small, much much smaller than our cells.

--- SENTENCE 1530 ---
But some bacteria can swim with a flagellum.

--- SENTENCE 1531 ---
The bacteria have not been identified.

#### Coralline Black Fungal Disease

A second disease on coralline algae is a black fungus.

--- SENTENCE 1532 ---
Originally it was only reported from American Samoa, but now it has also been found in the Line Islands at Palmyra and Kingman by Bernardo Vargas (Vargas-Angel, 2010).

--- SENTENCE 1533 ---
I've also recorded it in western Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1534 ---
It is less common than CLOD, but not really rare.

--- SENTENCE 1535 ---
I have only seen it on the slope.

--- SENTENCE 1536 ---
It is much harder to spot than CLOD, which stands out with the bright color.

--- SENTENCE 1537 ---
Thierry Work and Greta Aeby called this "Coralline Black Fungal Disease" in their 2006 set of disease identification cards for American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1538 ---
It has now been identified as Malassezia restricta by Williams et al, 2014.

### Coral Disease Signs and Lesions

 It is not possible to see diseases in living corals on the reefs, only signs of disease.

--- SENTENCE 1575 ---
Symptoms are what patients report themselves, so symptoms are only available in humans.

--- SENTENCE 1576 ---
Signs do not have a simple, one to one relationship with diseases.

--- SENTENCE 1577 ---
In human diseases, a few signs or symptoms are diagnostic, like the little red spots that indicate Chicken Pox (or measles).

--- SENTENCE 1578 ---
Other symptoms, like fever, are very non-specific, many different diseases can cause fever.

--- SENTENCE 1579 ---
A fever indicates there is likely to be a problem, but tells you very little about what the problem is.

--- SENTENCE 1580 ---
The things we view in the field are signs, and for most signs we don't know what the disease is.

--- SENTENCE 1581 ---
Further, some diseases appear to be found only in one host species, while other diseases appear to be found in several host species.

--- SENTENCE 1582 ---
Many human diseases are found only in humans, but some like rabies and bird flu, have primary animal hosts but can be transmitted to humans as well.

--- SENTENCE 1583 ---
Further, while some diseases are communicable and caused by infectious agents like bacteria, viruses, fungi, or protozoa, other diseases are not, such as metabolic diseases like diabetes, high blood pressure, stroke, high cholesterol, heart attacks, cancer and poisoning.

--- SENTENCE 1584 ---
In corals, what we see are signs, not diseases.

--- SENTENCE 1585 ---
In most cases we don't know what the disease is that causes the symptoms, we don't even know whether it is caused by an infectious disease agent, let alone what that agent might be.

--- SENTENCE 1586 ---
Further, we don't know if different symptoms are caused by different diseases, or whether similar-looking symptoms in different coral species are caused by the same or similar diseases or different diseases.

--- SENTENCE 1587 ---
We do know now that signs that appear the same in a species are sometimes produced by different disease agents.

--- SENTENCE 1588 ---
This is actually somewhat similar to disease diagnosis in humans, so, for instance the signs and symptoms of colds and flu and the new coronavirus are very similar, and without a lab test we often don't know which one we have.

--- SENTENCE 1589 ---
There are 4 or 5 serotypes of dengue, 4 different species of malaria, and several different causes of pneumonia, and so on.

--- SENTENCE 1590 ---
Because coral diseases have been so difficult to figure out, we need to be careful to not go beyond the information that we have, and presume that distinctive looking signs are caused by a disease that is different from that which causes other signs, or that the disease is caused by an infective agent.

--- SENTENCE 1591 ---
It may be, or it may not be, we don't know.

--- SENTENCE 1592 ---
We may have our suspicions, but those are essentially hypotheses that need to be tested.

--- SENTENCE 1593 ---
For these reasons, the things in the photographs presented here are referred to as signs.

--- SENTENCE 1594 ---
In some cases they may correspond to distinct diseases, in others they probably don't, and at this time we really don't know in most cases.

--- SENTENCE 1595 ---
Diseases are defined as anything that reduces functioning.

--- SENTENCE 1596 ---
So coral bleaching, caused by stresses like high temperature, low temperature, fresh water, and low light can technically be called a disease.

--- SENTENCE 1597 ---
Predation can also be technically considered disease.

--- SENTENCE 1598 ---
Mechanical damage from things like hurricanes, tsunamis, construction projects, and people breaking coral can also be technically considered disease.

--- SENTENCE 1599 ---
The same can be said

about sediment damage and competition with algae caused by nutrients.

--- SENTENCE 1600 ---
These have all been covered in sections above.

--- SENTENCE 1601 ---
The signs considered in this section are those that are not caused by the things listed above alone, and may be caused by infectious agents (though in most cases we don't know that for sure).

--- SENTENCE 1602 ---
Some things like bleaching and predation, can sometimes look quite similar to some of the diseases listed below, but hopefully this guide will help distinguish them.

--- SENTENCE 1603 ---
Although all we can see are just signs, which don't correspond with diseases very well, we are pretty sure that these symptoms indicate a reduction of functioning and therefore indicate diseases.

--- SENTENCE 1604 ---
What we don't know is what causes them, whether it is an infectious agent or agents, or something else.

--- SENTENCE 1605 ---
Even if there is an infectious agent, it may strike in part because the coral was weakened by some kind of stress like high temperatures.

--- SENTENCE 1606 ---
Disease outbreaks following bleaching events have been documented, so there is direct evidence for this.

--- SENTENCE 1607 ---
Some microbes or parasites live on or in a host without causing any problems until something changes such as an increase in stress or a break in surface protection, which allows the microbe or parasite to become an active disease agent.

--- SENTENCE 1608 ---
There are a variety of coral disease signs in American Samoa, and periodically the author finds another that we've never seen before.

--- SENTENCE 1609 ---
The causes of most are complete mysteries, but the causes of a few have been determined.

--- SENTENCE 1610 ---
The more scientific approach is to simply describe the appearance of the disease symptoms and let the scientists try to figure out what it is.

--- SENTENCE 1611 ---
However, I'm going to go out on a limb and show you some things that seem to be distinct, even though we don't know what causes them.

--- SENTENCE 1612 ---
Keep in mind that there are more things that look like disease than I am presenting here, but these are the most distinctive or common.

--- SENTENCE 1613 ---
Also, keep in mind that some of the things I am presenting as separate disease symptoms may actually be the same disease, so there may be several different "white tissue loss diseases" or some of them may be the same thing, we really don't know at this stage.

--- SENTENCE 1614 ---
As imperfect and incomplete as recognizing disease signs on the reef is, it is a first basic step that needs to be at least begun.

--- SENTENCE 1615 ---
We need to be able to recognize diseases in order to monitor them and study them.

--- SENTENCE 1616 ---
I think photos are critical, "a picture is worth a thousand words" as words don't always convey every important aspect of diseases (or host species for that matter).

--- SENTENCE 1617 ---
Looking through these disease photos can help you recognize things that appear to be distinct in the field, a first, if limited, step in studying coral diseases.

--- SENTENCE 1618 ---
At this point, the coral diseases in American Samoa look as diverse or more so than they do in the Caribbean, which has had much more study, and where a bewildering variety of coral diseases have appeared in recent years.

--- SENTENCE 1619 ---
Dr.'s Aeby and Work speak of three general groups of symptoms in Pacific coral diseases, "tissue loss," "discoloration," and "growth anomalies."

--- SENTENCE 1620 ---
Tissue loss means that tissue is dying and then is lost from the colony.

--- SENTENCE 1621 ---
When tissue loss occurs, often an area of coral appears white, so another name might be "white diseases" or "white signs."

--- SENTENCE 1622 ---
Coral tissue often doesn't die when there is discoloration, but it can.

--- SENTENCE 1623 ---
Many growth anomalies can be seen without mortality, but if you search long enough you will probably find at least a few with some tissue loss.

--- SENTENCE 1624 ---
So the three groups may not be completely separable, but they provide a good start to organizing these diverse phenomena.

--- SENTENCE 1625 ---
We will cover tissue loss, then discoloration, and last growth anomalies.

#### Tissue Loss

#### Acropora White Syndrome (AWS)

--- SENTENCE 1626 ---
This is a disease symptom that is well enough known to have a widely used name ("white syndrome").

--- SENTENCE 1627 ---
It is also one of the most lethal diseases we know of in the Pacific, and most widespread.

--- SENTENCE 1628 ---
It is most common and easy to observe on table corals.

--- SENTENCE 1629 ---
Typically, there is a white area, which on one side has a sharp dividing line between it and living healthy looking coral.

--- SENTENCE 1630 ---
On the other side, the white fades into grey or green as turf grows on the dead coral skeleton.

--- SENTENCE 1631 ---

--- SENTENCE 1632 ---
Typically once it starts on a table it is likely to keep going around it until it has killed the entire colony, but in an outbreak at Vatia after a hurricane, the white band stopped on many colonies before killing the whole colony.

--- SENTENCE 1633 ---
It is fairly common.

--- SENTENCE 1634 ---
It was reported to be caused by a bacterium, Vibrio sp., in the Marshall Islands.

--- SENTENCE 1635 ---
This was called "Acropora White Syndrome" in the 2006 disease card set for American Samoa by Thierry Work and Greta Aeby.

--- SENTENCE 1636 ---
It is relatively common.

--- SENTENCE 1637 ---

#### Pocillopora White Syndrome

 Pocillopora colonies commonly get disease symptoms which have a white band that moves over each branch killing it.

--- SENTENCE 1662 ---
Sometimes yellow algae can be growing below the band where it has killed the branch some time ago.

--- SENTENCE 1663 ---
This disease is certainly lethal for branches, and it may be lethal for most entire colonies too.

--- SENTENCE 1664 ---
This colony shows a typical pattern with some live branches, some newly dead white branches, and darker branches that have been dead longer.

--- SENTENCE 1665 ---
The slopes have many live, many diseased, and many intact dead colonies of Pocillopora.

--- SENTENCE 1666 ---
This appears to be a disease that has a steady moderate abundance and causes a lot of Pocillopora mortality.

--- SENTENCE 1667 ---
It and white syndrome seem to be the most common coral diseases in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1668 ---
This has been found on P. eydouxi, P. verrucosa, P. meandrina, P. setichelli and P. damicornis in American Samoa, so it appears to be on all species of Pocillopora here.

--- SENTENCE 1669 ---
It appears to be more common on P. eydouxi and less common on P. damicornis.

--- SENTENCE 1673 ---
Thierry Work and Greta Aeby call this "Pocillopora White Band" in their 2006 set of disease identification cards for American Samoa.

### Massive Porites White Patch Syndrome

 Some massive Porites have white patches on them.

--- SENTENCE 1903 ---
The white patches have sharp edges to them, they are irregular in size and shape, they are usually fairly small but not tiny (so about 1-3 inches across is common).

--- SENTENCE 1904 ---
They are never in pairs on bumps like fish bite marks, even though some people have suggested they are fish bites.

--- SENTENCE 1905 ---
Close examination shows no evidence of scraping.

--- SENTENCE 1906 ---
Plus, they are not uniform in size or shape and don't have the shape of fish bites.

--- SENTENCE 1907 ---
They are clearly not fish bites.

--- SENTENCE 1908 ---
Within the white patch, the coral is alive and appears as though it is healthy, tentacles can be extended.

--- SENTENCE 1909 ---
I have only once or twice seen any part of any of the patches appear to be dead (see below).

--- SENTENCE 1910 ---
The patches show no sign of becoming larger and spreading over the coral to engulf it.

--- SENTENCE 1911 ---
So, the disease, whatever it is, is very rarely if ever lethal.

--- SENTENCE 1912 ---
A few patches have the same appearance, except the patch is not white, rather it is some intermediate color between white and the color of healthy areas of the colony.

--- SENTENCE 1913 ---
That could (could!) be a patch recovering, or a patch getting the disease.

--- SENTENCE 1914 ---
Colonies with these white patches are uncommon but not rare.

--- SENTENCE 1915 ---
Lawrence et al (2015) named this "white patch syndrome" and that convention is followed here.

--- SENTENCE 1916 ---
They studied it in eastern Australia.

#### Growth Anomalies

#### Convex Growth Anomalies Produced by Accelerated Skeletal Extension

 These are unusual growths on corals, often lumps.

--- SENTENCE 2165 ---
Some people have called them tumors or neoplasms.

--- SENTENCE 2166 ---
They appear to be uncontrolled growth like a tumor or cancer, however, the growth (lump) is actually of the skeleton, not the tissues.

--- SENTENCE 2167 ---
At the very least, it is a loss of control of the calcification process.

--- SENTENCE 2168 ---
A more neutral term is "growth anomalies" meaning just unusual growths.

--- SENTENCE 2169 ---
Some colonies have lots of them, some have a few, and most colonies don't have any.

--- SENTENCE 2170 ---
I have seen a table (Acropora hyacinthus) with 140 on it.

--- SENTENCE 2171 ---
They are easiest to recognize on table corals, and indeed they may be most common on table corals, but they are also on other corals.

--- SENTENCE 2172 ---
They are often lighter colored than the rest of the table coral.

--- SENTENCE 2173 ---
Sometimes they have little branchlets, but the branchlets are not uniform and point in all sorts of directions.

--- SENTENCE 2174 ---
Others have what look like corallites, and others are just bumpy with no recognizable corallites or branches.

--- SENTENCE 2175 ---
Some places (like parts of Fagatele Bay and Vatia Bay) have quite a few of these, other places have very few.

--- SENTENCE 2176 ---
They are most often seen on Acropora hyacinthus, but then that is a common coral.

--- SENTENCE 2177 ---
They are the most common type of growth anomalies.

--- SENTENCE 2178 ---

#### Massive Porites Growth Anomalies

 Massive Porites colonies have growth anomalies that form smooth rounded low domes that are taller than the small lumps on normal areas.

--- SENTENCE 2331 ---
They retain normal coloration.

--- SENTENCE 2332 ---
They can be quite large, up to at least about 6 feet across.

--- SENTENCE 2333 ---
It may be that death occurs on white anomalies because the white tissue cannot produce its own food and if it can't get enough food from surrounding healthy tissue it starves to death.

--- SENTENCE 2334 ---
Growths that retain zooxanthellae and color may not die because they retain a sufficient food source.

--- SENTENCE 2335 ---
These are fairly common some places like the Ofu pools.

#### Cyanobacteria on Staghorn

This cyanobacteria is a bit unusual.

--- SENTENCE 2466 ---
The filaments are strong and do not break easily, and it is not slimy.

--- SENTENCE 2467 ---
It grows on live staghorn corals, and kills the branches.

--- SENTENCE 2468 ---
Some white newly dead branches can be seen here.

--- SENTENCE 2469 ---
After the coral is all dead, the black filaments disappear.

--- SENTENCE 2470 ---
Some clumps of filaments can be seen drifting in the water, and it seems likely that they get caught on the rough staghorn branches and then start to grow there and spread.

--- SENTENCE 2471 ---
Small amounts have been found on other corals like finger coral in the area of a larger growth on staghorn.

--- SENTENCE 2472 ---

#### Pigmentation Response

Porites corals commonly produce pink color when tissues are irritated or damaged.

--- SENTENCE 2477 ---
Here, a vermatid snail embedded in a massive Porites colony puts out mucus streams and then pulls them in to eat whatever it catches.

--- SENTENCE 2478 ---
Notice that the upper parts of bumps and ridges on the coral are bluish, and all pink spots are found on the tops of bumps or ridges.

--- SENTENCE 2479 ---
The mucus often catches on the coral, and it likely pulls in coral mucus as well as its own.

--- SENTENCE 2480 ---
The irritation causes the Porites to pull in the polyps and expose the blue color of this colony, and if it is intense enough, the tissue swells and becomes pink.

--- SENTENCE 2481 ---
This appears to be a general response to irritation or damage of many kinds, and not an infectious disease (Willis and Dinsdale, 2004).

!

--- SENTENCE 2482 ---
[](_page_634_Picture_2.jpeg)

Here is a photo of where the macroalgae in the top of the photo has rubbed against a Porites coral in the bottom part of the coral, abrading the coral tissue and producing a white area.

--- SENTENCE 2483 ---
Around the white area is an area where the Porites tissue is inflated and pink.

--- SENTENCE 2484 ---
This is the pigmentation response.

--- SENTENCE 2485 ---
Sometimes when it forms a narrow pink line around an injured area, people have thought that it was a disease, "pink line disease."

--- SENTENCE 2486 ---
But it is just a response to any of several kinds of irritation or damage.

--- SENTENCE 2577 ---
The Author

Douglas Fenner

B.A. Reed College, USA, 1971 Ph.D. University of Pennsylvania, USA, 1976

Born in Michigan, USA, the author lived in a variety of places in the states, including Florida during his high school years, which stimulated an interest in tropical marine life.

--- SENTENCE 2578 ---
During his years at Reed College in Portland, Oregon, he was introduced to biology, including invertebrate biology, studied sea urchin tube feet and respiration for his thesis and spent two summers in Hawaii studying fish behavior with his professors.

--- SENTENCE 2579 ---
Once he graduated, he attended the summer invertebrate zoology course at the Marine Biological Laboratory at Woods Hole, Massachusetts and then another summer was a course assistant for that course.

--- SENTENCE 2580 ---
Snorkeling trips to the Caribbean (including to Jamaica just before Hurricane Allen) during graduate school at the University of Pennsylvania were followed by scuba trips to the Caribbean.

--- SENTENCE 2581 ---
His coral reef research and publications began with surveys and descriptions of reefs in the Caribbean, including Cozumel, Roatan, Cayman Brac, Little Cayman, and St. Lucia.

--- SENTENCE 2582 ---
It became clear that to do transects you need to know your corals, and existing guides were inadequate, so Caribbean coral identification and taxonomy were next to be studied.

--- SENTENCE 2583 ---
By this time the author lived in Seattle, Washington.

--- SENTENCE 2584 ---
Then the author began to study corals in Hawaii, which led to his identification book for Hawaiian corals.

--- SENTENCE 2585 ---
He then worked in the Philippines for two years, learning many coral species in that area of high diversity.

--- SENTENCE 2586 ---
This was followed by six years of working with Dr. "Charlie" J.E.N. Veron at the Australian Institute of Marine Science on the "Coral ID" electronic key to corals of the world.

--- SENTENCE 2587 ---
At that time, the author began to be invited to study and record corals during Rapid Assessment Programs in a variety of places around the Indo-Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 2588 ---
In November 2003, the author began work at the Dept. Marine & Wildlife Resources, in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 2589 ---
He began working on coral reef monitoring there a year later and continued with that and continued to make trips to study corals around the Indo-Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 2590 ---
Currently, the author has studied coral at 14 islands in the Caribbean and 14 areas of the Indo-Pacific, plus southern Italy.

--- SENTENCE 2591 ---
He is the author of 17 book chapters and 46 peer-reviewed articles in scientific journals.

--- SENTENCE 2592 ---
He has worked as a contractor for NOAA NMFS Protected Species on the threatened coral species since 2013.

--- SENTENCE 2593 ---
That work has taken him around the Pacific each year to study corals and teach people how to identify corals.

--- SENTENCE 2594 ---
That effort includes photographing corals, writing field guides, and building "practice modules" for teaching coral ID and people to practice with.

--- SENTENCE 2595 ---
He currently works on describing new coral species and diseases and a variety of other coral reef topics.

--- SENTENCE 2596 ---
He continues to be based in American Samoa.

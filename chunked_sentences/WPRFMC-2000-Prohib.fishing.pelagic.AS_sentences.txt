## 2.0 SUMMARY

#### 2.1 Need for Action

The entry of new large US vessels (> 50 ft overall length) into the pelagics fishery in the Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) around American Samoa could conflict with objectives of the Council's management plan for pelagic fisheries (FMP) by 1) failing to achieve optimum yield, as defined in the FMP; 2) creating gear conflicts, particularly in areas of concentrated fishing; and 3) reducing the opportunities for (a) profitable fishing operations; (b) traditional fishing practices for non-market personal consumption and cultural benefits; and (c) satisfying recreational fishing experiences.

--- SENTENCE 5 ---
The nature and extent of interactions between large (> 50 ft overall length) and smallscale vessels (< 50 ft overall length) fishing for pelagic species in the EEZ around American Samoa are not clearly understood.

--- SENTENCE 6 ---
The information available suggests that an increase in the number of large vessels fishing in the same areas as the small-scale fleet could reduce pelagic fish densities and catch rates.

--- SENTENCE 7 ---
Gear conflicts could also arise between large and small pelagic fishing vessels.

--- SENTENCE 8 ---
The potential for interactions between large and small vessels is increasing because (a) the fishing range of the small-scale fishery is expanding offshore with the addition of new and safer types of boats; and (b) up to 40 large vessels previously based in Hawaii are seeking new home ports in the Pacific after a US court order in August 2000 almost completely closed the central North Pacific longline fishery for swordfish to vessels with Hawaii longline limited entry permits.

--- SENTENCE 9 ---
The proposed action would maintain the potential for economically viable catch rates in the small-scale fishery and would help to avoid gear conflicts.

--- SENTENCE 10 ---
This will provide for sustained community participation in the small-scale pelagic fishery, not only to produce food, income and employment, but also to contribute to the perpetuation of Samoan culture.

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
A significant reduction in the supply of fish would be potentially ruinous to the community, which depends on fisheries resources for cultural continuity, as well as for economic development.

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
Local overfishing, economic overfishing and/or gear conflict could occur in the EEZ around American Samoa if the overall size of the longline fleet becomes excessive.

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
Measures to restrict the total number of vessels in the fishery, such as a license limitation system, may be necessary in the future to control such problems.

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
Such controls would be considered "new management measures" under the FMP's framework procedure for adaptive management.

--- SENTENCE 15 ---
In anticipation of possibly creating a limited access system, the Council and NMFS established a control date of July 15, 2000, after which the owner of any size vessel entering the fishery will not be assured of being allowed to use longline gear to fish for pelagic management unit species in the EEZ around American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
The control date places the owners of longline vessels of any size on notice that they might be excluded if they entered the fishery after this date.

## 2.2 Comparison of management alternatives

The management alternatives considered by the Council included: 1) the preferred alternative, prohibiting US vessels more than 50 ft in length from fishing for pelagic management unit species (PMUS) within an area extending approximately 50 nm offshore from all islands of American Samoa; 2) prohibiting such vessels from fishing for PMUS within an area extending approximately 50 nm offshore from Tutuila island, Rose Atoll and the Manu'a islands and approximately 30 nm offshore from Swains Island; 3) prohibiting such vessels from fishing for PMUS within an area approximately 100 nm around all islands of American Samoa; and 4) prohibiting such vessels from fishing for PMUS in the existing area closure for foreign longline vessels.

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
The Council also considered the alternative of taking no action (Alternative No. 5).

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
Alternatives 1-4 all include exemptions for the owner of any large vessel (> 50 ft overall length) that was registered for use with a NMFS Longline General Permit and had landed PMUS in American Samoa on or prior to November 13, 1997.

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
At the time when the framework measure was first considered (1998), the small-scale fleet in American Samoa was comprised entirely of vessels under 30 ft in length, albacore tuna catch rates were relatively stable and the distant-water US purse seine fishery was not believed to interact with small-scale surface tuna fisheries over great distances.

### 2.3 Preferred Framework Measure

The preferred framework measure was selected by the Council at a meeting on June 14, 2000.

--- SENTENCE 83 ---
It would prohibit the taking of pelagic management unit species (PMUS) by US fishing vessels larger than 50 ft (length overall) from waters within an area that extends approximately 50 nautical miles (nm) offshore of the seaward boundary of the territorial sea around all islands of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 84 ---
An owner of a vessel greater than 50 ft in overall length that was registered for use with a National Marine Fisheries Service (NMFS) Longline General Permit and had landed PMUS in American Samoa on or prior to November 13, 1997, would be exempt from the prohibition to take PMUS within the closed area.

--- SENTENCE 85 ---
A landing of PMUS demonstrating eligibility for an exemption to fish for PMUS in the American Samoa pelagic fishery prohibited management areas shall be based on information in the Western Pacific federal daily longline log forms which were properly recorded and submitted to NMFS, as required in § 660.14.

--- SENTENCE 86 ---
An exemption is valid only for a qualifying large vessel or a replacement vessel of equal or smaller length overall than the vessel that was initially registered for use with a longline general permit on or prior to November 13, 1997.

--- SENTENCE 87 ---
An exemption may not be transferred to another vessel owner, whose vessel is greater than 50 ft.

--- SENTENCE 88 ---
To facilitate enforcement of the management measure, straight lines are used to define the boundaries of the area closures.

--- SENTENCE 89 ---
The area closure boundaries around Tutuila Island, Rose Atoll and the Manu'a Islands are identified by lines connecting the following coordinates:

### 2.4 Rationale

The preferred framework measure identified by the Council at a meeting on June 14, 2000, is a 50-nm area closure around all the islands of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 91 ---
This would encompass all of the areas where the small-scale fleet presently fishes and all of the known banks and seamounts which are likely to aggregate tuna, thus providing a buffer area for fishery expansion.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
Not only does this measure avoid gear conflicts by separating large vessels from small ones but it encourages domestic harvest of underutilized pelagic fishery resources at a small scale that is more likely to achieve optimum yield than uncontrolled and overcapitalized (i.e., "boom and bust") development by large vessels.

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
Just as importantly, the measure maintains the potential for economically viable catches of pelagic fish in the small-scale fishery as American Samoa's fishing culture evolves from a traditional subsistence activity harvesting heavily exploited, inshore marine resources to a more commercial activity harvesting underutilized offshore pelagic resources.

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
Due to a rapidly growing population and overexploitation of some inshore seafood resources, the American Samoa community is becoming increasingly dependent on pelagic fish for food, employment and income from fisheries and for perpetuation of *fa 'a Samoa* (the Samoan cultural heritage and way of life).

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
The role of the small-scale pelagic fishery in cultural continuity is at least as important as the contributions made to nutritional or economic well-being of island residents (Severance et al. 1999).

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
Establishing a closed area is consistent with management initiatives undertaken throughout the Pacific islands to minimize interactions between large and small-scale pelagic fisheries.

--- SENTENCE 97 ---
Precautionary management before a crisis occurs is preferable to repeating the experience in Hawaii, where fishery managers were forced to respond to sudden and uncontrolled expansion of the longline fishery in the late 1980s without adequate lead time or data.

--- SENTENCE 98 ---
Maintaining the potential for economically-viable catches by the small-scale fleet by establishing closed areas would impose some economic costs on large vessels that would be excluded from fishing for pelagic species.

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
The latter have the capability of traveling to more distant fishing grounds if catch rates decline nearshore, whereas small vessels do not have this option.

--- SENTENCE 100 ---
Owners of large vessels that were registered for use with NMFS General Longline Permits and had made landings of PMUS on or before November 13, 1997, are exempt from the area closures.

--- SENTENCE 101 ---
When the latter vessels were acquired, their owners could not have anticipated that they would be excluded from fishing for pelagic species in portions of the EEZ around American Samoa or that they would not be able to replace their vessels without penalty.

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
It is impossible to quantify the number of additional pelagic fish that will be available to the small-boat fishery as a result of the proposed action or the additional benefit that will accrue to American Samoa and the Nation by this increase.

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
It is clear, however, that the potential benefits to the small-boat pelagic fishery in American Samoa are substantial, whereas the potential costs that may be imposed on the excluded large vessels are likely to be low.

--- SENTENCE 104 ---
Hence, the expected benefits of the proposed action to American Samoa and the Nation are anticipated to outweigh the expected costs.

#### 2.5 Annual evaluation

An evaluation of the biological, economic and social impacts of the closed areas will be made each year as part of the annual status report prepared by the Council for the pelagic fisheries managed in the Western Pacific Region.

--- SENTENCE 105 ---
During the evaluation, the views and opinions of representatives of all sectors of the fishing industry in American Samoa will be solicited.

--- SENTENCE 106 ---
The size of the closed areas may be expanded or contracted as new information becomes available.

--- SENTENCE 107 ---
The existing FMP for pelagic fisheries provides a framework procedure allowing for rapid implementation of regulatory adjustments in response to changing conditions in the fishery or resource base.

--- SENTENCE 108 ---
Following these procedures, the Council may recommend to the Regional Director that established measures, such as the area closures in the present framework proposal, be rapidly modified.

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
The Council will also examine the need to restrict fishing effort or catches within the EEZ of American Samoa, including the possibility of a limited entry program for domestic longline fishing vessels.

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
In anticipation of the latter possibility, the Council and NMFS have established a control date of July 15, 2000.

--- SENTENCE 111 ---
Owners of any vessel of any size entering the fishery after this date will not be assured of being allowed to use longline gear to fish for pelagic management unit species in the EEZ around American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 112 ---
The control date of November 23, 1997, remains applicable with respect to owners of vessels greater than 50 ft overall length if the proposed framework measure to establish area closures to large pelagic fishing vessels is approved.

## 4.4 Existing management measures

A National Marine Fisheries Service (NMFS) longline general permit is required for longline fishing in American Samoa's EEZ.

--- SENTENCE 130 ---
This fishery is presently open access, with no limits on the number of longline vessels, individual or total vessel capacity, catch or effort.

--- SENTENCE 131 ---
A control date of November 13, 1997, has been established and all applicants for longline permits after that date are informed that they may not qualify for exemptions to limitations placed on longline vessels greater than 50 ft in overall length.

--- SENTENCE 132 ---
In anticipation of the possibility of a limited entry program for domestic longline fishing vessels, the Council and NMFS have established a control date of July 15, 2000, after which owners of any vessel of any size entering the fishery will not be assured of being allowed to use longline gear to fish for pelagic management unit species in the EEZ around American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 133 ---
There has been no legal fishing by foreign longline vessels in the EEZ around American Samoa since 1980, when the pelagic fisheries Preliminary Management Plan (PMP) for the Western Pacific Region was implemented.

--- SENTENCE 134 ---
The Fishery Management Plan prepared by the WPRFMC in 1986 for pelagic fisheries prohibited foreign longline fishing within a rectangle around the principal islands of American Samoa bounded by 14° and 15° S and 168° and 171° W, and in a one degree square surrounding Swain's Island bounded by 10° 33' and 11° 33' S and 170° 34' W and 171° 34" W.

There is a possibility that legal fishing in the EEZ by foreign vessels may resume at some time under a Pacific Insular Area Fishing Agreement (PIAFA), which could give foreign vessels access to the EEZ around American Samoa in exchange for a negotiated fee and subject to a variety of permit conditions.

### 5.0 PURPOSE AND NEED FOR ACTION

The proposed action is needed for several purposes:

- To maintain the potential for economically viable catch rates in the small-scale fishery as it evolves from a traditional subsistence activity harvesting heavily exploited inshore marine resources to a more commercial activity extending the range of fishing offshore to harvest underutilized pelagic fish (i.e., achieve optimum yield as defined in pelagic FMP).
-

--- SENTENCE 135 ---
To avoid gear conflicts between large and small-scale vessels within the fishing range of the small-scale pelagic fishery.
- To provide for sustained community participation in the small-scale pelagic fishery, recognizing that American Samoa is becoming increasingly dependent on pelagic fish for food, income, employment and perpetuation of Samoan culture.

--- SENTENCE 136 ---
Maintain the potential for economically viable catch rates in the small-scale fishery

Subsistence fishing traditionally provided most of the protein in the diet of the indigenous people who originally settled the Samoa islands.

--- SENTENCE 137 ---
As American Samoa's economy shifted from purely subsistence toward a more commercial orientation in contemporary times, so did small-scale fisheries (Hill 1978).

--- SENTENCE 138 ---

# 5.3 Provide for sustained community participation and cultural continuity

American Samoans are among the last full-blooded Polynesians.

--- SENTENCE 204 ---
Their dependence on fishing undoubtedly goes back as far as the peopled history of the Samoa islands, about 3,500 years ago (Severance and Franco, 1989).

--- SENTENCE 205 ---
Many aspects of the culture have changed in contemporary times but Samoans have retained a traditional social system that continues to strongly influence and depend upon the culture of fishing.

--- SENTENCE 206 ---
Centered around an extended family ('aiga) and allegiance to a hierarchy of chiefs (matai), this system is rooted in the economics and politics of communally-held village land.

--- SENTENCE 207 ---
It has effectively resisted Euro-American colonial influence and has contributed to a contemporary cultural resiliency unique in the Pacific islands region (Severance et al. 1999).

--- SENTENCE 208 ---
American Samoa Government policy stated in the Revised Constitution of 1966 emphasizes the need to safeguard the traditional system of social organization and provide for cultural continuity.

--- SENTENCE 209 ---
Traditional Samoan values still exert a strong influence on when and why people fish, how they distribute their catch and the meaning of fish within the society.

--- SENTENCE 210 ---
When distributed, fish and other resources move through a complex and culturally embedded exchange system that supports the food needs of 'aiga, as well as the status of both matai and village ministers (Severance et al. 1999).

--- SENTENCE 211 ---
Customary exchanges include:

<u>Fa'alavelave</u> --

--- SENTENCE 212 ---
As a noun, mutual assistance to kinsmen in times of need; as a verb, to provide assistance in times of need.

--- SENTENCE 213 ---
This assistance can be in the form of food from the land or sea, or money derived from local or overseas labor markets.

--- SENTENCE 214 ---
<u>Tautua</u> – As a noun, service to the kin group and to the <u>matai</u> as leader of the kin group; as a verb, to serve the kin group and its <u>matai</u>.

<u>Fesoasoani</u> – To help out; a less formalized, more individualized, response to a less serious need than in the case of fa'alayelaye.

--- SENTENCE 215 ---
<u>To'onai</u> – A ceremonial need served after Sunday service, where ministers, matai, other village leaders and important visitors to the village, reaffirm cultural and spiritual solidarity.

--- SENTENCE 216 ---
<u>Fa'ataualofa</u> -- To give away or sell at a reduced price to friends or kinsmen as an expression of an ongoing, sustained relationship.

--- SENTENCE 217 ---
American Samoa has a long history of harvesting pelagic fish species, especially skipjack and small yellowfin tuna, which has special significance in customary exchanges (Severance and Franco, 1989).

--- SENTENCE 218 ---
Due to a rapidly growing population and overexploitation of some inshore seafood resources, the American Samoa community is becoming even more dependent on pelagic fish for food, employment and income from fisheries and for perpetuation of fa'a Samoa (Samoan cultural heritage and way of life).

--- SENTENCE 219 ---
Despite increasing commercialization, the small-scale pelagic fishery continues to contributes strongly to the cultural identity and social cohesion of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 220 ---
The role of pelagic fish in meeting cultural obligations is at least as important as the contributions made to nutritional or economic well-being of island residents (Severance et al. 1999).

--- SENTENCE 221 ---
The Samoa islands are an area of only modest productivity for pelagic fish resources compared to areas to the north, south and west.

--- SENTENCE 222 ---
Fluctuations in pelagic fish densities and catches are inevitable as local fish movement patterns change in response to the ocean environment and the availability of forage.

--- SENTENCE 223 ---
Community participation in American Samoa's small-scale fishery can be sustained only by maintaining the potential for economically viable catch rates of pelagic fish within the entire fishing range of the small vessels (< 50 ft length).

--- SENTENCE 224 ---
A large choice of fishing areas allows flexibility is locating high densities of pelagic fish.

--- SENTENCE 225 ---
Pelagic fishing areas off any particular island, such as Tutuila or Swains, should not be thought of as supporting only that island but the collective fishing community of the American Samoa islands.

--- SENTENCE 226 ---
The proposed action is needed so that the collective fishing grounds close to the islands of American Samoa can provide a more stable resource base for sustained community participation in the small-scale pelagic fishery.

--- SENTENCE 227 ---
Disruption or collapse of this fishery could jeopardize the availability of pelagic fish for customary food exchanges to meet cultural obligations.

# 7.0 INITIAL ACTIONS

The Council was asked at the 92<sup>nd</sup> meeting in April, 1997, to assist in forming a fishermen's working group to consider various management options to ensure the long term sustainability of the small-boat fishery.

--- SENTENCE 235 ---
Various meetings of the working group and other fishermen were convened by the Council and the American Samoa Department of Marine and Wildlife Resources between June and October 1997.

--- SENTENCE 236 ---
The consensus among fishermen was that the most effective management action would be to establish a 100 nm closed area around the islands of American Samoa that prevented entry into the fishery by new pelagic fishing vessels larger than 50 ft in length.

--- SENTENCE 237 ---
Fishermen contended that vessels larger than 50 ft are generally highly-capitalized, "industrial-scale" vessels whose fishing activities are capable of having a significant negative impact on the small-boat fishery.

--- SENTENCE 238 ---
During the meetings fishermen stated that as the small-scale pelagic fishery develops, they may acquire larger vessels in the 35-45 ft range.

--- SENTENCE 239 ---
Such boats would have a greater fishing range, be capable of landing more fish of a higher quality and provide a safer fishing platform.

--- SENTENCE 240 ---
According to fishermen, a 100 nm area closure would help ensure that adequate pelagic resources are available to support this increase in the fishing capacity of the small-scale fleet by encompassing all the major offshore banks, seamounts and pinnacles around which pelagic species aggregate, as well as create a "buffer zone" to further minimize potential interactions with industrial-scale vessels.

--- SENTENCE 241 ---
During the meetings in American Samoa, fishermen also noted that reduced catch rates and gear conflict could occur if the size of the small-boat fleet becomes excessive.

--- SENTENCE 242 ---
If a significant increase in fishing effort occurs, however, measures to control effort, such as a license limitation system, may be required.

--- SENTENCE 243 ---
In anticipation of possibly creating a limited access system, NMFS established a control date of November 13, 1997, after which the owner of any vessel entering the fishery will not be assured of being allowed to use longline gear to fish for pelagic management unit species in the EEZ around American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 244 ---
The control date placed the owners of longline vessels on notice that they might be excluded if they entered the fishery after this date.

--- SENTENCE 245 ---

# 8.2 Biological/Ecological Impacts

## 8.2.1 Impacts on Target Pelagic Fish Resources

The preferred alternative would have little or no stockwide impact on tunas or secondary pelagic species, including billfish, sharks and epipelagic fish, which are taken in the pelagic fishery in the EEZ around American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 281 ---
Pelagic species are not confined to any particular EEZ or country but have a wide geographical distribution in the central and western Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 282 ---
None of these stocks are presently overexploited in the South Pacific and stockwide effects are unlikely even if the American Samoa catch increased several fold.

--- SENTENCE 283 ---
The stock structures of pelagic fish are by no means well defined.

--- SENTENCE 284 ---
Long distance movements are evident for all tuna species but mixing of various fractions of stocks between areas, at least over short and medium time periods, seems to be quite incomplete.

--- SENTENCE 285 ---
Fluctuations in local ocean environmental conditions or prey availability can cause striking and unpredictable changes in the relative abundance and catch rates of pelagic fish, as a function of local movement patterns rather than "overfishing."

--- SENTENCE 286 ---
If these fluctuations reduce the availability of pelagic fish in a localized area, they have a much more severe impact on small-scale fleets with limited fishing range than on larger, more mobile vessels.

--- SENTENCE 287 ---
Local reductions in pelagic fish density can be amplified by interactions with nearby fisheries.

--- SENTENCE 288 ---
The effect of fishing tuna in one area may affect the performance of a fishery harvesting the same stock in a nearby area.

--- SENTENCE 289 ---
How various gears fishing in the same time and area compete "locally" to exploit pelagic fish resources and the effects on availability of the target fish are poorly understood.

--- SENTENCE 290 ---
It is not understood whether the sub-surface albacore tuna taken in American Samoa's small-scale longline fishery are primarily a local sub-population or are part of a more widely distributed regional mass (Stanley and Toloa 1998).

--- SENTENCE 291 ---
The albacore catch by small-scale longline fisheries in American Samoa and neighboring Samoa totaled 6,800 mt in 1998, which represents 19% of the South Pacific regional albacore harvest by longline fisheries (Lawson, 1999).

--- SENTENCE 292 ---
Albacore catches in American Samoa's longline fishery decreased from 454 mt in 1998 to 302 mt in 1999 (Coan et al. 2000).

--- SENTENCE 293 ---
The effect of rapid expansion and high catches by the small-scale longline fishery in western Samoa on the regional "throughput" of albacore cannot be estimated but may be a contributing factor in a recent decline in CPUE experienced by the American Samoa small-scale fishery (see Figure 5.2).

--- SENTENCE 294 ---
Nor is it known whether catches obtained in the recent expansion years of the western Samoa fishery are representative of the long-term average.

--- SENTENCE 295 ---
Most of the other island nations which are neighbors to American Samoa are also developing domestic longline fisheries.

--- SENTENCE 296 ---
In general, the large vessels (> 50 ft) that constitute the US longline fleet are highly mobile.

The dozens of longline vessels that arrived in Hawaii during the late 1980s were from Alaska, California, the Gulf of Mexico and the East Coast (Travis 1999).

--- SENTENCE 298 ---
Some of these vessels later returned to the continental US or moved on to new fishing areas such as the waters around Fiji.

It is likely that some of the longline vessels that remained in Hawaii are currently seeking alternative fishing grounds because the area around the Hawaiian Islands in which longline vessels are allowed to operate is becoming increasingly restricted.

--- SENTENCE 301 ---
This area encompasses some of the principal fishing grounds for Hawaii-based longline vessels targeting swordfish.

--- SENTENCE 302 ---
A court order issued in August 2000 continued the closed area and added other restrictions on longline operations that have crippled Hawaii's swordfish longline fishery.

--- SENTENCE 303 ---
As many as 40 longline vessels have been displaced and some may look to American Samoa as a potential new home port.

--- SENTENCE 304 ---
In addition, quotas, limited access programs, commercial trip limits, incidental catch restrictions, prohibitions on sale, minimum size limits and time and area closures are among the array of measures that have been implemented or are being discussed by federal and state entities to manage pelagic stocks in the Atlantic and Gulf of Mexico.

--- SENTENCE 305 ---

--- SENTENCE 308 ---
During El Nino events, however, these vessels are known to shift their fishing activity to the equatorial central Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 309 ---
The percentage of yellowfin tuna in their catches often increases with this shift.

--- SENTENCE 310 ---
The purse seine fleet does not catch albacore, so there is no interaction with American Samoa's small-scale longline fishery, whose main target is albacore.

--- SENTENCE 311 ---
Yellowfin tuna comprise a minor portion of the present longline catch but this species could become a more desirable target in the future.

--- SENTENCE 312 ---
There is not yet any compelling evidence that the increased purse seine catches of large and small yellowfin have had any significant effect on yellowfin longline catch rates on a broad scale.

## 8.2.1.1 Alternative No. 1 (50-nm) – Preferred

At a meeting on June 14, 2000, the Council selected Alternative No. 1 as the preferred framework measure.

--- SENTENCE 328 ---
This alternative would close an area extending approximately 50 nm offshore from all islands of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 329 ---
A 50-nm closure would encompass all of the areas where the small-scale fleet presently fishes and all of the known banks and seamounts which are likely to aggregate tuna, thus providing for small-scale fishery expansion.

--- SENTENCE 330 ---
Approximately one-third of the EEZ would be closed to pelagic fishing by large vessels (> 50 ft).

--- SENTENCE 331 ---
Existing or potential interactions between large and small-scale vessels fishing for pelagic species in the EEZ around American Samoa cannot be quantified with available information.

--- SENTENCE 332 ---
In general, pelagic fisheries interactions are difficult to document and model because of inadequate data, insufficient knowledge of the biology and population dynamics of the resource and poor understanding of environmental influences (Shomura et al. 1994; Shomura 1996).

--- SENTENCE 333 ---
Migratory routes of specific fractions of stocks are of major importance in determining the availability of pelagic fish in localized areas fished by small-scale fleets.

--- SENTENCE 334 ---
Specific routes are not scientifically documented but tuna fishermen in some islands of the Pacific have learned to "read" local tuna dynamics after many years of careful observation (Kaneko et al. 2000).

--- SENTENCE 335 ---
Areas closed to large fishing vessels compensate for uncertainty about pelagic fish resource abundance and dynamics because they limit pelagic fish mortality while improving fishery data.

--- SENTENCE 336 ---
New information will be acquired by small-scale fishermen as they venture farther from offshore and discover new grounds.

--- SENTENCE 337 ---
Entry into the fishery by large vessels represents a much greater increase in fishing effort and potential fishing mortality than the addition of small-scale boats.

--- SENTENCE 338 ---
A typical 50+ ft longliner in neighboring Samoa, for example, sets 1,200-1,600 hooks per day, compared to 250-900 hooks set by small-scale longline vessels (< 50 ft) (Mulipola 2000).

--- SENTENCE 339 ---
In the Pacific basin, the establishment of area closures is increasingly becoming the preferred management tool to resolve conflicts associated with competition among fisheries harvesting the same local populations of pelagic fish resources.

--- SENTENCE 340 ---
The government of neighboring Samoa has implemented regulations prohibiting longline fishing by vessels larger than 15 m within 50 nm of shore (Mulipola 2000).

--- SENTENCE 341 ---
The Marshall Islands established a 50-nm longline exclusion zone around the atolls of Majuro and Kwajalein in 1996, after sportsfishermen contended that trolling catch rates for game fish species such as blue marlin and yellowfin tuna had declined as a result of fishing by the locally-based longline fleet (Bigelow and Lewis undated).

--- SENTENCE 342 ---
There is evidence from other pelagic fisheries that intensive fishing effort within core areas can reduce catch per unit of effort (CPUE) on a localized scale.

--- SENTENCE 343 ---
Such an effect was observed on the Pacific coast of Mexico, where an increase of longline fishing effort led to marked overall decreases of CPUE in both longline and troll fisheries (Muhlia-Melo 1996).

--- SENTENCE 344 ---
After Mexico established a sport fishery preserve which extended 50 nm offshore along the Pacific coast, Squire and Au (1990) noted that an improvement in striped marlin catch rates, which reflected the fishing down and rebuilding of two localized near shore areas where fish are attracted and regularly linger during their life cycle.

--- SENTENCE 345 ---
In 1987, Mexico extended the area closed to longline fishing farther offshore (Muhlia-Melo 1996).

--- SENTENCE 346 ---
A 50-nm closed area may not be sufficient to encompass the natural variations in local tuna movement patterns or to encompass undiscovered seamounts where tuna aggregate in the EEZ of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 347 ---
Albacore tuna concentrations have shifted farther offshore since late 1998, according to the owners of larger, mobile longline vessels based in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 348 ---
The preferred alternative considers the newly expanded fishing range of new and safer vessels entering American Samoa's small-scale fleet and provides sufficient buffer area to encourage controlled expansion of the small-scale fishery by the newer alia catamaran vessels.

--- SENTENCE 349 ---
However, the alia design, even in newer versions, does not provide sufficient capacity to store and chill large fresh fish catches and long-range fishing beyond 50 nm from shore is not economically efficient.

--- SENTENCE 350 ---
If a significant number of monohull vessels with larger carrying capacity and longer range join the small-scale fleet in the future, a 50-nm closure may not be sufficient to prevent interactions with large-scale vessels (> 50 ft).

--- SENTENCE 351 ---
During ENSO events, US purse seine fishing activity shifts toward the central equatorial Pacific, with sets made rarely in the portion of American Samoa's EEZ near Swains Island.

--- SENTENCE 352 ---
Considering that negative impacts on small-scale tuna fisheries by purse seine fishing are much more likely within 60 nm of shore (Hampton et al. 1996), inclusion of Swains Island in a uniform 50-nm area closure would avoid the potential for yellowfin tuna catch competition with the purse seine fishery.

--- SENTENCE 361 ---

# 8.2.1.5 Alternative No. 5 (no action)

Uncontrolled expansion of the pelagic fishery in the EEZ of American Samoa is possible with no action.

--- SENTENCE 372 ---
Highly capitalized, mechanized vessels with greater fishing power could fish within the range of the small-scale fishery.

--- SENTENCE 373 ---
An Asian longline fishery operated near American Samoa from the mid-1950s through the 1970s, until the Magnuson Act was implemented in 1977.

--- SENTENCE 374 ---
The history of that fishery demonstrates the potential for a decline in tuna catch rates in conjunction with increasing fishing effort by large longline vessels.

--- SENTENCE 375 ---
Albacore catch rates in the waters around American Samoa declined as the Asian longline fleet expanded rapidly in the 1950s.

--- SENTENCE 376 ---
Analysis of this fishery from the 1950s to the 1970s by Otsu and Sumida (1968) and Yoshida (1975) indicates that the large increase in longline fishing effort may have had an effect on the South Pacific albacore stock.

--- SENTENCE 377 ---
The average catch per day and catch per 1000 hooks (CPUE) of Asian longline vessels based in Pago Pago declined steadily between 1959 and 1971.

--- SENTENCE 378 ---
That the apparent effect was not greater was due to expansion of the fishery into areas south of 20°S, where better catch rates were obtained.

--- SENTENCE 379 ---
Little data specific to American Samoa's EEZ are available from the earliest period of the fishery (1954-1959), when Asian longliners were still learning the albacore tuna grounds and confined their fishing largely to the vicinity of the Samoa islands.

--- SENTENCE 380 ---
It is difficult to estimate the level of longline fishing effort in nearshore areas around American Samoa prior to 1959 but it no doubt exceeded the 1971-1977 average effort of 1.5 million hooks in American Samoa's EEZ (Yong and Wetherall 1980).

--- SENTENCE 381 ---
The South Pacific longline fishery for albacore set an estimated 15-20 million hooks in 1963-1964 (Yoshida 1975), so it would not be surprising if the fishing effort in the EEZ of American Samoa during the late 1950s exceeded 5 million hooks per year.

--- SENTENCE 382 ---
Increased effort was enough to cause a decline from 5 fish/100 hooks to 3 fish/hooks in the older grounds north of 20°S during the period 1966-1971 (Yoshida 1975).

--- SENTENCE 383 ---
American Samoa's domestic pelagic fishery is constrained by small boats with a limited fishing range.

--- SENTENCE 384 ---
Most island nations which are neighbors to American Samoa have, or are developing, domestic longline fisheries which compete for pelagic fish resources available within the region.

--- SENTENCE 385 ---
Neighboring Samoa has the smallest EEZ of any Pacific island nation and the catch of albacore per unit area in that country's rapidly expanding domestic longline fishery is among the highest in the Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 386 ---
Catch competition which already exists would be exacerbated if there is uncontrolled expansion of pelagic fishing by large domestic vessels with greater fishing power in the same areas fished by the American Samoa's small-scale fleet.

--- SENTENCE 387 ---
Even short-term reductions in pelagic fish densities and catch rates could jeopardize the economic viability of the small-scale fleet.

--- SENTENCE 388 ---
Thus, no action has the potential for greater negative impacts on pelagic fishery resources than the other alternatives.

--- SENTENCE 393 ---
The area closures proposed in the preferred Alternative No. 1 (50-nm) encourages expansion of the existing small-scale pelagic fishery, thereby shifting some fishing effort away from inshore resources.

--- SENTENCE 394 ---
Alternative No. 2 (50/30-nm closures) would have marginally less beneficial impact because it provides a smaller buffer zone for pelagic fishery expansion around Swains Island.

--- SENTENCE 395 ---
Alternative No. 3 (100-nm closures) would be more positive because of the large buffer zone established for expansion of the small-scale pelagic fishery.

--- SENTENCE 396 ---
Alternative No. 4 (foreign longline closures) is only slightly better than no action in encouraging fishing pressure to shift offshore.

# 8.2.3 Impacts on bycatch

Most of the non-tuna fish taken by longline fisheries in the US Pacific islands are secondary or incidental species, rather than bycatch, because few are discarded.

--- SENTENCE 398 ---
In 1997, NMFS logbook data for domestic longline vessels based in American Samoa indicated that discards amounted to 4.5 percent of the total catch of large (>50 ft) longline vessels, while 0.2 percent of the total catch of small vessels using longline gear was discarded.

--- SENTENCE 399 ---
Longsnouted lancet fish, *Alepisaurus ferox*, is one of the most common species caught on longline gear by fishermen in neighboring Samoa and is always discarded (Mulipola 2000).

--- SENTENCE 400 ---
Most of the catch of large longline boats that has no market value, including small skipjack and yellow tuna, may be discarded, particularly where fish storage capacity or ice is limited (Mulipola 2000)

--- SENTENCE 401 ---
but much of the unmarketable portion of the catch is taken home by the crew for personal consumption.

--- SENTENCE 402 ---
For example, marlin is often cut up and distributed to the crew before the boat returns to port.

--- SENTENCE 403 ---
Some sharks are finned at sea and the carcasses discarded (Mulipola 2000).

--- SENTENCE 404 ---
In 1999, 90 percent of the fishing effort by the western Pacific US purse seine fishery was around untethered fish aggregation devices (i.e., rafts known as payao) (Coan et al. 2000).

--- SENTENCE 405 ---
With increased deployment of payao, there could be undesirable effects, such as discarding of dead undersized tunas and bycatch species.

--- SENTENCE 406 ---
The shift towards payao operations has affected the US purse seine fleet's catch in three ways.

--- SENTENCE 407 ---
First, the average size of tuna caught tend to be smaller in floating object sets than in free-swimming school sets.

--- SENTENCE 408 ---
A higher percentage of tuna that is caught in floating object sets is undersized for US canning and, hence, is discarded.

--- SENTENCE 409 ---
Second, floating object sets tend to contain a higher proportion of bigeye tuna than free-swimming school sets.

--- SENTENCE 410 ---
Third, because floating objects tend to aggregate a large number of pelagic species than tuna, they produce more bycatch than free-swimming school sets (Coan et al. 1999).

--- SENTENCE 411 ---
The Forum Fishery Agency (FFA) administers an observer program covering a minimum of 20 percent of the fishing trips by US purse seiners for sampling per year.

--- SENTENCE 412 ---
FFA observers collected bycatch and discard information from 616 sets made in 1998.

--- SENTENCE 413 ---
The data have not been fully reviewed for accuracy and some data appear to be inconsistent with logbook information.

--- SENTENCE 414 ---
Nevertheless, FFA observer data for 1998 indicate almost a 25-fold difference in the bycatch rate between floating object sets (1.59 mt of bycatch per set) and free-swimming school sets (0.06 mt of bycatch per set).

--- SENTENCE 415 ---
Rainbow runner, oceanic triggerfish, sharks, mahimahi, wahoo, mackerel scad, mackerel and marlins were the most frequently taken bycatch species, according to FFA observers.

--- SENTENCE 416 ---
Over 90 percent (by number) of the rainbow runner, triggerfish, mackerel scad and mackerel were discarded.

--- SENTENCE 417 ---
Over 50 percent (by number) of wahoo and marlin were discarded.

--- SENTENCE 418 ---
Only 17 percent of the sharks were discarded as whole fish (Coan et al. 1999) and the remainder were presumably finned.

--- SENTENCE 419 ---
By closing some areas of American Samoa's EEZ to pelagic fishing by large longline vessels and purse seiners, Alternative No. 1 (50-nm) could reduce the potential for bycatch, particularly from the purse seine fishery.

--- SENTENCE 420 ---
Alternative No. 2 (50/30-nm closures) would offer marginally less beneficial impacts, whereas Alternative No. 3 (100-nm closures) has the greatest potential for positive impacts.

--- SENTENCE 421 ---
Alternative No. 4 (foreign longline closures) is not as positive as the others but the smaller closed areas would still be more beneficial than no action, which does not offer any potential for reduction of bycatch in pelagic fisheries in American Samoa's EEZ.

## 8.2.4 Impacts on protected species and wildlife refuge resources

Interactions with protected species are uncommon in American Samoa's small-scale pelagic fishery.

--- SENTENCE 422 ---
One hawksbill turtle and one olive ridley turtle were reported hooked in 1999.

--- SENTENCE 423 ---
Both were released alive.

--- SENTENCE 424 ---
These were the only interactions with any protected species reported for the domestic longline fishery during the 1996-1999 period.

--- SENTENCE 425 ---
Both large-scale and small-scale longline vessels set mainline deep enough (45 fm) to target albacore tuna (Kaneko et al. 2000), thus reducing the potential for interactions with turtles.

--- SENTENCE 426 ---
Although there have been no reported interactions, other protected species occur in the waters around American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 427 ---
In Fagatele Bay National Marine Sanctuary, southern humpback whales mate and calve from June through September.

--- SENTENCE 428 ---
Sperm whales are occasionally seen in the Sanctuary as well.

--- SENTENCE 429 ---
Both species are listed as endangered under the Endangered Species Act (ESA) and the Marine Mammal Protection Act (MMPA).

--- SENTENCE 430 ---
Several species of dolphins protected under the MMPA but not listed as threatened or endangered also frequent the Sanctuary.

--- SENTENCE 431 ---
Twelve species of migratory seabirds reside on Rose Atoll, one of which is the bristle-thighed curlew, which is listed as vulnerable under the ESA.

--- SENTENCE 432 ---
Rose Atoll is a National Wildlife Refuge co-administered by the US Fish and Wildlife Service and American Samoa Department of Marine and Wildlife Resources.

--- SENTENCE 433 ---
Entry to the refuge is prohibited without a special permit so there are few human impacts compared to the inhabited islands of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 434 ---
Thus, Rose Atoll affords protection for nesting sea turtles on the beaches and potential foraging habitat for turtles on reefs and in the lagoon.

--- SENTENCE 435 ---
Like many places, however, the habitat and wildlife resources of the refuge are subject to the destructive effects of vessel groundings.

--- SENTENCE 436 ---
In 1993, for example, the reef at Rose Atoll was damaged by a fuel spill and debris after the grounding and breakup of a large Asian longline vessel.

--- SENTENCE 437 ---
Alternative No. 1 (preferred) would provide a closed area (50 nm) around Rose Atoll to greatly reduce the risk of grounding by large US pelagic fishing vessels.

--- SENTENCE 438 ---
Alternative No. 2 would have the same positive impact.

--- SENTENCE 439 ---
Alternative No. 3 would substantially increase the closed area around Rose Atoll and, for this reason, might be considered a higher level of protection against vessel groundings.

--- SENTENCE 440 ---
Alternative No. 4 does not include a closed area around Rose Atoll, and like no action (Alternative No. 5), it does not provide any protection against the risk of grounding by large US pelagic fishing vessels.

The most productive fishing grounds for purse seiners are far from American Samoa.

The US purse seine fleet's increasing dependence on untethered FAD sets since 1999 introduces another possible impact.

--- SENTENCE 516 ---

--- SENTENCE 517 ---

--- SENTENCE 527 ---

--- SENTENCE 528 ---
Decreasing that portion of the EEZ around the Territory in which foreign boats are allowed to fish is likely to reduce the interest of foreign nations in acquiring access rights.

--- SENTENCE 529 ---
On the other hand, a PIAFA may not be entered into if it is determined by the Governor of the applicable Pacific Insular Area that the agreement will adversely affect the fishing activities of the indigenous people of the Pacific Insular Area.

--- SENTENCE 530 ---

--- SENTENCE 531 ---
Alternatives No. 4 and No. 5 would be the most positive for maintaining the potential for foreign fishing opportunities under a Pacific Insular Area Fishery Agreement (PIAFA).

# 8.3.2 Fishing gear conflict

In American Samoa, the small-scale longline fleet deploys relatively short lengths of mainline and they can usually avoid tangling gear.

--- SENTENCE 533 ---
The larger vessels which are active in the longline fishery usually fish in offshore areas which produce higher catch rates.

--- SENTENCE 534 ---
If additional large vessels enter the fishery and set gear in the same areas as the small-scale fleet, there would be a much greater potential for gear conflicts with the small-boat fleet.

--- SENTENCE 535 ---
Large vessels (> 50 ft) may set 30-50 miles of mainline, compared to 7-25 miles of mainline set by small-scale vessels (< 50 ft) in Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 536 ---
Dramatic expansion of the small-scale longline fishing fleet in western Samoa led to congestion and gear conflict in nearshore areas of concentrated fishing during the dramatic growth period of 1996-1997.

--- SENTENCE 537 ---
To minimize further conflicts, the Samoa government has implemented regulations that prohibit longline fishing by vessels over 15 m within 50 nm of the coast (Chapman 1998).

--- SENTENCE 538 ---
Gear conflict was evident between the small-boat (troll and handline) and longline fleets during the rapid expansion of the Hawaii longline fleet in the late 1980s.

--- SENTENCE 539 ---
The fleet increased from 37 vessels in 1987 to 75 in 1989, and then doubled again to 156 vessels in 1991.

--- SENTENCE 540 ---
Many of the new longline vessels were recent arrivals from the continental USA.

--- SENTENCE 541 ---
In addition to straining harbor facilities, the increased fishing effort led to gear conflicts and precipitated heated confrontations between the longliners and the established local fishing fleet, which consisted mainly of small troll and handline vessels (Pooley 1990).

--- SENTENCE 542 ---
To avoid further gear conflicts, the Council established longline area closures extending 50-75 nmi offshore of the main Hawaiian Islands (MHI).

--- SENTENCE 543 ---
This action occurred at a time when the majority of commercial troll fishing trips by small boats were taken within 20 nmi of the MHI, with a minority of small-scale vessels reporting troll fishing at distances of 40-60 nmi offshore of the MHI (WPRFMC 1991).

--- SENTENCE 544 ---
The preferred alternative (No. 1) would reduce the potential for gear conflict more than no action (Alternative No. 5) and Alternatives No. 2 and 4 based on the percentage of American Samoa's EEZ that would be closed to large vessels.

--- SENTENCE 545 ---
Alternative No. 3 would close a larger percentage of the EEZ to large vessels but *alia* have limited carrying for fish and there is presently little economic incentive to set gear beyond 50 nm from shore.

--- SENTENCE 546 ---
Should a significant number of monohull vessels with larger carrying capacity join the small-scale fleet in the future, however, the range of economically efficient fishing would be extended and this alternative could become beneficial in preventing gear conflicts in the EEZ beyond 50 nm from shore.

--- SENTENCE 547 ---
# 8.3.3 Harvest of underutilized fish resources and opportunity for new entry

The preferred alternative (50-nm closures) would have some negative impact on large longline vessels (> 50 ft) which are being displaced from the swordfish fishery north of the Hawaiian Islands and may look to American Samoa as a possible new home port.

--- SENTENCE 548 ---

--- SENTENCE 549 ---
Therefore, they would incur any new impacts on fishing cost that they have faced while based in Hawaii.

--- SENTENCE 550 ---
Owners of large longline vessels based in American Samoa report that the additional cost of fuel consumption to travel to offshore portions of the EEZ beyond the proposed closed areas is offset by higher catch rates (A.M. Hunkin, pers.

--- SENTENCE 551 ---
Large longline vessels relocating to American Samoa from Hawaii would no doubt have the same experience.

--- SENTENCE 552 ---
Alternatives 2 (50/30-nm) and 4 (foreign longline closures) would not have substantially different impacts on new entry than the preferred alternative (50-nm).

--- SENTENCE 553 ---
Alternative 3 (100-nm), which would extend the area closures to 100-nm offshore, would add to the distance traveled and the fuel consumed by large vessels to reach offshore areas where they would be permitted to fish.

--- SENTENCE 554 ---
The latter alternative would impose somewhat higher costs on large vessels relocating from the Hawaii longline fishery than what they have previously experienced in Hawaii.

--- SENTENCE 555 ---
No action (Alternative No. 5) would not present any economic barriers to new entry and, in fact, could reduce travel and fuel consumption if large vessels chose to fish in nearshore areas of American Samoa's EEZ compared to costs that they have incurred because of nearshore longline area closures in Hawaii.

# 8.3.4 Fairness and equity to fishermen

The allocation of fishing privileges resulting from the preferred alternative is rationally connected to the furtherance of FMP objectives (Section 6).

--- SENTENCE 563 ---
Hence, the sustained participation of the fishing community could be better served by increased local ownership of less costly, small-scale boats that provide greater employment opportunities.

# 8.3.5 Safety of fishermen

Development of small-scale longline fisheries in western and American Samoa was based on an FAO-designed catamaran-style vessel called an *alia*.

--- SENTENCE 570 ---
The original design was for a multipurpose 28 ft fishing boat.

--- SENTENCE 571 ---
The economic success of the fishery encouraged boat builders in western Samoa to produce a larger *alia*.

--- SENTENCE 572 ---
A large number of the latter vessels were "stretched" to 32-34 ft, beyond the size recommended by the FAO project, and they were constructed without consultation from marine surveyors or naval architects.

--- SENTENCE 573 ---
Many of the larger vessels proved incapable of sustaining rough ocean conditions.

--- SENTENCE 574 ---
Consequently, during the dramatic growth years of 1996-1997, the *alia* longline fishery in western Samoa had a poor safety record.

--- SENTENCE 575 ---
This was a period when gear conflicts and congestion in heavily fished nearshore areas forced fishing crews to venture farther from shore (Stanley and Toloa 1998).

--- SENTENCE 576 ---
In the space of 15 months during 1997 and early 1998, at least 14 major accidents occurred, with a loss of 9 vessels and 25 fishermen lost at sea (Chapman 1998).

--- SENTENCE 577 ---
Lack of safety equipment, inexperienced crews, engine failures and poor quality fuels have also contributed to the loss of life and vessels in western Samoa (Fa'asili 1997).

--- SENTENCE 578 ---
One of the recent developments to improve safety standards is the design and construction of new versions of the *alia* which are more seaworthy and are properly equipped with navigational aids, safety and communication equipment.

--- SENTENCE 579 ---
One type of new vessel is a 38-42 ft *alia* assembled from a kit manufactured in Australia.

--- SENTENCE 580 ---
In December 1999, at least 6 fishing enterprises in American Samoa were known to have such vessels under construction.

--- SENTENCE 581 ---
These boats have larger fuel tanks than earlier versions of the *alia* and they are capable of safely extending the range of small-scale fishermen in American Samoa to at least 100 nm from shore (Capt. W. Thompson, letter to WPRFMC dated 3 Feb. 2000).

--- SENTENCE 582 ---
The FAO is designing a 45 ft version of the *alia* for the next phase of longline fishery expansion in western Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 583 ---
When this type of vessel becomes available, it will no doubt be acquired by some small-scale fishing enterprises in American Samoa to extend the range of fishing to 200 nm offshore.

--- SENTENCE 584 ---
The preferred alternative (No. 1) may promote vessel safety by maintaining the potential for good catches of pelagic fish in the closed areas.

--- SENTENCE 585 ---
The 50 ft restriction on vessel size, therefore, is not expected to place the safety of fishery participants at risk.

--- SENTENCE 586 ---
The impacts on safety of Alternatives No. 2 and No. 3 would not be substantially different from that of the preferred alternative.

--- SENTENCE 587 ---
Alternative No. 3 would establish much larger closed areas but this would not necessarily compromise vessel safety.

--- SENTENCE 588 ---
In Hawaii fisheries, troll and handline vessels averaging 40 ft in length regularly make trips of 150 nm or more to seamounts and weather buoys without compromising safety.

--- SENTENCE 589 ---
The vessels that make trips as far as 1000 nm to participate in the federally-regulated Northwestern Hawaiian Islands bottomfish fishery have an average size of 54 ft.

--- SENTENCE 590 ---
Large vessels (> 50 ft) presently fishing or most likely to relocate to American Samoa are seaworthy monohull vessels which can safely fish hundreds to thousands of miles from port, depending on fuel capacity.

--- SENTENCE 591 ---
None of the alternatives, therefore, would affect the safety of large fishing vessels (> 50 ft).

--- SENTENCE 592 ---
Samoa is a major source of vessels for fishing enterprises in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 593 ---
The acquisition of at least 6 new and safer *alia* in the 38-42 ft class by fishermen in American Samoa will allow the small-scale fishery to extend the range of operation to 100 nm offshore without major risk (Capt. W. Thompson, letter to WPRFMC dated 3 Feb. 2000).

--- SENTENCE 594 ---
Vessel length is only one facet of boat seaworthiness.

--- SENTENCE 595 ---
Proper safety training and the acquisition of communication and emergency equipment are also important factors in reducing risk.

--- SENTENCE 596 ---
The US Coast Guard has recently undertaken a campaign to inform fishermen in American Samoa about US commercial fishing vessel safety regulations and to ensure compliance with these regulations.

--- SENTENCE 597 ---
Alternative No. 4 and no action (Alternative No. 5) provide little encouragement to the small-scale fishermen in American Samoa to invest in safer vessels and obtain safety and communications equipment and training.

--- SENTENCE 598 ---
If additional large vessels begin to harvest pelagic fish in the same areas as the small-scale fleet, fish densities and catch rates could decline, inducing small-scale vessels to venture to unsafe distances offshore in order to maintain catch rates.

# 9.0 CONSISTENCY OF PREFERRED ALTERNATIVE (NO. 1) WITH NATIONAL STANDARDS AND PROVISIONS OF THE SUSTAINABLE FISHERIES ACT

This section evaluates the consistency of the preferred alternative with national standards for fishery conservation and management and provisions of the Sustainable Fisheries Act.

## 9.1 National Standards for Fishery Conservation and Management

National Standard 1 -- Conservation and management measures shall prevent overfishing while achieving, on a continuing basis, the optimum yield from each fishery for the United States fishing industry.

--- SENTENCE 638 ---
The FMP defines optimum yield as the amount of each management unit species or species complex that can be harvested by domestic and foreign fishing vessels in the EEZ and adjacent waters to the extent regulated by the FMP without causing "local overfishing" or "economic overfishing" within the EEZ of each island area, and without causing or significantly contributing to "growth overfishing" or "recruitment overfishing" on a stock-wide basis.

--- SENTENCE 639 ---
The preferred alternative will only affect small fractions of the stocks of pelagic fish species but it will promote conservation of pelagic fish in the EEZ around American Samoa because large vessels with greater fishing power would be excluded, thereby limiting the potential increase in total fishing effort and fishing mortality for pelagic species.

--- SENTENCE 640 ---
The Council recognizes that localized overfishing and economic overfishing could occur in the EEZ around American Samoa if the size of the small-boat fleet becomes excessive.

--- SENTENCE 641 ---
Measures to restrict the number of small vessels in the fishery, such as a license limitation system, may be required in the future.

# 10.6 Draft Regulatory Impact Review/Initial Regulatory Flexibility Analysis (RIR/RFA)

--- SENTENCE 764 ---
A complete analysis of the economic impacts of these alternatives is attached as Appendix III (Draft Regulatory Impact Review/Initial Regulatory Flexibility Analysis for proposed framework measure to prohibit fishing for pelagic management unit species within closed areas around American Samoa by vessels more than 50 feet in length).

--- SENTENCE 765 ---
In summary, the direct economic impacts of the above five alternatives ranges are difficult to quantify.

--- SENTENCE 766 ---
The direct benefits of any area closure clearly fall to those vessels are allowed to continue fishing inside, while the costs fall on those which are excluded.

--- SENTENCE 767 ---
The benefits of closing an area around American Samoa to large fishing vessels are not easy to quantify as pelagic fisheries interactions are difficult to document and model due to inadequate data, insufficient knowledge of the biology and population dynamics of the resource and poor understanding of environmental influences.

--- SENTENCE 768 ---
In addition, how various gears fishing in the same time and area compete for locally available fishery resources and the effects on availability of the target fish are poorly understood.

--- SENTENCE 769 ---
As an example of the differences in fishery impacts between the American Samoa based fleet and the types of vessels which might be expected to be excluded by this measure, a typical 65 foot longliner in Hawaii sets1,200-1,500 hooks per day with an annual catch of 250,000 pounds, compared to the 200-500 hooks per day and 35,000 pounds landed per year by a typical American Samoa small-scale longline vessel.

--- SENTENCE 770 ---
The average annual catch of skipjack by a US purse seine vessel operating in the central and western Pacific from 1990 to 1997 was 3,161 metric tons.

--- SENTENCE 771 ---
In addition to the potential for catch competition, gear conflicts are likely if large longliners set their (Hawaii) average of 30-50 miles of mainline within an area that extends only 50 miles offshore.

--- SENTENCE 772 ---
Although predicting likelihood and magnitude of such occurrences is difficult, the intent of this alternative is to reasonably preclude or minimize such negative shocks to the catches of small-scale vessels.

--- SENTENCE 773 ---
The five alternatives under consideration range from a 0% closure (no action) to a 100 nm closure which comprises 77% of American Samoa's EEZ (Alternative 3).

--- SENTENCE 774 ---
The preferred alternative of a 50-nm closure would close one-third of American Samoa's EEZ to pelagic fishing by vessels more than 50 feet in length.

--- SENTENCE 775 ---
This alternative is intended to provide a balanced approach which allows large vessels to continue fishing within two-thirds of the EEZ, while maintaining one-third for the exclusive use of small-scale vessels.

--- SENTENCE 776 ---
These fishing vessels represent very small owner-operated businesses which have limited abilities to withstand even short term revenue interruptions.

--- SENTENCE 777 ---
Small-scale fishery participants have little access to credit advances or other mechanisms to help them weather such an event, and a boom and bust fishing pattern could severely disrupt American Samoa's developing fishery infrastructure.

--- SENTENCE 778 ---
It is believed that the costs associated with this measure can be borne relatively easily by operators of excluded large vessels which are easily able to access alternate fishing areas, while the costs of no action may prove ruinous to American Samoa's small-scale fleet which has a limited range.

--- SENTENCE 779 ---
These measures are also intended to allow for the continuing and controlled development of a profitable, small-scale fishery which is accessible to residents of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 780 ---
These residents have an average annual per capita income of \$5,000 and a median annual household income of \$16,000.

--- SENTENCE 781 ---
With an unemployment rate approaching 17% and few alternate employment opportunities, small-scale fishing ventures represent an important path to sustainable development and economic self-reliance for American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 782 ---
A sustainable fishery also has significant social importance to residents of American Samoa who have historically utilized fishery resources, and whose traditional cultural practices rely heavily on access to these resources.

# APPENDIX I: American Samoa Fishing Community Impact Statement

# I.1 Overview of community

American Samoa is an unincorporated territory of the U.S.A consisting of the islands of Tutuila, Swains and the Manu'a group (Ofu, Olosega and Ta'u) and Rose Atoll.

--- SENTENCE 975 ---
The total land area is 77 square miles.

--- SENTENCE 976 ---
The Territory's population is about 60,000 and is growing rapidly, with a doubling time of only 20 years.

--- SENTENCE 977 ---
Most of the islands are mountainous with limited flat land suitable for agriculture.

--- SENTENCE 978 ---
American Samoa is lowest in gross domestic product and highest in donor aid per capita among the US Pacific islands (Adams et al. 1998).

--- SENTENCE 979 ---
American Samoa has a small developing economy, dependent mainly on two primary income sources: the American Samoa Government (ASG), which receives income and capital subsidies from the United States, and two tuna canneries on the island of Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 980 ---
These two primary income sources have given rise to a third: a services sector that derives from and complements the first two.

--- SENTENCE 981 ---
In 1993, the latest year for which ASG has compiled detailed labor force and employment data, the local government employed 4,355 people (32.2 percent) of total employment, followed by the two canneries with 3,977 people (29.9 percent) and the rest of the services economy with 5,211 workers (38.4 percent).

--- SENTENCE 982 ---
Altogether, the three segments employed 13,543 workers, while 2,718 people were registered as unemployed (that is, actively seeking employment).

--- SENTENCE 983 ---
This gives a total labor force of 16,261 and an unemployment rate of 16.7 percent.

--- SENTENCE 984 ---
With a total population in 1993 estimated at 52,900, the labor force represented 30.7 percent of the population, very low when compared with the overall US labor force ratio (well over 50 percent) but typical of the smaller developing Pacific island economies.

--- SENTENCE 985 ---
Of the 31,822 residents 16 years or older, the total labor force was equivalent to 51.1 percent.

--- SENTENCE 986 ---
That half of the 16 years-plus population is not in the labor force is explained by American Samoa's lack of major industry other than government and fish canning.

--- SENTENCE 987 ---
Work opportunities are certainly limited but not having a job in the money economy does not necessarily equate with unemployment in the territory, where subsistence activity contributes to the extended family's total well-being.

--- SENTENCE 988 ---
Official data notwithstanding, by many measures, American Samoa is not a poor economy.

--- SENTENCE 989 ---
Its estimated per capita income of \$5,000 is almost twice the average for all the Pacific island economies (at \$2,700)

--- SENTENCE 990 ---
(Bank of Hawaii Economic Research Dept. 1999).

--- SENTENCE 991 ---
Per capita income in American Samoa does not represent the same market basket and value as it would, for example, in Honolulu.

--- SENTENCE 992 ---
There are aspects of work and the creation of value in the communal societies of the Pacific islands that are not captured by market measures.

--- SENTENCE 993 ---
For instance, to the extent that unemployment among the younger population can cause both economic and social ills, American Samoa's tightly organized aiga (extended family) system is one way to keep young people from becoming economically unproductive and socially disruptive.

--- SENTENCE 994 ---
Another avenue for American Samoan youth not available to the vast majority of youth in the Pacific islands is emigration to the United States, where an estimated 70,000 Samoans live, 20,000 of them in Hawaii.

--- SENTENCE 995 ---
A large proportion of the territory's workers (in the case of the canneries as much as 90 percent) is from western Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 996 ---
While it is correct to say that western Samoans working in the territory are legally alien workers, in fact, they are the same people, by culture, history and family ties.

--- SENTENCE 997 ---
# I.2 Description of the fisheries

## I.2.1 History of exploitation, vessel characteristics and fleet composition

# I.2.1.1 Small-scale fishery

The harvest of pelagic fish has been a part of the Samoan way of life since the islands were first settled some 3,500 years ago.

--- SENTENCE 998 ---
Until the 1950s on the island of Tutuila, and even into the 1970s in the Manu'a Islands, the indigenous residents of American Samoa captured skipjack tuna in offshore waters using traditional canoes and gear (Severance and Franco 1989).

--- SENTENCE 999 ---
Other tuna species, billfish, wahoo and mahimahi were also occasionally taken by traditional techniques.

--- SENTENCE 1000 ---
The introduction of outboard motors in the 1950s and 1960s brought about a decline in traditional fishing methods in favor of motorized dinghies and skiffs for trolling and handlining.

--- SENTENCE 1001 ---
The development of offshore fisheries began in earnest during the early 1980s.

--- SENTENCE 1002 ---
It was at this time that the FAO-designed alia catamaran was introduced into the islands.

--- SENTENCE 1003 ---
The number of small vessels participating in commercial pelagic and bottomfish fisheries quadrupled between 1980 and 1985.

--- SENTENCE 1004 ---
During the latter period, almost all of the commercial catch of pelagic species was taken by trolling.

--- SENTENCE 1005 ---
Most pelagic fishing occurred near banks and seamounts where seabird flocks feed (thus indicating the presence of baitfish that tuna may also be feeding upon), or at fish aggregating devices (FADs) deployed around Tutuila Island.

--- SENTENCE 1006 ---
FADs were introduced to American Samoan coastal waters in 1979 and proved to be a popular way to increase the catch rates of widely dispersed pelagic fish (Craig et al. 1993).

--- SENTENCE 1007 ---
FADs attracted and retained schools of fish and made it easier for vessels to locate concentrations of tuna.

--- SENTENCE 1008 ---
The extensive use of longline gear by the small-scale fleet in American Samoa is a recent phenomenon, with longline catches rising from zero prior to 1994 to almost 900,000 lbs in 1998.

--- SENTENCE 1009 ---
The stimulus for American Samoan fishermen to shift from troll or handline gear to longline gear was the fishing success of 28-34 ft *alia* catamarans equipped with longline gear operating in the EEZ around western Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1010 ---
Following the example of the western Samoa fleet, the fishermen in American Samoa deploy a short monofilament longline with 250-350 hooks from a hand-powered reel.

--- SENTENCE 1011 ---
The predominant catch is albacore tuna, which is marketed to the local tuna canneries.

--- SENTENCE 1012 ---
Fishermen who set longline gear in the Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) around American Samoa are required to obtain a federal permit from the National Marine Fisheries Service (NMFS) Pacific Islands Area Office.

--- SENTENCE 1013 ---
There is presently no limit on permits (i.e., no limited entry).

--- SENTENCE 1014 ---
To date, over 60 permits have been issued, although only 26 were active on a regular basis in 1999 (Curren 2000).

--- SENTENCE 1015 ---
The technology employed by the small-scale fishing fleet in American Samoa is relatively unsophisticated.

--- SENTENCE 1016 ---
Typically, the boats are double- or single-hulled vessels equipped with outboard engines.

--- SENTENCE 1017 ---
Until recently, average boat length was 28 ft.

--- SENTENCE 1018 ---
Many boats are outfitted with wooden handreels that are used for bottomfish fishing as well as for trolling.

--- SENTENCE 1019 ---
Less than ten percent of the boats carry a depth finder, fish finder or global positioning system (Severance et al. 1998).

--- SENTENCE 1020 ---
The small vessels equipped for longline fishing store their gear on deck on a hand-powered reel, which can hold as much as 10 nautical miles (nm) of monofilament mainline.

--- SENTENCE 1021 ---
Most longliners leave for the fishing grounds in the early morning and return in the afternoon or early evening.

--- SENTENCE 1022 ---
The small *alia* fish up to 25 nm from shore, but effort has been mainly concentrated on banks 5 to 10 nm off the southern coast of Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 1023 ---
Less information is available on the fishing grounds of the small boats using trolling gear.

--- SENTENCE 1024 ---
Moana (1988) noted 12 years ago that small boats were increasingly traveling to distant banks and seamounts such as South Bank and Southeast Bank, both of which are located about 45 nm from land.

--- SENTENCE 1025 ---
This trend of fishing further offshore was also observed in a more recent study of the small-scale pelagic fishery in American Samoa (Severance et al. 1999)

Fishermen in American Samoa are acquiring larger boats (38-50 ft) with a greater fishing range and capacity for chilling fish.

--- SENTENCE 1026 ---
In 1998, a local private, non-profit organization received a grant from the Administration for Native Americans to equip a 40 ft double-hull fishing vessel with hydraulically powered longline gear.

--- SENTENCE 1027 ---
The new boat is being built by a local firm and is expected to be completed early in 2000<sup>1</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 1028 ---
<sup>&</sup>lt;sup>1</sup>Since 1981, boat builders in American Samoa have been constructing plywood and fiberglass alia catamarans for the local fishing industry (Itano 1996).

--- SENTENCE 1029 ---
Western Samoa has recently submitted a request to FAO to design a larger alia.

--- SENTENCE 1030 ---
The new design will also be available to boat-builders in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1031 ---
In addition, a 52 ft steel vessel joined the fleet late in 1999 and six 38-42 ft *alia* are reportedly being purchased by local businessmen.

--- SENTENCE 1032 ---
Most of the *alia* are assembled in western Samoa from pre-cut aluminum plates manufactured in Australia.

--- SENTENCE 1033 ---
This newer version of the *alia* has a higher freeboard and is equipped with a larger fuel tank, navigational aids and proper safety gear and it is capable of extending the range of American Samoa's small-scale fishery to at least 100 nm offshore (Capt. W. Thompson letter to WPRFMC dated 3 Feb. 2000).

--- SENTENCE 1034 ---
The older, "stretched" version of the original *alia* design was constructed in western Samoa without consultation with naval architects or marine surveyors.

--- SENTENCE 1035 ---
It proved to be unseaworthy and is blamed for the high loss of boats and crews during 1996-1997, the years of dramatic expansion in the western Samoa longline fishery (Stanley and Toloa 1998).

--- SENTENCE 1036 ---
Tournament fishing for pelagic species began in American Samoa in the 1980s.

--- SENTENCE 1037 ---
Most of the boats that participate are *alia* catamarans and small skiffs.

--- SENTENCE 1038 ---
Catches from tournaments are often sold, as most of the entrants are local small-scale commercial fishermen.

--- SENTENCE 1039 ---
In 1996, three days of tournament fishing contributed about one percent of the total domestic landings.

--- SENTENCE 1040 ---
Typically, 7 to 14 local boats carrying 55 to 70 fishermen participate in each tournament, which are held 2 to 5 times per year (Craig et al. 1993).

--- SENTENCE 1041 ---
# I.2.1.2 Large-scale distant water fishery

Large-scale commercial longline fishing in what is now the EEZ around American Samoa was initiated by Japanese vessels in the late 1940s.

--- SENTENCE 1042 ---
The foreign vessels later supplied albacore tuna to the two canneries established in the Territory by Van Camp Seafood Company and Star-Kist Foods in 1954 and 1963, respectively.

--- SENTENCE 1043 ---
From 1950 to 1965 there was a progressive expansion of the area of operation of these longliners from the waters in the immediate vicinity of American Samoa to more distant waters (Otsu and Sumida 1968; Yoshida 1975).

--- SENTENCE 1044 ---
The expansion of fishing area paralleled an increase in fleet size.

--- SENTENCE 1045 ---
Between 1954 and 1965 the number of foreign longline vessels off-loading in Pago Pago increased from less than 20 to over 150.

--- SENTENCE 1046 ---
In the mid-1960s, the Japanese vessels began to be replaced by Taiwanese and Korean longline vessels as the canneries' major suppliers of albacore.

--- SENTENCE 1047 ---
In recent years, the number of foreign longline vessels delivering fish to the canneries has sharply declined, and, presently, only about 40 vessels are making landings in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1048 ---
A typical Asian longline vessel is 80-150 ft in length, highly mechanized and sets 50-60 nm of mainline with 1,500-2,000 hooks each day (WPRFMC 1995).

--- SENTENCE 1049 ---
Legal fishing by foreign longline vessels in the waters around American Samoa ceased completely in 1980 after the implementation of the pelagic fisheries Preliminary Management Plan for the Western Pacific Region,<sup>2</sup> which placed onerous requirements (e.g., permits, fees, observers) on foreign vessels.

--- SENTENCE 1050 ---
However, foreign longline vessels occasionally fish illegally in the EEZ around American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1051 ---
In 1992, for example, the Coast Guard seized a Taiwanese longline vessel fishing near Swains Island.

--- SENTENCE 1052 ---
The PMP was superceded by the Council's Pelagic Fisheries Management Plan in 1986.

--- SENTENCE 1053 ---

--- SENTENCE 1054 ---
This agreement could give foreign vessels access to EEZ waters around American Samoa in exchange for a negotiated fee and subject to a variety of permit conditions.

--- SENTENCE 1055 ---
The domestic longline fleet based in American Samoa consists mainly of small vessels but five additional locally-owned vessels larger than 50 ft based in Pago Pago are also engaged in the longline fishery.

--- SENTENCE 1056 ---
These vessels are outfitted with modern electronic equipment for navigation, communications and fish finding and they are able to chill and freeze tuna catches.

--- SENTENCE 1057 ---

--- SENTENCE 1058 ---
The owners of five other large US longline vessels that are not based in American Samoa have received NMFS longline general permits that allow the boats to longline in the EEZ around the Territory, but only one of the owners has used his vessel for this purpose.

US purse seine vessels began exploratory fishing in the central and western Pacific in the late 1970s.

--- SENTENCE 1060 ---

--- SENTENCE 1061 ---

--- SENTENCE 1062 ---

--- SENTENCE 1063 ---

--- SENTENCE 1064 ---

--- SENTENCE 1065 ---

--- SENTENCE 1067 ---

--- SENTENCE 1070 ---

--- SENTENCE 1071 ---

# I.5 Description of social and cultural framework of domestic commercial, recreational and subsistence fishermen and the fishing community

Samoa has a long history of dependence on pelagic fishery resources.

--- SENTENCE 1192 ---
The narrow reef shelf around the main islands of American Samoa and the lack of shallow productive lagoon waters limit potential inshore fishery yields.

--- SENTENCE 1193 ---
Severance and Franco (1989) and Severance et al. (1999) documented the traditional importance of capturing large pelagic fish, particularly skipjack tuna, and described the technology and skills developed by Samoans to catch these fish.

--- SENTENCE 1194 ---
This included special canoes (va'a alo) designed for lightness and speed which could follow tuna schools, and tuna hooks made from mother-of-pearl and turtle shell.

--- SENTENCE 1195 ---
In the past, fishermen in canoes might fish as far as 30 miles from shore when following tuna schools.

--- SENTENCE 1196 ---
Other tunas, billfish, wahoo and mahimahi were occasionally caught with baited lines and trolling gear.

--- SENTENCE 1197 ---
The methods and equipment for catching skipjack and other pelagic species have evolved and island residents are no longer entirely dependent on local fishing for food.

--- SENTENCE 1198 ---
In contemporary Samoa, seafood continues to be a major component of the local diet.

--- SENTENCE 1199 ---
There has been no recent attempt to formally assess the subsistence fishing contribution to American Samoa<sup>4</sup> but subsistence fishing is known to be an important supplement to cash income in many communities in the Territory (Severance et al. 1999).

--- SENTENCE 1200 ---
<sup>&</sup>lt;sup>4</sup> Wass (1980) reported that annual per capita consumption of seafood in American Samoa is 148 lbs, which is several times higher than the US national average.

--- SENTENCE 1201 ---
In addition, fishing continues to contribute to the perpetuation of Samoan culture, which involves exchange of food and other resources to support extended families and traditional leaders.

--- SENTENCE 1202 ---
Participation in commercial activities, wage labor and a cash economy has not weakened these obligations so much as it has allowed new opportunities for customary exchange of goods and services, both formally and informally, through kinship and friendship networks.

--- SENTENCE 1203 ---
Individual Samoans participate as members of extended families or aiga that share resources and responsibilities.

--- SENTENCE 1204 ---
Each aiga is headed by a titled "chief" or matai who is the decision-maker and spokesperson for the family in many matters of village life.

--- SENTENCE 1205 ---
Untitled men and women of the village have many obligations for service and are expected to contribute goods (including fish), cash and labor to important village ceremonies ranging from holidays to weddings and title investitures.

--- SENTENCE 1206 ---
Traditional Samoan values still exert a strong influence on when and why people fish, how they distribute their catch and the meaning of fish within the society.

--- SENTENCE 1207 ---
When distributed, fish and other resources move through a complex and culturally embedded exchange system that supports the food needs of 'aiga, as well as the status of both matai and village ministers (Severance, et al., unpubl.

--- SENTENCE 1208 ---
Customary exchanges include:

<u>Fa'alavelave</u> --

--- SENTENCE 1209 ---
As a noun, mutual assistance to kinsmen in times of need; as a verb, to provide assistance in times of need.

--- SENTENCE 1210 ---
This assistance can be in the form of food from the land or sea, or money derived from local or overseas labor markets.

--- SENTENCE 1211 ---
<u>Tautua</u> – As a noun, service to the kin group and to the <u>matai</u> as leader of the kin group; as a verb, to serve the kin group and its <u>matai</u>.

<u>Fesoasoani</u> – To help out; a less formalized, more individualized, response to a less serious need than in the case of fa`alayelaye.

--- SENTENCE 1212 ---
<u>To`onai</u> – A ceremonial need served after Sunday service, where ministers, matai, other village leaders and important visitors to the village reaffirm cultural and spiritual solidarity.

--- SENTENCE 1213 ---
<u>Fa'ataualofa</u> -- To give away or sell at a reduced price to friends or kin as an expression of an ongoing, sustained relationship.

--- SENTENCE 1214 ---
Commercial fishermen are expected to fish when village ceremonies are pending and to be generous in sharing their catch.

--- SENTENCE 1215 ---
Some keep fish in freezers with the expectation that they may be called upon by their *matai* to provide food for cultural purposes.

--- SENTENCE 1216 ---
Reef fish and bottomfish are acceptable offerings but yellowfin and skipjack tuna are preferred.

--- SENTENCE 1217 ---
At times, tuna are ceremonially cut up for formal presentation to the *matai* and village pastor (Severance and Franco 1989).

--- SENTENCE 1218 ---
Severance et al. (1999) recently conducted a survey of fishermen in American Samoa who fish for pelagic species.

--- SENTENCE 1219 ---
The 60 fishermen interviewed in 26 villages represent about 50% of the total number of fishermen in the Territory who fish for pelagic species.

--- SENTENCE 1220 ---
Thirty-five percent of the fishermen surveyed reported that they sell less than half of their catch.

--- SENTENCE 1221 ---
Forty percent of these fishermen also reported that half or more of the catch that they sold was done so as fa 'ataulofa, that is, sold at a reduced price to friends or kinsmen as an expression of a sustained social relationship.

--- SENTENCE 1222 ---
The survey examined the cultural importance of the distribution of the unsold portion of the catch.

--- SENTENCE 1223 ---
The average number of times during the past year that individual fishermen contributed fish to Sunday village meetings was 22.

--- SENTENCE 1224 ---
Nineteen percent of the fishermen surveyed reported that half or more of their catch was contributed to a matai as a form of tautua, that is, service to the kin group.

--- SENTENCE 1225 ---
This service is expected of untitled men if they are to rise in status and perhaps achieve a matai title themselves.

--- SENTENCE 1226 ---
Twenty-five percent of the fishermen surveyed already hold matai titles, but they may be obligated to contribute fish to the village pastor or to a higher-ranked individual.

--- SENTENCE 1227 ---
Another form of obligatory contribution takes the form of assistance to kinsmen in times of need known as fa'alavelave.

--- SENTENCE 1228 ---
Forty-two percent of the fishermen surveyed reported contributing fish as fa'alavelave three or more times during the past year.

--- SENTENCE 1229 ---
A more individualized way of assisting kinsmen is referred to as fesoasoani.

--- SENTENCE 1230 ---
Thirty-two percent of the fishermen stated that half or more of the unsold portion of their catch was offered as fesoasoani.

--- SENTENCE 1231 ---
In summary, despite increasing commercialization of the catch, fishing continues to contribute to the perpetuation of Samoa culture and social cohesion of American Samoa communities.

--- SENTENCE 1232 ---
The dependence of the early Samoans on fishing for food security shaped their social organization, cultural values and religion.

--- SENTENCE 1233 ---
Of course, many aspects of Samoan culture have evolved but fishing remains an important cultural practice for many villages.

--- SENTENCE 1234 ---
The role of fishing in cultural continuity is at least as important as the contributions made to the nutritional or economic well-being of island residents.

## I.6 Social impacts of proposed action

Pelagic fish landings by the small-scale fleet are important to American Samoa as a source of food for local consumption, for local income and employment and a means of preserving and perpetuating Samoan cultural values.

--- SENTENCE 1235 ---
The preferred alternative enhances the economic and social values associated with the pelagic fishery by maintaining the potential for economically viable catch rates of pelagic fish by the small-scale fleet, thereby furthering sustained community participation in the fishery.

--- SENTENCE 1236 ---
The allocation of a portion of the EEZ to small vessels is consistent with the FMP objective to promote, within the limits of managing at optimum yield, domestic harvest of the management unit species in the Western Pacific EEZ and domestic fishery values associated with these species by enhancing the opportunities for a) satisfying recreational fishing experiences; b) continuation of traditional fishing practices for non-market personal consumption and cultural benefits; and c) domestic commercial fishermen to engage in profitable fishing operations.

--- SENTENCE 1237 ---
The area closures will provide for sustained community participation in the pelagic fishery without significantly decreasing the catches of large vessels which target pelagic fish.

--- SENTENCE 1238 ---
Taking no action could lead to reduced pelagic fish densities and catch rates within the fishing range of the small-scale fleet.

--- SENTENCE 1239 ---
Economic and social costs would be severe for the small-scale fishery, which does not have the option of large pelagic vessels to travel farther from port to obtain higher catch rates.

--- SENTENCE 1240 ---
The establishment of closed areas ensures that fishing grounds of traditional importance to the small-scale fishing fleet will be reserved for its continued use.

--- SENTENCE 1241 ---
This is consistent with Article 6.18 of the FAO Code of Conduct for Responsible Fisheries:

Recognizing the important contributions of artisanal and small-scale fisheries to employment, income and food security, States should appropriately protect the rights of fishers and fish-workers, particularly those engaged in subsistence, small-scale and artisanal fisheries, to a secure and just livelihood, as well as preferential access, where appropriate, to traditional fishing grounds and resources in the waters under their national jurisdiction.

--- SENTENCE 1242 ---
Furthermore, partitioning large and small fishing vessels into appropriate areas to reduce the likelihood of interaction is consistent with the objective of the pelagic fisheries FMP to diminish gear conflicts in the EEZ, particularly in areas of concentrated domestic fishing, and Article 7.6.5 of the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) Code of Conduct for Responsible Fisheries:

States and fisheries management organizations and arrangements should regulate fishing in such a way as to avoid the risk of conflict among fishers using different vessels, gear and fishing methods.

--- SENTENCE 1243 ---
The area closures are also consistent with the policy of the American Samoa Government, as expressed in the Revised Constitution (1966) "...to protect persons of Samoan ancestry against...the destruction of the Samoan way of life....(and) to protect the lands, customs, culture, and traditional Samoan family organization of persons of Samoan ancestry, and to encourage business enterprises by such persons...."

II.3.1.3.1 Potential fishery induced changes

The future condition of the component PMUS stocks occurring in the EEZ around American Samoa will be affected by changes in the size and composition of pelagic fishing fleets operating both within and outside the EEZ around the Territory.

--- SENTENCE 1308 ---
Predicting what these changes may be is difficult.

--- SENTENCE 1309 ---
The source of uncertainty which could have the greatest impact on the pelagic fisheries within the Council's jurisdiction is the international arrangement currently being negotiated to manage highly migratory fish stocks in the central and western Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 1310 ---
Participants in the negotiations recognize that in order for a multilateral arrangement to achieve its objective of conserving and managing stocks, it will need to agree on catch limitations, either directly, through quotas, or indirectly through a limit on fishing effort.

--- SENTENCE 1311 ---
Decisions have not yet been made regarding the approach to catch and/or effort limitation and where, when and by whom the fish will be caught.

--- SENTENCE 1312 ---
During 1999, 90 percent of western Pacific US purse seine fishing effort was around untethered FADs (i.e. rafts known as payao).

--- SENTENCE 1313 ---
With increased deployment of FADs by the US fleet, there is an increased risk that the migratory behavior of tuna in the western Pacific might be affected.

--- SENTENCE 1314 ---
FADs might retain tuna in areas where they would otherwise quickly pass through, and not be enticed by concentrated forage to remain.

--- SENTENCE 1315 ---
This could affect their biological parameters (growth, maturity, survival) and population dynamics.

--- SENTENCE 1316 ---
These potential effects on the population biology of tunas and on their ecosystem are currently largely unknown and require research attention (Sakagawa in press).

## II.3.1.3.2 Potential environmental influences

Environmental variables have a considerable influence on the abundance and condition of pelagic fish stocks.

--- SENTENCE 1318 ---
The three tropical tunas, skipjack, yellowfin and bigeye, and billfish such as blue and striped marlin prefer waters ranging in temperature from 18-31° C, whereas subtropical fish such as albacore and swordfish prefer cooler waters ranging from 10-25° C. Abundance of these tropical and sub-tropical stocks is related to the abundance of prey items, which in turn may be the result of a physical structure such as a seamount, or an oceanographic feature such as a frontal system where two different water masses converge.

--- SENTENCE 1319 ---
The Samoa islands are an area of modest productivity relative to areas to the north and west.

--- SENTENCE 1320 ---
The region is traversed by two main currents – the southern branch of the westward-flowing South Equatorial Current during June-October and the eastward-flowing South Equatorial Counter Current during November-April.

--- SENTENCE 1321 ---
Surface temperatures vary between 27-29° C and are highest in the January-April period.

--- SENTENCE 1322 ---
The upper limit of the thermocline in ocean areas is relatively shallow (27°C isotherm at 100m depth) but the thermocline itself is diffuse (lower boundary at 300m depth).

--- SENTENCE 1323 ---
Catch/effort data collected from longline fisheries in American Samoa and Samoa suggest that the regional abundance of albacore tuna, which has been the principal target of small-scale longline fisheries in the Samoa islands, is seasonal, although the peak and low seasons are not extreme.

--- SENTENCE 1324 ---
The largest and strongest environmental influence on pelagic stocks in the western Pacific are *El Niño*-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) events (negative values of the Southern Oscillation Index<sup>1</sup>).

--- SENTENCE 1325 ---
ENSO events are associated with a weakening of the prevalent easterly trade winds in the tropical Pacific and an eastward shift of the western Pacific warm pool, the warm water mass that lies between New Guinea and the Micronesian islands.

--- SENTENCE 1326 ---
The eastward displacement of the warm pool during an ENSO event results in a greater abundance of skipjack and yellowfin tuna in the central Pacific (SPC 1997; Lehodey et al. 1997).

--- SENTENCE 1327 ---
Further, ENSO events appear to have a negative impact on recruitment of South Pacific albacore, with poor recruitment following albacore spawning during an ENSO event, and good recruitment following spawnings during La Niña periods when the Southern Oscillation Index is strongly positive (SPC 1997).

--- SENTENCE 1328 ---
A long-term shift in the physical environment of the equatorial Pacific Ocean began in 1977 (Miller et al. 1994).

--- SENTENCE 1329 ---
Conditions included more clouds, more rainfall, warmer sea surface temperatures and weaker trade winds, similar to a weak decadal el Nino state.

--- SENTENCE 1330 ---
They were most pronounced in the central equatorial Pacific, so American Samoa was close to the center of this shift, which persisted until 1999, when conditions were very different.

--- SENTENCE 1331 ---
Whether 1999 marks another regime shift will not be known for several years (J. Polovina, pers.

--- SENTENCE 1332 ---
The Southern Oscillation Index is the difference between the barometric pressure recorded in Northern Australia and French Polynesia.

--- SENTENCE 1333 ---
Normally, the pressure differential leads to prevailing easterly trade winds.

--- SENTENCE 1334 ---
During an *El Niño* the pressure gradient reverses, the trade winds fail and warm ocean water in the western Pacific spreads eastwards into the central and eastern Pacific (Philander 1983).

## II.3.2 Economic and social environment

American Samoa is an unincorporated Territory of the US consisting of the islands of Tutuila, Swain's and the Manu'a group (Ofu, Olosega and Ta'u) and Rose Atoll.

--- SENTENCE 1335 ---
The total land area is 77 square miles.

--- SENTENCE 1336 ---
The Territory's population is about 60,000 and is growing rapidly, with a doubling time of only 20 years.

--- SENTENCE 1337 ---
Most of the islands are mountainous with limited flat land suitable for agriculture.

--- SENTENCE 1338 ---
American Samoa is lowest in gross domestic product and highest in donor aid per capita among the US Pacific islands (Adams et al. 1998).

--- SENTENCE 1339 ---
American Samoa has a small developing economy, dependent mainly on two primary income sources: the American Samoa Government (ASG), which receives income and capital subsidies from the United States, and two tuna canneries on the island of Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 1340 ---
These two primary income sources have given rise to a third: a services sector that derives from and complements the first two.

--- SENTENCE 1341 ---
In 1993, the latest year for which ASG has compiled detailed labor force and employment data, the local government employed 4,355 people (32.2 percent) of total employment), followed by the two canneries with 3,977 people (29.9 percent) and the rest of the services economy with 5,211 workers (38.4 percent) of total employment.

--- SENTENCE 1342 ---
Altogether, the three segments employed 13,543 workers, while 2,718 people were registered as unemployed (that is, actively seeking employment).

--- SENTENCE 1343 ---
This gives a total labor force of 16,261 and an unemployment rate of 16.7 percent, certainly out of line with unemployment rates in the states but not unusual when compared with unemployment rates throughout the Pacific islands.

--- SENTENCE 1344 ---
With a total population in 1993 estimated at 52,900, the labor force represented 30.7 percent of the population, very low when compared with the US labor force ratio (well over 50 percent of total population) but typical of the smaller developing Pacific island economies.

--- SENTENCE 1345 ---
Of the 31,822 residents 16 years or older, the total labor force was equivalent to 51.1 percent.

--- SENTENCE 1346 ---
That half of the 16 years-plus population is unemployed is explained by American Samoa's lack of major industry other than government and fish canning.

--- SENTENCE 1347 ---
Work opportunities are certainly limited but not having a job in the money economy does not necessarily equate with unemployment in the territory, where subsistence activity contributes to the extended family's total well-being.

--- SENTENCE 1348 ---
Official data notwithstanding, by many measures, American Samoa is not a poor economy.

--- SENTENCE 1349 ---
Its estimated per capita income of \$5,000 is almost twice the average for all the Pacific island economies (at \$2,700)

--- SENTENCE 1350 ---
(Bank of Hawaii Economic Research Dept. 1999).

--- SENTENCE 1351 ---
Per capita income in American Samoa does not represent the same market basket and value as it would, for example, in Honolulu.

--- SENTENCE 1352 ---
There are aspects of work and the creation of value in the communal societies of the Pacific islands that are not captured by market measures.

--- SENTENCE 1353 ---
For instance, to the extent that unemployment among the younger population can cause both economic and social ills, American Samoa's tightly organized aiga (extended family) system is one way to keep young people from becoming economically unproductive and socially disruptive.

--- SENTENCE 1354 ---
Another avenue for American Samoan youth not available to the vast majority of youth in the Pacific islands is emigration to the states, where as estimated 70,000 Samoans live, 20,000 of them in Hawaii.

--- SENTENCE 1355 ---
A large proportion of the territory's work force (in the case of the canneries as much as 90 percent) is from western Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1356 ---
While it is correct to say that western Samoans working in the territory are legally alien workers, in fact, they are the same people, by culture, history and by family ties.

--- SENTENCE 1357 ---
American Samoa has a long history of dependence on pelagic fishery resources.

--- SENTENCE 1358 ---
The narrow reef shelf around the islands and the lack of shallow productive lagoon waters limit potential inshore fishery yields.

--- SENTENCE 1359 ---
The methods and equipment for catching skipjack and other pelagic species have evolved and island residents are no longer entirely dependent on local fishing for food.

--- SENTENCE 1360 ---
In contemporary Samoa, seafood continues to be a major component of the local diet (Severance et al. 1999).

--- SENTENCE 1361 ---
Encouraging domestic harvest of offshore pelagic fishery resources is highly compatible with existing economic activities.

--- SENTENCE 1362 ---
A fish processing industry developed in American Samoa in the 1950s and 1960s with the establishment of two tuna cannery operations.

--- SENTENCE 1363 ---
Since that time, the canneries have been the largest private sector employer in American Samoa and its leading exporter.

--- SENTENCE 1364 ---
Very little of the raw fish processed by the canneries is landed by the domestic fleet.

--- SENTENCE 1365 ---
Most is supplied by distant-water purse seine, longline and albacore troll fleets.

--- SENTENCE 1366 ---
In 1998, the canneries at Pago Pago received 208,300 tons of fish worth approximately \$232 million, making it the leading port in the USA in terms of the dollar value of fish landings.

--- SENTENCE 1367 ---
Ancillary businesses associated with the tuna processing industry, including those involved in re-provisioning the tuna fleet, also contribute significantly to America Samoa's economy.

--- SENTENCE 1368 ---

--- SENTENCE 1369 ---
The American Samoa Government calculates that the canneries represent directly and indirectly about 15 percent of current money wages, 10-12 percent aggregate household incomes, 7 percent in local government receipts and 20 percent of power sales.

--- SENTENCE 1370 ---
As in most other Pacific islands, stocks of pelagic species in the vicinity of American Samoa offer far greater resource potential than deep slope bottomfish or inshore fish stocks.

--- SENTENCE 1371 ---
Inshore resources are heavily exploited or over-exploited in most areas of American Samoa (Wass 1980; Saucerman 1995).

--- SENTENCE 1372 ---
The exploitation of slow growing, deep slope snappers in American Samoa is limited by suitable habitat and the low standing stock of the resource (Itano 1996).

--- SENTENCE 1373 ---
Pelagic fisheries are viewed by the American Samoa Government as having an important role in the expansion and diversification of the local economy and in helping the Territory attain a higher level of economic self-reliance.

--- SENTENCE 1374 ---
The development of the local fish harvesting sector in American Samoa continues to be constrained by a shortage of private capital and, to some extent, by the economic preferences and cultural values of local fishermen.

--- SENTENCE 1375 ---
Most residents interested in commercial fishing do not have sufficient financial resources to invest in large, expensive vessels.

--- SENTENCE 1376 ---
In addition, the majority of the fishermen in American Samoa do not rely on the sale of their catch as their only source of income.

--- SENTENCE 1377 ---
The technologies and patterns of fishing that have evolved over the years in American Samoa are culturally acceptable as well as economically reasonable for small-scale fishermen.

--- SENTENCE 1378 ---
They have demonstrated a willingness to adopt new types of fishing gear and methods so that their catching power and efficiency has increased incrementally.

--- SENTENCE 1379 ---
The small to medium-size vessels favored by fishermen are easily and inexpensively built and maintained and they are capable of harvesting diverse fishery resources utilizing a variety of gear types.

--- SENTENCE 1380 ---
Despite increasing commercialization of the catch, fishing continues to contribute to the perpetuation of Samoa culture and social cohesion in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1381 ---
The dependence of the early Samoans on fishing for food security shaped their social organization, cultural values and religion.

--- SENTENCE 1382 ---
Of course, many aspects of Samoan culture have evolved but fishing remains an important cultural practice.

--- SENTENCE 1383 ---
The role of fishing in cultural continuity is at least as important as the contributions made to the nutritional or economic well-being of island residents.

# II.4.1 Biological/Ecological Impacts

# II.4.1.1 Impacts on Target Pelagic Fish Resources

The preferred alternative would have little or no stockwide impact on tunas or secondary pelagic species, including billfish, sharks and epipelagic fish, which are taken in the pelagic fishery in the EEZ around American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1431 ---
Pelagic species are not confined to any particular EEZ or country but have a wide geographical distribution in the central and western Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 1432 ---
None of these stocks are presently overexploited in the South Pacific and stockwide effects are unlikely even if the American Samoa catch increased several fold.

--- SENTENCE 1433 ---
The stock structures of pelagic fish are by no means well defined.

--- SENTENCE 1434 ---
Long distance movements are evident for all tuna species but mixing of various fractions of stocks between areas, at least over short and medium time periods, seems to be quite incomplete.

--- SENTENCE 1435 ---
Fluctuations in local ocean environmental conditions or prey availability can cause striking and unpredictable changes in the relative abundance and catch rates of pelagic fish, as a function of local movement patterns rather than "overfishing."

--- SENTENCE 1436 ---
If these fluctuations reduce the availability of pelagic fish in a localized area, they have a much more severe impact on small-scale fleets with limited fishing range than on larger, more mobile vessels.

--- SENTENCE 1437 ---
Local reductions in pelagic fish density can be amplified by interactions with nearby fisheries.

--- SENTENCE 1438 ---
The effect of fishing tuna in one area may affect the performance of a fishery harvesting the same stock in a nearby area.

--- SENTENCE 1439 ---
How various gears fishing in the same time and area compete "locally" to exploit pelagic fish resources and the effects on availability of the target fish are poorly understood.

--- SENTENCE 1440 ---
It is not understood whether the sub-surface albacore tuna taken in American Samoa's small-scale longline fishery are primarily a local sub-population or are part of a more widely distributed regional mass (Stanley and Toloa 1998).

--- SENTENCE 1441 ---
The albacore catch by small-scale longline fisheries in American Samoa and neighboring Samoa totaled 6,800 mt in 1998, which represents 19% of the South Pacific regional albacore harvest by longline fisheries (Lawson, 1999).

--- SENTENCE 1442 ---
Albacore catches in American Samoa's longline fishery decreased from 454 mt in 1998 to 302 mt in 1999 (Coan et al. 2000).

--- SENTENCE 1443 ---
The effect of rapid expansion and high catches by the small-scale longline fishery in western Samoa on the regional "throughput" of albacore cannot be estimated but may be a contributing factor in a recent decline in CPUE experienced by the American Samoa small-scale fishery.

--- SENTENCE 1444 ---
Nor is it known whether catches obtained in the recent expansion years of the western Samoa fishery are representative of the long-term average.

--- SENTENCE 1445 ---
Most of the other island nations which are neighbors to American Samoa are also developing domestic longline fisheries.

--- SENTENCE 1446 ---
In general, the vessels that constitute the US longline fleet are highly mobile.

--- SENTENCE 1447 ---
The dozens of longline vessels that arrived in Hawaii during the late 1980s were from Alaska, California, the Gulf of Mexico and the East Coast (Travis 1999).

--- SENTENCE 1448 ---
Some of these vessels later returned to the continental US or moved on to new fishing areas such as the waters around Fiji.

--- SENTENCE 1449 ---
It is likely that some of the longline vessels that remained in Hawaii are currently seeking alternative fishing grounds because the area around the Hawaiian Islands in which longline vessels are allowed to operate is becoming increasingly restricted.

--- SENTENCE 1450 ---
In November 1999, a US District Court issued an injunction prohibiting vessels holding a Hawaii longline limited access permit from fishing within a 1.2 million square mile area north of the Hawaiian Islands.

--- SENTENCE 1451 ---
This area encompasses some of the principal fishing grounds for Hawaii-based longline vessels targeting swordfish.

--- SENTENCE 1452 ---
A court order issued in August 2000 continued the closed area and added other restrictions on longline operations that have crippled Hawaii's swordfish longline fishery.

--- SENTENCE 1453 ---
As many as 40 longline vessels have been displaced and some may look to American Samoa as a potential new home port.

--- SENTENCE 1454 ---
In addition, quotas, limited access programs, commercial trip limits, incidental catch restrictions, prohibitions on sale, minimum size limits and time and area closures are among the array of measures that have been implemented or are being discussed by federal and state entities to manage pelagic stocks in the Atlantic and Gulf of Mexico.

--- SENTENCE 1455 ---
It is likely that these measures will restrict longline fishing and will cause some longline vessels currently operating in these areas to seek alternative fishing grounds.

--- SENTENCE 1456 ---
In addition, restrictions in other fisheries, such as the Gulf of Mexico shrimp fishery, may induce some fishermen to equip their vessels with longline gear and move to areas where there are productive fishing grounds for pelagic species.

--- SENTENCE 1457 ---
Most of the fishing activity by US purse seine vessels occurs in the EEZ waters of Papua New Guinea, Federated States of Micronesia and other Pacific island nations far west of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1458 ---
During El Nino events, however, these vessels are known to shift their fishing activity to the equatorial central Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 1459 ---
The percentage of yellowfin tuna in their catches often increases with this shift.

--- SENTENCE 1460 ---
The purse seine fleet does not catch albacore, so there is no interaction with American Samoa's small-scale longline fishery, whose main target is albacore.

--- SENTENCE 1461 ---
Yellowfin tuna comprise a minor portion of the present longline catch but this species could become a more desirable target in the future.

--- SENTENCE 1462 ---
There is not yet any compelling evidence that the increased purse seine catches of large and small yellowfin have had any significant effect on yellowfin longline catch rates on a broad scale.

--- SENTENCE 1463 ---
Even in areas where high levels of effort by longline and surface gears co-occur, interaction has been difficult to demonstrate.

--- SENTENCE 1464 ---
A study in Kiribati suggests that, at a scale of separation of 300-600 nmi, purse seine catches of yellowfin are not negatively correlated with troll fishery catches.

--- SENTENCE 1465 ---
When purse seiners fished within 60 nm from Kiribati shorelines, however, some negative impacts were observed (Hampton et al. 1996).

--- SENTENCE 1466 ---
There is some indication from preliminary modeling of theoretical skipjack tuna movement that purse seine fishing could have an impact on the availability of skipjack tuna for small-scale trolling as far away as 600 nm (P. Kleiber pers.

--- SENTENCE 1467 ---
During 1999, 90 percent of western Pacific US purse seine fishing effort was around untethered FADs (i.e. rafts known as payao) (Coan et al. 2000).

--- SENTENCE 1468 ---
With increased deployment of FADs by the US fleet, there is an increased risk that the migratory behavior of tuna in the western Pacific might be affected.

--- SENTENCE 1469 ---
FADs might retain tuna in areas where they would otherwise quickly pass through, and not be enticed by concentrated forage to remain.

--- SENTENCE 1470 ---
This could affect their biological parameters (growth, maturity, survival) and population dynamics.

--- SENTENCE 1471 ---
These potential effects on the population biology of tunas and on their ecosystem are currently largely unknown and require research attention (Sakagawa in press).

--- SENTENCE 1472 ---
Small-scale fishermen in American Samoa are concerned that the US purse seine vessels which land fish at the tuna canneries in the Territory occasionally harvest surface tuna in the adjacent EEZ.

--- SENTENCE 1473 ---
Available data suggest that this is a rare occurrence.

--- SENTENCE 1474 ---
Even so, the large quantity of pelagic fish (30+ mt) that can be taken by a single purse seine set could have a severe impact if fish densities were reduced for a season or longer in the areas fished by the small-scale fleet.

--- SENTENCE 1475 ---
The effects would be magnified if purse seines were set at specific localities in the EEZ where tuna may aggregate seasonally.

--- SENTENCE 1476 ---
It is well known, for example, that seamounts and submarine features that rise sharply from the sea floor can aggregate commercial concentrations of tuna, although the responsible mechanisms are poorly understood (Rogers 1994;

--- SENTENCE 1477 ---
No. 1 (50-nm) - Preferred

At a meeting on June 14, 2000, the Council selected Alternative No. 1 as the preferred framework measure (Figure 12.1).

--- SENTENCE 1478 ---
This alternative would close an area extending approximately 50 nm offshore from all islands of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1479 ---
A 50-nm closure would encompass all of the areas where the small-scale fleet presently fishes and all of the known banks and seamounts which are likely to aggregate tuna, thus providing for small-scale fishery expansion.

--- SENTENCE 1480 ---
Approximately one-third of the EEZ would be closed to pelagic fishing by large vessels (> 50 ft).

--- SENTENCE 1481 ---
Existing or potential interactions between large and small-scale vessels fishing for pelagic species in the EEZ around American Samoa cannot be quantified with available information.

--- SENTENCE 1482 ---
In general, pelagic fisheries interactions are difficult to document and model because of inadequate data, insufficient knowledge of the biology and population dynamics of the resource and poor understanding of environmental influences (Shomura et al. 1994; Shomura 1996).

--- SENTENCE 1483 ---
Migratory routes of specific fractions of stocks are of major importance in determining the availability of pelagic fish in localized areas fished by small-scale fleets.

--- SENTENCE 1484 ---
Specific routes are not scientifically documented but tuna fishermen in some islands of the Pacific have learned to "read" local tuna dynamics after many years of careful observation (Kaneko et al. 2000).

--- SENTENCE 1485 ---
Areas closed to large fishing vessels compensate for uncertainty about pelagic fish resource abundance and dynamics because they limit pelagic fish mortality while improving fishery data.

--- SENTENCE 1486 ---
New information will be acquired by small-scale fishermen as they venture farther from offshore and discover new grounds.

--- SENTENCE 1487 ---
Entry into the fishery by large vessels represents a much greater increase in fishing effort and potential fishing mortality than the addition of small-scale boats.

--- SENTENCE 1488 ---
A typical 50+ ft longliner in neighboring Samoa, for example, sets 1,200-1,600 hooks per day, compared to 250-900 hooks set by small-scale longline vessels (< 50 ft) (Mulipola 2000).

--- SENTENCE 1489 ---
In the Pacific basin, the establishment of area closures is increasingly becoming the preferred management tool to resolve conflicts associated with competition among fisheries harvesting the same local populations of pelagic fish resources.

--- SENTENCE 1490 ---
The government of neighboring Samoa has implemented regulations prohibiting longline fishing by vessels larger than 15 m within 50 nm of shore (Mulipola 2000).

--- SENTENCE 1491 ---
The Marshall Islands established a 50-nm longline exclusion zone around the atolls of Majuro and Kwajalein in 1996, after sportsfishermen contended that trolling catch rates for game fish species such as blue marlin and yellowfin tuna had declined as a result of fishing by the locally-based longline fleet (Bigelow and Lewis undated).

--- SENTENCE 1492 ---
There is evidence from other pelagic fisheries that intensive fishing effort within core areas can reduce catch per unit of effort (CPUE) on a localized scale.

--- SENTENCE 1493 ---
Such an effect was observed on the Pacific coast of Mexico, where an increase of longline fishing effort led to marked overall decreases of CPUE in both longline and troll fisheries (Muhlia-Melo 1996).

--- SENTENCE 1494 ---
After Mexico established a sport fishery preserve which extended 50 nm offshore along the Pacific coast, Squire and Au (1990) noted that an improvement in striped marlin catch rates, which reflected the fishing down and rebuilding of two localized near shore areas where fish are attracted and regularly linger during their life cycle.

--- SENTENCE 1495 ---
In 1987, Mexico extended the area closed to longline fishing farther offshore (Muhlia-Melo 1996).

--- SENTENCE 1496 ---
A 50-nm closed area may not be sufficient to encompass the natural variations in local tuna movement patterns or to encompass undiscovered seamounts where tuna aggregate in the EEZ of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1497 ---
Albacore tuna concentrations have shifted farther offshore since late 1998, according to the owners of larger, mobile longline vessels based in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1498 ---
The preferred alternative considers the newly expanded fishing range of new and safer vessels entering American Samoa's small-scale fleet and provides sufficient buffer area to encourage controlled expansion of the small-scale fishery by the newer *alia* catamaran vessels.

--- SENTENCE 1499 ---
However, the *alia* design, even in newer versions, does not provide sufficient capacity to store and chill large fresh fish catches and long-range fishing beyond 50 nm from shore is not economically efficient.

--- SENTENCE 1500 ---
If a significant number of monohull vessels with larger carrying capacity and longer range join the small-scale fleet in the future, a 50-nm closure may not be sufficient to prevent interactions with large-scale vessels (> 50 ft).

--- SENTENCE 1501 ---
During ENSO events, US purse seine fishing activity shifts toward the central equatorial Pacific, with sets made rarely in the portion of American Samoa's EEZ near Swains Island.

--- SENTENCE 1502 ---
Considering that negative impacts on small-scale tuna fisheries by purse seine fishing are much more likely within 60 nm of shore (Hampton et al. 1996), inclusion of Swains Island in a uniform 50-nm area closure would avoid the potential for yellowfin tuna catch competition with the purse seine fishery.

No. 5 (no action)

Uncontrolled expansion of the pelagic fishery in the EEZ of American Samoa is possible with no action.

--- SENTENCE 1522 ---
Highly capitalized, mechanized vessels with greater fishing power could fish within the range of the small-scale fishery.

--- SENTENCE 1523 ---
An Asian longline fishery operated near American Samoa from the mid-1950s through the 1970s, until the Magnuson Act was implemented in 1977.

--- SENTENCE 1524 ---
The history of that fishery demonstrates the potential for a decline in tuna catch rates in conjunction with increasing fishing effort by large longline vessels.

--- SENTENCE 1525 ---
Albacore catch rates in the waters around American Samoa declined as the Asian longline fleet expanded rapidly in the 1950s.

--- SENTENCE 1526 ---

--- SENTENCE 1528 ---
That the apparent effect was not greater was due to expansion of the fishery into areas south of 20°S, where better catch rates were obtained.

--- SENTENCE 1529 ---
Little data specific to American Samoa's EEZ are available from the earliest period of the fishery (1954-1959), when Asian longliners were still learning the albacore tuna grounds and confined their fishing largely to the vicinity of the Samoa islands.

--- SENTENCE 1530 ---

--- SENTENCE 1531 ---

--- SENTENCE 1532 ---

American Samoa's domestic pelagic fishery is constrained by small boats with a limited fishing range.

--- SENTENCE 1534 ---

# II.4.1.3 Impacts on bycatch

Most of the non-tuna fish taken by longline fisheries in the US Pacific islands are secondary or incidental species, rather than bycatch, because few are discarded.

--- SENTENCE 1548 ---
In 1997, NMFS logbook data for domestic longline vessels based in American Samoa indicated that discards amounted to 4.5 percent of the total catch of large (>50 ft) longline vessels, while 0.2 percent of the total catch of small vessels using longline gear was discarded.

--- SENTENCE 1549 ---
Longsnouted lancet fish, *Alepisaurus ferox*, is one of the most common species caught on longline gear by fishermen in neighboring Samoa and is always discarded (Mulipola 2000).

--- SENTENCE 1550 ---
Most of the catch of large longline boats that has no market value, including small skipjack and yellow tuna, may be discarded, particularly where fish storage capacity or ice is limited (Mulipola 2000)

--- SENTENCE 1551 ---
but much of the unmarketable portion of the catch is taken home by the crew for personal consumption.

--- SENTENCE 1552 ---
For example, marlin is often cut up and distributed to the crew before the boat returns to port.

--- SENTENCE 1553 ---
Some sharks are finned at sea and the carcasses discarded (Mulipola 2000).

--- SENTENCE 1554 ---
In 1999, 90 percent of the fishing effort by the western Pacific US purse seine fishery was around untethered fish aggregation devices (i.e., rafts known as payao) (Coan et al. 2000).

--- SENTENCE 1555 ---
With increased deployment of payao, there could be undesirable effects, such as discarding of dead undersized tunas and bycatch species.

--- SENTENCE 1556 ---
The shift towards payao operations has affected the US purse seine fleet's catch in three ways.

--- SENTENCE 1557 ---
First, the average size of tuna caught tend to be smaller in floating object sets than in free-swimming school sets.

--- SENTENCE 1558 ---
A higher percentage of tuna that is caught in floating object sets is undersized for US canning and, hence, is discarded.

--- SENTENCE 1559 ---
Second, floating object sets tend to contain a higher proportion of bigeye tuna than free-swimming school sets.

--- SENTENCE 1560 ---
Third, because floating objects tend to aggregate a large number of pelagic species than tuna, they produce more bycatch than free-swimming school sets (Coan et al. 1999).

--- SENTENCE 1561 ---
The Forum Fishery Agency (FFA) administers an observer program covering a minimum of 20 percent of the fishing trips by US purse seiners for sampling per year.

--- SENTENCE 1562 ---
FFA observers collected bycatch and discard information from 616 sets made in 1998.

--- SENTENCE 1563 ---
The data have not been fully reviewed for accuracy and some data appear to be inconsistent with logbook information.

--- SENTENCE 1564 ---
Nevertheless, FFA observer data for 1998 indicate almost a 25-fold difference in the bycatch rate between floating object sets (1.59 mt of bycatch per set) and free-swimming school sets (0.06 mt of bycatch per set).

--- SENTENCE 1565 ---
Rainbow runner, oceanic triggerfish, sharks, mahimahi, wahoo, mackerel scad, mackerel and marlins were the most frequently taken bycatch species, according to FFA observers.

--- SENTENCE 1566 ---
Over 90 percent (by number) of the rainbow runner, triggerfish, mackerel scad and mackerel were discarded.

--- SENTENCE 1567 ---
Over 50 percent (by number) of wahoo and marlin were discarded.

--- SENTENCE 1568 ---
Only 17 percent of the sharks were discarded as whole fish (Coan et al. 1999)

--- SENTENCE 1569 ---
but the remainder were presumably discarded after finning.

--- SENTENCE 1570 ---
By closing some areas of American Samoa's EEZ to pelagic fishing by large longline vessels and purse seiners, Alternative No. 1 (50-nm) could reduce the potential for bycatch, particularly from the purse seine fishery.

--- SENTENCE 1571 ---
Alternative No. 2 (50/30-nm closures) would offer marginally less beneficial impacts, whereas Alternative No. 3 (100-nm closures) has the greatest potential for positive impacts.

--- SENTENCE 1572 ---
Alternative No. 4 (foreign longline closures) is not as positive as the others but the smaller closed areas would still be more beneficial than no action, which does not offer any potential for reduction of bycatch in pelagic fisheries in American Samoa's EEZ.

# II.4.1.4 Impacts on protected species and wildlife refuge resources

Interactions with protected species are uncommon in American Samoa's small-scale pelagic fishery.

--- SENTENCE 1574 ---
One hawksbill turtle and one olive ridley turtle were reported hooked in 1999.

--- SENTENCE 1575 ---
Both were released alive.

--- SENTENCE 1576 ---
These were the only interactions with any protected species reported for the domestic longline fishery during the 1996-1999 period.

--- SENTENCE 1577 ---
Both large-scale and small-scale longline vessels set mainline deep enough (45 fm) to target albacore tuna (Kaneko et al. 2000), thus reducing the potential for interactions with turtles.

--- SENTENCE 1578 ---
Although there have been no reported interactions, other protected species occur in the waters around American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1579 ---
In Fagatele Bay National Marine Sanctuary, southern humpback whales mate and calve from June through September.

--- SENTENCE 1580 ---
Sperm whales are occasionally seen in the Sanctuary as well.

--- SENTENCE 1581 ---
Both species are listed as endangered under the Endangered Species Act (ESA) and the Marine Mammal Protection Act (MMPA).

--- SENTENCE 1582 ---
Several species of dolphins protected under the MMPA but not listed as threatened or endangered also frequent the Sanctuary.

--- SENTENCE 1583 ---

# II.4.2.2 Fishing gear conflict

In American Samoa, the small-scale longline fleet deploys relatively short lengths of mainline and they can usually avoid tangling gear.

--- SENTENCE 1683 ---
The larger vessels which are active in the longline fishery usually fish in offshore areas which produce higher catch rates.

--- SENTENCE 1684 ---
If additional large vessels enter the fishery and set gear in the same areas as the small-scale fleet, there would be a much greater potential for gear conflicts with the small-boat fleet.

--- SENTENCE 1685 ---
Large vessels (> 50 ft) may set 30-50 miles of mainline, compared to 7-25 miles of mainline set by small-scale vessels (< 50 ft) in Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1686 ---
Dramatic expansion of the small-scale longline fishing fleet in western Samoa led to congestion and gear conflict in nearshore areas of concentrated fishing during the dramatic growth period of 1996-1997.

--- SENTENCE 1687 ---
To minimize further conflicts, the Samoa government has implemented regulations that prohibit longline fishing by vessels over 15 m within 50 nm of the coast (Chapman 1998).

--- SENTENCE 1688 ---
Gear conflict was evident between the small-boat (troll and handline) and longline fleets during the rapid expansion of the Hawaii longline fleet in the late 1980s.

--- SENTENCE 1689 ---
The fleet increased from 37 vessels in 1987 to 75 in 1989, and then doubled again to 156 vessels in 1991.

--- SENTENCE 1690 ---
Many of the new longline vessels were recent arrivals from the continental USA.

--- SENTENCE 1691 ---
In addition to straining harbor facilities, the increased fishing effort led to gear conflicts and precipitated heated confrontations between the longliners and the established local fishing fleet, which consisted mainly of small troll and handline vessels (Pooley 1990).

--- SENTENCE 1692 ---
The preferred alternative (No. 1) would reduce the potential for gear conflict more than no action (Alternative No. 5) and Alternatives No. 2 and 4 based on the percentage of American Samoa's EEZ that would be closed to large vessels.

--- SENTENCE 1693 ---
Alternative No. 3 would close a larger percentage of the EEZ to large vessels but *alia* have limited carrying for fish and there is presently little economic incentive to set gear beyond 50 nm from shore.

--- SENTENCE 1694 ---
Should a significant number of monohull vessels with larger carrying capacity join the small-scale fleet in the future, however, the range of economically efficient fishing would be extended and this alternative could become beneficial in preventing gear conflicts in the EEZ beyond 50 nm from shore.

--- SENTENCE 1695 ---

--- SENTENCE 1699 ---
Large longline vessels relocating to American Samoa from Hawaii would no doubt have the same experience.

--- SENTENCE 1700 ---
Alternatives 2 (50/30-nm) and 4 (foreign longline closures) would not have substantially different impacts on new entry than the preferred alternative (50-nm).

--- SENTENCE 1701 ---

## II.4.2.4 Fairness and equity to fishermen

The allocation of fishing privileges resulting from the preferred alternative is rationally connected to the furtherance of FMP objectives (Section 6).

--- SENTENCE 1705 ---

--- SENTENCE 1706 ---
The preferred measure (Alternative No. 1) is reasonably calculated to promote conservation, and no particular individual, corporation or other entity is expected to acquire an excessive share of fishing privileges.

--- SENTENCE 1707 ---

--- SENTENCE 1708 ---

--- SENTENCE 1710 ---

--- SENTENCE 1711 ---
Hence, the sustained participation of the fishing community could be better served by increased local ownership of less costly, small-scale boats that provide greater employment opportunities.

--- SENTENCE 1712 ---
The newest (and safest) 40 ft version of the *alia*-style vessel can be purchased for about \$60,000, with earlier versions available for \$24,000-40,000.

--- SENTENCE 1713 ---

Assuming a 30% down payment and a 10-year loan at 10% annual interest rate, the initial payment of \$18,000 and an annual loan and interest payment of \$6,835 for a 40 ft *alia* would be an affordable investment for many small-scale fishing enterprises in American Samoa, whereas a down payment of \$105,000 with an annual loan and interest payment of nearly \$40,000 for a 65 ft longliner would be financially feasible only for a select few.

--- SENTENCE 1715 ---
The small-scale vessels favored by local fishermen are inexpensively built and maintained and are capable of multi-purpose harvesting of pelagic and other fisheries resources utilizing a variety of gear types.

--- SENTENCE 1716 ---

# II.4.2.5 Safety of fishermen

Development of small-scale longline fisheries in western and American Samoa was based on an FAO-designed catamaran-style vessel called an *alia*.

--- SENTENCE 1718 ---
The original design was for a multipurpose 28 ft fishing boat.

--- SENTENCE 1719 ---
The economic success of the fishery encouraged boat builders in western Samoa to produce a larger *alia*.

--- SENTENCE 1720 ---
A large number of the latter vessels were "stretched" to 32-34 ft, beyond the size recommended by the FAO project, and they were constructed without consultation from marine surveyors or naval architects.

--- SENTENCE 1721 ---
Many of the larger vessels proved incapable of sustaining rough ocean conditions.

--- SENTENCE 1722 ---
Consequently, during the dramatic growth years of 1996-1997, the *alia* longline fishery in western Samoa had a poor safety record.

--- SENTENCE 1723 ---
This was a period when gear conflicts and congestion in heavily fished nearshore areas forced fishing crews to venture farther from shore (Stanley and Toloa 1998).

--- SENTENCE 1724 ---
In the space of 15 months during 1997 and early 1998, at least 14 major accidents occurred, with a loss of 9 vessels and 25 fishermen lost at sea (Chapman 1998).

--- SENTENCE 1725 ---
Lack of safety equipment, inexperienced crews, engine failures and poor quality fuels have also contributed to the loss of life and vessels in western Samoa (Fa`asili 1997).

--- SENTENCE 1726 ---
One of the recent developments to improve safety standards is the design and construction of new versions of the *alia* which are more seaworthy and are properly equipped with navigational aids, safety and communication equipment.

--- SENTENCE 1727 ---
One type of new vessel is a 38-42 ft *alia* assembled from pre-cut aluminum plates manufactured in Australia.

--- SENTENCE 1728 ---
In December 1999, at least 6 fishing enterprises in American Samoa were known to have such vessels under construction.

--- SENTENCE 1729 ---
These boats have larger fuel tanks than earlier versions of the *alia* and they are capable of safely extending the range of small-scale fishermen in American Samoa to at least 100 nm from shore (Capt. W. Thompson, letter to WPRFMC dated 3 Feb. 2000).

--- SENTENCE 1730 ---
The FAO is designing a 45 ft version of the *alia* for the next phase of longline fishery expansion in western Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1731 ---
When this type of vessel becomes available, it will no doubt be acquired by some small-scale fishing enterprises in American Samoa to extend the range of fishing to 200 nm offshore.

--- SENTENCE 1732 ---
The preferred alternative (No. 1) may promote vessel safety by maintaining the potential for good catches of pelagic fish in the closed areas.

--- SENTENCE 1733 ---
The 50 ft restriction on vessel size, therefore, is not expected to place the safety of fishery participants at risk.

--- SENTENCE 1734 ---
The impacts on safety of Alternatives No. 2 and No. 3 would not be substantially different from that of the preferred alternative.

--- SENTENCE 1735 ---
Alternative No. 3 would establish much larger closed areas but this would not necessarily compromise vessel safety.

--- SENTENCE 1736 ---
In Hawaii fisheries, troll and handline vessels averaging 40 ft in length regularly make trips of 150 nm or more to seamounts and weather buoys without compromising safety.

--- SENTENCE 1737 ---
The vessels that make trips as far as 1000 nm to participate in the federally-regulated Northwestern Hawaiian Islands bottomfish fishery have an average size of 54 ft.

--- SENTENCE 1738 ---
Large vessels (> 50 ft) presently fishing or most likely to relocate to American Samoa are seaworthy monohull vessels which can safely fish hundreds to thousands of miles from port, depending on fuel capacity.

--- SENTENCE 1739 ---
None of the alternatives, therefore, would affect the safety of large fishing vessels (> 50 ft).

--- SENTENCE 1740 ---
Samoa is a major source of vessels for fishing enterprises in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1741 ---
The acquisition of at least 6 new and safer *alia* in the 38-42 ft class by fishermen in American Samoa will allow the small-scale fishery to extend the range of operation to 100 nm offshore without major risk (Capt. W. Thompson, letter to WPRFMC dated 3 Feb. 2000).

--- SENTENCE 1742 ---
Vessel length is only one facet of boat seaworthiness.

--- SENTENCE 1743 ---
Proper safety training and the acquisition of communication and emergency equipment are also important factors in reducing risk.

--- SENTENCE 1744 ---
The US Coast Guard has recently undertaken a campaign to inform fishermen in American Samoa about US commercial fishing vessel safety regulations and to ensure compliance with these regulations.

--- SENTENCE 1745 ---
Alternative No. 4 and no action (Alternative No. 5) provide little encouragement to the small-scale fishermen in American Samoa to invest in safer vessels and obtain safety and communications equipment and training.

--- SENTENCE 1746 ---
If additional large vessels begin to harvest pelagic fish in the same areas as the small-scale fleet, fish densities and catch rates could decline, inducing small-scale vessels to venture to unsafe distances offshore in order to maintain catch rates.

# II.4.2.6 Administrative and enforcement costs of regulation

The preferred alternative (No. 1) would minimize enforcement costs by using straight lines to define the boundaries of the area closures.

--- SENTENCE 1748 ---
Without an automated, satellite-based vessel monitoring system (VMS), however, an area closure can be very difficult and expensive to enforce, requiring at-sea and aerial surveillance.

--- SENTENCE 1749 ---
The NMFS Law Enforcement Office has determined that it is not cost-effective at this time for it to develop and administer a VMS to enforce an area closure in the EEZ around American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1750 ---
The Department of Marine and Wildlife Resources, in cooperation with NMFS Law Enforcement Office, has initiated a test program for an appropriately scaled-down VMS for alia.

--- SENTENCE 1751 ---
During the next year, VMS will be deployed in a trial on several vessels for 2-3 week periods (DMWR seminar with alia owners, August 2000).

--- SENTENCE 1752 ---
It is possible that, in the near future, international fisheries agreements in the central and western Pacific will require vessels that harvest pelagic species in more than one EEZ or on the high seas to carry VMS units as part of a regional surveillance and monitoring program (FFA 1996a).

--- SENTENCE 1753 ---
If such a regional program is implemented, all foreign longline and domestic purse seine vessels based in American Samoa would be required to carry a VMS unit.

--- SENTENCE 1754 ---
In this event, all large domestic longline vessels based in the Territory would probably have to be equipped with a VMS unit if they fished outside American Samoa's EEZ or in the EEZs of neighboring countries.

--- SENTENCE 1755 ---
In the initial stage of implementation, enforcement of the proposed area closures would have to rely on surveillance and reporting by the small-scale fleet itself.

--- SENTENCE 1756 ---

--- SENTENCE 1766 ---

An area closure which extends to the northern limit of American Samoa's EEZ would reinforce the US claim.

--- SENTENCE 1768 ---
Alternative No. 4 and no action (Alternative No. 5) would not improve surveillance or enforcement from the status quo, nor would they increase administrative and enforcement costs related to fishery management.

## III.2 Problem Statement and Need for Action

The entry of large vessels into the pelagics fishery in the Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) around American Samoa could conflict with the objectives of the Council's management plan for pelagic fisheries (FMP) by 1) failing to achieve optimum yield, as defined in the FMP; 2) creating gear conflicts, particularly in areas of concentrated fishing; 3) reducing the opportunities for (a) profitable fishing operations; (b) traditional fishing practices for non-market personal consumption and cultural benefits; and (c) satisfying recreational fishing experiences.

--- SENTENCE 1792 ---
The proposed action (a limitation on fishing by certain size vessels within a specified area around American Samoa) is needed to maintain sustained community fishing effort and cultural continuity, encourage the harvest of underutilized pelagic fishery resources without overcapitalization, maintain catch rates of pelagic fish at commercially viable levels within the fishing range of the small-boat fleet and provide a framework for adaptive management and rapid implementation of regulatory adjustments.

--- SENTENCE 1793 ---
# III.3 Existing Management Measures

A National Marine Fisheries Service (NMFS) longline general permit is required for longline fishing in American Samoa's EEZ.

--- SENTENCE 1794 ---
This fishery is presently open access, with no limits on the number of longline vessels, individual or total vessel capacity, catch or effort.

--- SENTENCE 1795 ---
A control date of November 13, 1997, has been established and all applicants for longline permits after that date are informed that they may not qualify for exemptions to limitations placed on longline vessels greater than 50 ft in overall length.

--- SENTENCE 1796 ---
There has been no legal fishing by foreign longline vessels in the EEZ around American Samoa since 1980, when the pelagic fisheries Preliminary Management Plan (PMP) for the Western Pacific Region was implemented.

--- SENTENCE 1797 ---
Although this plan has mechanisms for foreign fishing, they involve onerous requirements (e.g., permits, fees, observers) on foreign vessels which have made them unattractive.

--- SENTENCE 1798 ---
There is a possibility that legal fishing in the EEZ by foreign vessels may resume at some time under a Pacific Insular Area Fishing Agreement (PIAFA), which could give foreign vessels access to the EEZ around American Samoa in exchange for a negotiated fee and subject to a variety of permit conditions.

--- SENTENCE 1799 ---
# III.4 Management Objectives

The following management objectives are address in this management measure:

- Maintain the potential for economically viable catch rates in American Samoa's small-scale fishery as it evolves from a traditional subsistence activity harvesting heavily exploited inshore marine resources to a more commercial activity extending the range of fishing offshore to harvest underutilized pelagic fish (i.e., achieve optimum yield as defined in pelagic FMP).

--- SENTENCE 1800 ---
- Avoid gear conflicts between large and small-scale vessels within the fishing range of the small-scale pelagic fishery.

--- SENTENCE 1801 ---
- Provide for sustained community participation in the small-scale pelagic fishery, recognizing that American Samoa is becoming increasingly dependent on pelagic fish for food, income, employment and perpetuation of Samoan culture.
-

--- SENTENCE 1802 ---
Provide a framework for adaptive management and rapid implementation of regulatory adjustments.

Alternative No. 1 (Preferred) -

--- SENTENCE 1819 ---
Under this alternative, the closed areas comprise one-third of American Samoa's total EEZ (Figure 12.1).

--- SENTENCE 1820 ---
A 50-nm area closure would encompass all of the areas where the small-scale fleet presently fishes, as well as several additional banks and seamounts which are likely to aggregate tuna.

--- SENTENCE 1821 ---
The direct benefits of any area closure clearly fall to those vessels which receive permits, while the costs fall on those who do not.

--- SENTENCE 1822 ---
The benefits of closing an area around American Samoa to large fishing vessels are awkward to quantify as pelagic fisheries interactions are difficult to document and model due to inadequate data.

--- SENTENCE 1823 ---
insufficient knowledge of the biology and population dynamics of the resource and poor understanding of environmental influences.

--- SENTENCE 1824 ---
In addition, how various gears fishing in the same time and area compete for locally available fishery resources and the effects on availability of the target fish are poorly understood.

--- SENTENCE 1825 ---
There is evidence from other pelagic fisheries that intensive fishing effort within core areas can reduce catch per unit of effort (CPUE) on a localized scale.

--- SENTENCE 1826 ---
Such an effect was observed on the Pacific coast of Mexico, where an increase of longline fishing effort led to marked overall decreases of CPUE in both longline and troll fisheries.

--- SENTENCE 1827 ---
After Mexico established a sport fishery preserve which extended 50 nm offshore along the Pacific coast, improvements in striped marlin catch rates were reported by trollers, which reflected the fishing down and rebuilding of two localized near shore areas where fish are attracted and regularly linger during their life cycle.

--- SENTENCE 1828 ---
In addition, fluctuations in local ocean environmental conditions or prey availability can cause striking and unpredictable changes in the relative abundance and catch rates of pelagic fish, as a function of local movement patterns rather than overfishing.

--- SENTENCE 1829 ---
If these fluctuations reduce the availability of pelagic fish in a localized area, they have a much more severe impact on smallscale fleets with limited fishing range than on larger, more mobile vessels which can seek alternate fishing grounds, including the two-thirds of the EEZ which would remain open under this alternative.

--- SENTENCE 1830 ---
As an example of the differences in fishery impacts between the American Samoa-based fleet and the types of vessels which might be expected to be excluded by this measure, a typical 65 ft longliner in Hawaii sets 1,200-1,500 hooks per day with an annual catch of 250,000 pounds, compared to 200-500 hooks per day and 35,000 pounds landed per year by a typical American Samoa small-scale longline vessel.

--- SENTENCE 1831 ---
Similarly, the average annual catch of skipjack by a US purse seine vessel operating in the central and western Pacific from 1990 to 1997 was 3,161 mt.

--- SENTENCE 1832 ---
Again, the fishery effects of an influx of vessels such as these into American Samoa's nearshore EEZ are difficult to quantify.

--- SENTENCE 1833 ---
The costs to excluded vessels are similarly speculative as few such vessels have fished anywhere in American Samoa's EEZ within the last decade.

--- SENTENCE 1834 ---
One large, non-American Samoa based longliner made one trip to fish in this area since 1990 and a total of six US purse vessels made seven sets within the EEZ around American Samoa between 1988 and 1997.

--- SENTENCE 1837 ---
These fishing vessels represent very small owner operated businesses which have limited abilities to withstand even short term revenue interruptions.

--- SENTENCE 1838 ---
Small-scale fishery participants have little acess to credit advances or other mechanisms to help them weather such an event, and a boom and bust fishing pattern could severely disrupt American Samoa's developing fishery infrastructure.

# III.8 Cost/Benefit Analysis of Alternatives

Because the intent of this measure is to avert potential future events, it is difficult to predict and quantify its costs and benefits.

--- SENTENCE 1890 ---
Obvious and direct benefits are the avoidance of gear conflicts and catch competition between large and small-scale vessels fishing within the (limited) fishing range of the small-scale pelagic fishery.

--- SENTENCE 1891 ---
These preventive measures are, in turn, expected to allow for the continuing and controlled development of a profitable, small-scale fishery which is accessible to residents of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1892 ---
These residents have an average annual per capita income of \$5,000 and a median annual household income of \$16,000.

--- SENTENCE 1893 ---
With an unemployment rate approaching 17 percent and few alternate employment opportunities, small-scale fishing ventures represent an important path to sustainable development and economic self-reliance for American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1894 ---
A sustainable fishery also has significant social importance to residents of American Samoa who have historically utilized fishery resources and whose traditional cultural practices rely heavily on access to these resources.

--- SENTENCE 1895 ---
It is believed that the costs associated with this measure can be borne relatively easily by operators of excluded large vessels, while the costs of no action may prove ruinous to American Samoa's small-scale fleet.

--- SENTENCE 1896 ---
For further discussion of the status of the American Samoa economy, see Appendix I to the framework proposal (Fishing Community Impact Statement), as well as Appendix II (Environmental Assessment).

#### Introduction

Coral reef surgeonfish (Acanthuridae) exhibit a distinctive "square" growth pattern during the course of their long lives (e.g., Choat and Robertson 2002).

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
This growth pattern is characterized by a rapid growth spurt during the first year or two of the fish's life followed by a quick cessation in somatic growth for the remaining decades of the fish's life, yielding a nearly right-angled growth "curve."

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
This pattern has also been observed in some lutjanids and chaetodontids, as well as some other non-tropical reef species (e.g., Newman et al. 2000; Berumen 2005).

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
Although the asymptotic leg of the square growth curve has been substantiated by numerous age-based studies, the rapid, nearly vertical growth of the first leg of the square growth curve warrants some scrutiny, because most published acanthurid

!

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
[](_page_0_Picture_19.jpeg)

<span id="page-1-0"></span>growth curves are based on few, if any, age-0 fish.

--- SENTENCE 15 ---
This generally occurs for logistical reasons because acanthurid recruits tend to be sporadically available, modest in number, and multiple sampling trips to remote sites may be necessary to acquire a full size range of fish during this period of rapid growth.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
The lack of demographic information about this early stage is especially important for two reasons.

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
Are the young fish actually growing as fast as the models indicate (i.e., to nearly adult size in their first year)?

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
Second, this early-growth period is critical in setting the values of the growth parameters for a fish species.

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
The growth parameters of the commonly used von Bertalanffy growth function (VBGF) can swing 100 % depending on how many young fish anchor the growth equation, which explains some disparities in growth parameter values in the earlier literature.

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
The present study contributes to an understanding of these early demographic processes by tracking an unusually massive recruitment of the bristletooth surgeonfish Ctenochaetus striatus over a 16-month period.

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
Two general issues are highlighted by this focus.

--- SENTENCE 22 ---
Firstly, mass recruitments appear to be restricted mainly to members of the families Acanthuridae and Siganidae (Kami and Ikehara [1976;](#page-10-0) Pillai et al.

--- SENTENCE 23 ---
[1983](#page-10-0); Doherty et al.

--- SENTENCE 24 ---
[2004;](#page-10-0) Priest et al.

--- SENTENCE 25 ---
Mass recruitments are usually followed by rapid and very high mortality rates.

--- SENTENCE 26 ---
The significance of these events is unclear with the possibility that mass recruitments represent a process to swamp high mortalities of recruits by predators (Doherty et al.

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
Given the importance of mass recruiting species as food fishes, details of these events and their subsequent contribution to adult populations are important.

--- SENTENCE 28 ---
Secondly, the study species shares the important characteristic of highly asymptotic growth patterns with a number of reef fishes with the consequence that age and size are decoupled.

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
Changes in size distributions of targeted reef fish populations are considered to be the most consistent predictor of fishing pressure (Jennings et al.

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
[1999;](#page-10-0) Dulvy et al.

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
For species such as C. striatus in which size structure of adult populations cannot be used as an indicator of fishing pressure, additional information of initial growth rates and age structure is a high priority.

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
We first focus on this early life history period for C. striatus, and then show its linkage to the adult growth stage of this species described by Trip et al.

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
The specific aims of this study are to (1) examine recruit dynamics following a mass recruitment event; (2) determine whether the growth rate of recruits during their first year supported the square growth concept; the latter point also involves validating the first annual ring on the otolith, which is critical in generating a growth curve with accurate parameters for species with square growth characteristics; and (3) evaluate the effects of age-0 recruits on acanthurid growth models.

### Materials and methods

Study population and area

Ctenochaetus striatus is among the most abundant and widely distributed coral reef fishes in the Indo-Pacific region.

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
It is common in American Samoa where it is harvested in the local subsistence fishery (Ponwith

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
[1991](#page-10-0); Green [2002;](#page-10-0) Brainard et al.

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
[2007](#page-9-0); Sabater and Tofaeono [2007](#page-10-0);

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
[2008;](#page-10-0) Ochavillo et al.

--- SENTENCE 39 ---
This study was conducted at Ofu Island in the Samoan Archipelago (14S and 170W), which is a small volcanic island (7.5 km2 ) with a well-developed back reef moat (6.6 km long, 100–300 m wide, and 0.5–2 m deep at low tide, but flooded at high tide).

--- SENTENCE 40 ---
The moat supports a high diversity of corals and fish (Craig et al.

--- SENTENCE 41 ---
[2002](#page-10-0); Birkeland et al.

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
The study area consisted of three sampling sites (Pools 100, 200, and 400) within a 4.1-km section of the moat: Pool 100 was located on the western end of the moat in front of Ofu Village (14.17166S, 169.67841W, surface area \*30,000 m<sup>2</sup> ); Pool 200 was located 2.4 km southeast of Pool 100) near the island airstrip (14.18457S, 169.66611W, surface area \*10,000 m<sup>2</sup> ); Pool 400 was located 1.7 km eastward from Pool 200 in front of the locally known ''Hurricane House'' (14.17888S, 169.65416W, surface area \*30,000 m2 ).

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
A further 2.5 km eastward from Pool 400 would be the end of the moat at the eastern end of Ofu Island.

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
Recruits for age and growth analysis were collected at all three sites (usually at one site per sampling period), but quantitative measurements of recruit densities were taken only at Pools 200 and 400.

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
Water temperature was recorded continuously in Pool 400.

## Definition of recruits

Recruits were defined as a mixture of recently settled fish together with other age-0 fish that had settled over previous months.

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
Virtually, all fish described herein were age-0 fish that recruited in 2002; thus, they are referred to as recruits to distinguish them from older C. striatus juveniles, a few of which may be adult sized and several years old (Ochavillo et al.

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
[2011](#page-10-0); P Craig unpub.

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
For all aged fish, their recruitment date was defined as the first day after the settlement band mark on their otoliths (see below).

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
Initially, recruitment of C. striatus was operationally defined as the sighting of large numbers of small, uniquely colored, and newly recruited juveniles in their adult benthic habitat (e.g., Caley et al.

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
Their sudden appearance throughout Ofu moat was designated Day 0 for subsequent quantitative counts of recruits over the next 16 months.

--- SENTENCE 54 ---
During this time, they soon adopted the brown coloration of adults and grew from 5 to 7 mm (fork length) to a nearly adult size of 150–170 mm; thus, a sliding scale was used to count and collect recruits through the study.

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
This was not difficult because nearly all C. striatus in Ofu moat were either growing recruits or considerably larger adults (150–200 mm).

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
It is possible that some larger recruits may have been excluded from early samples, but this omission would not greatly affect either density counts (because such larger recruits were relatively few in number) or age analyses (because larger recruits were sampled as the fish grew).

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
## Recruitment dynamics

Recruitment dynamics were determined by quantitative field counts aided by analysis of recruit ages from separate collections (described below).

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
Recruits were monitored by snorkel at two sites (Pools 200 and 400) at 1–3 month intervals from March 10, 2002 to July 10, 2003 (Table 1).

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
At each site, average counts along five transects (3 m wide and 50 m long) were recorded.

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
Transects had fixed starting points, but due to the shallow waters in the moat, the specific location of the 50-m transect lines was not well defined because they followed coral edges in the moat (not a depth profile) and thus might take slightly different courses in each survey.

--- SENTENCE 61 ---
This is unlikely to introduce error because the recruits were highly mobile.

--- SENTENCE 62 ---
It is acknowledged that recruit survival rates and potential movements within or out of the moat may have affected these calculations, but were not assessed (see ''[Discussion'](#page-6-0)').

#### Age, growth, and maturity

To determine recruit age, growth, and maturity, random collections based on recruit availability were made almost monthly at typically one of three sites (Pools 100, 200, or 400), where large schools of new recruits had originally been observed on Day-0 (Table 1).

--- SENTENCE 63 ---
Each monthly sample of 15–97 recruits (total 334 recruits) was caught by hand net for measurement of fork length (FL) to the nearest mm; 281 of these fish were further examined for weight to the nearest 0.1 g; sexual maturity was assessed by a visual inspection of the gonads; and otoliths were removed, cleaned, and stored dry.

--- SENTENCE 64 ---
Age (in days) was estimated for a subsample of 82 fish that were randomly selected from each 5-mm FL size class within each monthly collection in order to subsample the complete size range present, while selecting a similar number of individuals across months.

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
Note that this size selection produced a sampling bias for some aspects of this study because recruits were not aged in proportion to their recruitment abundance (see below).

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
Age estimates were based on sectioned sagittal otoliths (Choat and Axe [1996](#page-9-0)).

--- SENTENCE 67 ---
Daily ring formation has previously been validated for juvenile acanthurids of the same genus (Ctenochaetus binotatus; Lou and Moltschaniwsky [1992](#page-10-0)).

Use of aging to interpret recruitment dynamics

Otolith analyses were used to assist the interpretation of recruitment dynamics, but statistical limitations occurred for several reasons: Firstly, although these aged fish were randomly collected, they were subsampled by size group rather than in proportion to their field abundance; secondly, peak recruitment pulses may have been missed in months after the mass recruitment; and thirdly, individual sample sizes were small (Table 1).

--- SENTENCE 112 ---
As a result, the ages represented monthly snapshots that were qualitatively used in three ways.

--- SENTENCE 113 ---
First, the recruitment dates of the 80 aged recruits provided the minimum number of months that recruitment occurred through the study and second, to estimate the relative contribution of each month's recruits to total recruitment, a reconstruction was made by pairing density counts and collections of aged fish, when these two data sources were taken during similar time periods (the mean time difference between paired samples was 6.3 d, range 0–16 d; Table 1).

A density baseline for this reconstruction was calculated by averaging densities at Pools 200 and 400 to expand the sample size and area of coverage  $(y = 12.358x^{-0.831}, R^2 = 0.9851)$ .

# Results

A mass recruitment of C. striatus recruits occurred on Ofu's reefs in March 2002, first appearing in low densities on reef slopes about one week after the full moon (5–9 March), followed by massive numbers in shallow waters on 10 March (Day 0, Fig. 1a).

--- SENTENCE 123 ---
Age data indicated that peak recruitment occurred during the two-week period, 8–24 March, with no recruitment during the month proceeding this period or for one week afterward.

--- SENTENCE 124 ---
Mean recruitment size at Day 4 after the mass event was 64.3 mm FL (range 56–70 mm, n = 97).

--- SENTENCE 125 ---
Their initial coloration was a vivid purple–blue body with orange lateral stripes, an orange eye ring, and dark fins with orange-tipped caudal fin lobes (Fig. 1b).

--- SENTENCE 126 ---
Within three to four weeks, their color faded to the brown color of adults, with light brown horizontal lines along their sides.

Age and growth, with implications for acanthurid growth models

By combining the growth phase of the recruits with that of adults (from samples of larger and older C. striatus collected in Ofu by Trip et al.

--- SENTENCE 230 ---
[2008](#page-10-0)), the present study clearly validates the square growth pattern of acanthurid fishes (Fig.

--- SENTENCE 231 ---
The recruits grew rapidly, achieving 90 % of their adult size in their first year, and their asymptotic size of 175.6 mm was reached soon thereafter.

--- SENTENCE 232 ---
Somatic growth then essentially ceased although individuals continued to live for decades.

--- SENTENCE 233 ---
Furthermore, the formation of the first annulus on the otolith provides a firm basis for otolith interpretation of fish ages during the early life history phase of this species.

--- SENTENCE 234 ---
This growth pattern has significant implications for coral reef fish management.

--- SENTENCE 235 ---
First, it confirms that, for all but the smallest individuals, a fish's size and age are not linked; thus, size is not useful for determining acanthurid age, maturity, fishing pressure, or fishing regulations.

--- SENTENCE 236 ---
Second, it impacts estimates of growth parameters (described below), particularly those of the oft-used VBGF, unless selection criteria are standardized (e.g., Choat and Axe

--- SENTENCE 237 ---
[1996;](#page-9-0) Craig et al.

--- SENTENCE 238 ---
[1999](#page-10-0); Trip et al.

--- SENTENCE 239 ---
([2008,](#page-10-0) and citations therein) noted that the biological interpretation and statistical properties of the VGBF parameters K (growth coefficient), Linf (asymptotic length), and t<sup>0</sup> (time when theoretical length would be zero) have often been questioned.

--- SENTENCE 240 ---
The present study demonstrated that K was significantly affected by the presence and number of small fish (particularly age-0) in the sample.

--- SENTENCE 241 ---
For example, when the aged recruits from this study were added to the previous Ofu growth data for older fish presented by Trip et al.

--- SENTENCE 242 ---
([2008\)](#page-10-0), K increased by 16–51 % or decreased by 37 %, depending on the constraint used to model growth (Table [2\)](#page-7-0).

--- SENTENCE 243 ---
We tested three different constraining methods: fixing length-at-age zero to size at settlement and to 0, and fixing age-at-length zero to PLD.

--- SENTENCE 244 ---
All three methods generated K values that differed from the value of K found when growth was modeled using the recruits aged in this study.

--- SENTENCE 245 ---
These results demonstrate two points.

--- SENTENCE 246 ---
First, the presence of young fish affects the value of VBGF parameters, especially parameter K, a fact that has also been noted for several other fishes exhibiting the square growth characteristics (e.g., Craig et al.

--- SENTENCE 247 ---
Second, using a constraint when fitting the VBGF, a method that is routinely used to take into account the lack of young individuals in a sample, does not generate a value of K that adequately represents early growth.

--- SENTENCE 248 ---
This is most likely due to the fact that K is a curvature parameter (not a measure of growth rate) that varies with the age at which the growth curve reaches asymptotic size (Linf) and thus varies directly with the presence and number of samples in the ascending arm of the growth curve.

--- SENTENCE 249 ---
In contrast, adding the aged new recruits from the present study to the previous Ofu growth data from Trip et al.

--- SENTENCE 250 ---
([2008\)](#page-10-0) had a smaller effect on the parameters of the re-parameterized equation of the VBGF (rVBGF; Francis [1988](#page-10-0)).

--- SENTENCE 251 ---
That is, rVBGF parameters were comparatively more stable than the VBGF parameters.

--- SENTENCE 252 ---
This was particularly evident for VBGF parameters K and t0, which showed 95 % CIs that span up to ten times the parameter values, whereas rVBGF parameter mean size-at-age 1 yr L<sup>1</sup> varied over a range of values that covered less than 0.05 times the parameter value.

--- SENTENCE 253 ---
Additionally, the value of rVBGF parameter L<sup>1</sup> increased by only

seven to 22 % when adding the new recruits, depending on the constraining method used.

--- SENTENCE 255 ---
The constraining method that produced parameter values that were closest to those found when including the recruits (i.e., closest to the ''true'' values) was that of constraining age-at-length zero to PLD.

--- SENTENCE 256 ---
This was true for both the VBGF and rVBGF.

--- SENTENCE 257 ---
However, while both growth functions produced parameter values that were relatively close to the ''true'' values under this constraint, the VBGF and rVBGF differed significantly in the following aspect.

--- SENTENCE 258 ---
There was a distinct difference between the two equations in terms of how close the constrained parameter values were found to be to the ''true'' values.

--- SENTENCE 259 ---
While the values for rVBGF parameter L<sup>1</sup> with constraint versus recruits were nearly identical (153.6 mm when constrained versus 153.8 mm with the recruits, Table

--- SENTENCE 260 ---
[2](#page-7-0)), there was a comparatively greater effect on VBGF parameter K, which varied from 1.80 under the constrained model to 2.08 with the recruits (Table [2](#page-7-0)).

--- SENTENCE 261 ---
These results are significant as they suggest that the rVBGF produces parameter values that better represent growth, especially growth of the ascending leg of the growth trajectory.

--- SENTENCE 262 ---
In turn, this indicates that the rVBGF parameters (especially parameter L1) are not as sensitive to the presence or absence of young individuals in the sample as VBGF parameter K and that K is thus a poor descriptor of fish growth and should be avoided when comparing populations.

--- SENTENCE 263 ---
A possible explanation for this difference in parameter stability between the two parameterizations of the VBGF may lie in the fact that rVBGF parameters are expressions of mean size-at-age, which may thus be inherently more stable than the curvature coefficient K.

Finally, we tested the potential effect of combining daily ages for recruits together with annual ages of older fish in parameter calculations by converting all annual ages to fractions of a year that reflect their month of capture, thus aligning the age of adult individuals with that of the recruits, which were estimated in days and later expressed as a fraction of years.

--- SENTENCE 264 ---
In the present study, there was little difference in the parameter values between the two types of age calculation (except when the curves were unconstrained and without the aged recruits from the present study), and therefore the effect of using whole ages of older fish as opposed to fractional ages was negligible (Table

--- SENTENCE 265 ---
Given that the majority of demographic studies of coral reef fishes use whole ages, we believe that using whole ages for older fish may provide a better platform for comparison in future studies.

--- SENTENCE 266 ---
In conclusion, we advocate that (1) Francis' [\(1988](#page-10-0)) reparameterization of the VBGF (rVBGF) be preferentially used to model square growth in fish (over the traditional VBGF equation) and that (2) fixing age-at-length zero to PLD be used as the preferred method to constrain growth models when lacking adequate numbers of age-0 fish (for both the VBGF and rVBGF).

--- SENTENCE 267 ---
We believe these two recommendations are of particular significance when the aim is to produce estimates of growth rate (mean size-at-age instead of K) or to compare growth values between populations.

--- SENTENCE 268 ---
As the rVBGF and VBGF are two parameterizations of the same growth function, the issues we address with these recommendations are that of biological interpretability and statistical properties of the growth parameters.

--- SENTENCE 269 ---
Given the widespread use of the traditional VBGF parameters (in particular parameters K and L?) in fisheries modeling and related areas, however, we believe that the most sensible approach will be one of documenting the VBGF in combination with the rVBGF parameters, and selecting the function that will be most appropriate for the questions under analysis.

--- SENTENCE 270 ---
Such factors are essential to an understanding of the biology of square growth coral reef fishes and to management efforts to assess or regulate their harvest.

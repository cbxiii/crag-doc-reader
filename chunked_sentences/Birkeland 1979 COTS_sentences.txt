#### ABSTRACT

Outbreaks of adult Acanthaster planci have appeared at irregular intervals, arriving three years after extra heavy rains or after a record drought precedes a heavy rain near the beginning of the Acanthaster breeding season.

--- SENTENCE 9 ---
Acanthaster outbreaks follow typhoons which bring heavy rains, but they do not follow "dry" typhoons of equivalent wind force.

--- SENTENCE 10 ---
Outbreaks occur around the high islands in the Carolines, but not around the atolls at intermediate locations in the Carolines.

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
Phytoplankton blooms appear at the beginning of the rainy season in bays with large watersheds off high islands and these are the initial sites of Acanthaster abundance on Guam.

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
The breeding seasons of Acanthaster occur at the beginning of the rainy season on both sides of the equator, and the larvae of Acanthaster are adapted to salinities as low as  $30^{\circ}/_{\circ\circ}$ .

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
We present an hypothesis that on rare occasions, terrestrial runoff from heavy rains following the dry season may provide enough nutrients to stimulate an adequate phytoplankton bloom to produce enough food for Acanthaster larvae to result in an outbreak of adults three years later.

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
The hypothesis predicts an outbreak at Saipan in 1981 and at Palau in 1982.

--- SENTENCE 15 ---
The place of <u>Acanthaster</u> in the cultures of some Micronesians,

Melanesians, and Polynesians from high islands suggest that <u>Acanthaster</u>

outbreaks are a natural phenomenon that recurs at irregular intervals.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
However, we have not yet found evidence that the coral communities ultimately depend on <u>Acanthaster</u> outbreaks and we have found no predator that is totally dependent on an abundance of <u>Acanthaster</u> for food.

## E fofo e le alamea le alamea

Samoan proverb: "The cure for Acanthaster is Acanthaster"

#### INTRODUCTION

The crown-of-thorns starfish, Acanthaster planci or alamea, is a part of Samoan culture.

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
Alamea is scarce and widely scattered in its distribution for decades at a time.

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
Occasionally, however, there are abrupt increases in abundance.

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
During these times of extreme abundance, fishermen are reluctant to go fishing on the reefs at night for fear of stepping on the alamea and being stung by the spines.

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
If they step on alamea, they are advised by the Samoan proverb to turn over the alamea and step on the mouth and tube feet.

--- SENTENCE 22 ---
The mouth and tube feet are reputed in the legend to suck out the spines or the toxin.

--- SENTENCE 23 ---
Despite the familiarity of the <u>alamea</u> in legend, which implies that <u>alamea</u> may have been prevalent at certain previous times (see also Appendix A), <u>Acanthaster</u> has rarely been seen for decades prior to 1977.

--- SENTENCE 24 ---
Vaolui of Alofau has been a fishermen-farmer since 1958 and has been on the reef flat nearly everyday for 21 years.

--- SENTENCE 25 ---
He didn't see a single <u>Acanthaster</u> until he found one in 1972.

--- SENTENCE 26 ---
Never in his lifetime has he seen an outbreak of <u>Acanthaster</u> such as is occurring now.

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
Leuila Alaimaleata has been a fisherman-farmer at Alofau all his life (68 years).

--- SENTENCE 28 ---
He said that <u>Acanthaster</u> was abundant in both shallow (reef flat) and deep (reef front) water in 1916, but between 1916 and 1978 the <u>Acanthaster</u> were very scarce.

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
Upuese Taifane of Poloa told us that <u>alamea</u> were abundant around 1932 but were scattered and scarce ever since.

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
Chief Faiaipa'u of Onenoa said that before the present outbreak in 1978 they were never abundant in his lifetime, but he remembers from the talk of his elders that they were

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
The familiarity of <u>alamea</u> in proverbs and legend is probably because of these past events, but adult <u>Acanthaster</u> may have been very scarce and scattered from at least 1938 until late 1977.

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
The increase in abundance of <u>Acanthaster</u> was very abrupt.

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
In extensive diving around Tutuila (American Samoa) for 3 years prior to 1977, Dr. Richard C. Wass observed a total of no more than 6 <u>Acanthaster</u>.

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
At the suggestion of Patrick G. Bryan, Fisheries Officer, and the invitation of Henry Sesepasara, Director of the Office of Marine Resources, Government of American Samoa, we visited American Samoa to do a study on the <u>Acanthaster</u> infestation.

--- SENTENCE 39 ---
In this report we will summarize the information available and state our conclusions concerning the possible causes of the <u>Acanthaster</u> infestations, the nature of the processes of coral reef recovery, and comment on the approaches to be taken in handling the problems.

--- SENTENCE 60 ---

--- SENTENCE 61 ---
The population is most likely to have resulted from an especially successful year class that originated in January or February of 1975.

## Widespread Nature of the Population Increase

In late 1977, large groups of <u>Acanthaster</u> were found moving inshore from deep water on both the north coast of Tutuila (Fagatuitui Cove) and off the south coast of Tutuila (Taema Banks).

--- SENTENCE 63 ---
The largest group (384,477 +)

was first seen in May 1978, moving into Aoa and Onenoa Bays at the northeast tip of Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 64 ---
Alex Banse, a resident of Western Samoa, informed us that the first appearance of large numbers of Acanthaster occurred on Western Samoa also during 1977.

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
This appearance of Acanthaster in large numbers at approximately the same time in such widely separated locations suggests that we should not expect to trace the origin of this population increase to a particular reef, river mouth or other specific location.

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
It seems far more likely that the cause of the population exposion will be traced ultimately to some meteorological, hydrographic or other widespread environmental phenomenon (and the abruptness of this population increase of 3 year old adults suggests that this phenomenon took place about 3 years before 1977).

--- SENTENCE 67 ---
The widespread nature of origin of <u>Acanthaster</u> infestations also characterized the recent infestation in Palau.

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
By following the changes in location of the centers of abundance of <u>Acanthaster</u> and the increases in extent of damage to <u>Acropora</u>, the infestations seem to have started at discrete locations, widely separated by Urukthapel (Ngeruktabel), a very long island (Birkeland 1979).

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
Although <u>Acanthaster</u> outbreaks are concentrated on a geographic scale, occurring in some island groups and not in others, the outbreaks appear to start at several locations within these island groups subjected to similar climatic conditions.

## Influence of Rainfall and Terrestrial Runoff on Coral Reef Systems

Marsh (1977:334) noted that phytoplankton "are usually associated with the beginning of the rainy season.

--- SENTENCE 122 ---
It is probable that heavy rains coming after an extended dry season wash a pulse of nutrients, especially phosphorus, off the watershed and stimulate a bloom.

--- SENTENCE 123 ---
Eventually, as the most easily available nutrients was off the land, the runoff water becomes

more dilute and the plankton bloom in the bay dies out.

--- SENTENCE 124 ---
With the onset of the dry season, accumulation of easily leachable nutrients begins again on the watershed and the seasonal cycle is repeated."

--- SENTENCE 125 ---
Marsh (1977) further noted that no phytoplankton blooms have been observed in Pago Bay which has a generally smaller watershed and a lower input of groundwater than Tumon Bay.

--- SENTENCE 126 ---
Phytoplankton blooms have been recorded in Tumon Bay as far back as the earliest Spanish occupation of Guam.

--- SENTENCE 127 ---
It is interesting to note that Acanthaster infestations have not been seen originating in Pago Bay, but the most abundant Acanthaster at this time are at the north end of Tumon Bay, near San Vitores cut, where Marsh did his study.

--- SENTENCE 128 ---
Acanthaster infestations seem to have generally started around Agana and Tumon Bays, large bays with large watersheds.

--- SENTENCE 129 ---
Acanthaster Outbreaks Around High and Low Islands

. . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . .

--- SENTENCE 130 ---
Marsh (1977) found that phytoplankton blooms around Guam, a high island, were associated with availability of nitrate-nitrogen and reactive phosphorus.

--- SENTENCE 131 ---
He also found that his values for nitrate-nitrogen levels around Guam, a high island, were over an order of magnitude higher than those found by Webb et al. (1975) around Enewetak, an atoll or low island.

--- SENTENCE 132 ---
Further, he found slightly higher values of reactive phosphorus around Guam than Pilson and Betzer (1973) found around Enewetak.

--- SENTENCE 133 ---
He went on to predict that reefs around high islands might generally be more heavily influenced by terrestrial runoff of nutrients than would low islands or atolls.

--- SENTENCE 134 ---
In the previous two sections, we have seen that Acanthaster outbreaks generally occur in bays with a large watershed and three years after periods of heavy rain, both characterized by terrestrial runoff.

--- SENTENCE 135 ---
If

terrestrial runoff of nitrate-nitrogen and reactive phosphorus is the factor which favors an Acanthaster outbreak, then we would predict that Acanthaster outbreaks would occur more frequently around high islands or continents than around atolls.

--- SENTENCE 136 ---
Indeed, Tutuila, Western Samoa, Guam, Palau, Ponape and Okinawa are all high islands and the infestation of the Great Barrier Reef appeared to have begun near the continental coast of Queensland, Australia (ARC 1975).

--- SENTENCE 137 ---
Infestations have been found around the high islands of Palau (Chesher 1969a, b; Marsh and Tsuda 1973; Birkeland 1979) but not around Kayangel, the atoll of Palau (Marsh and Tsuda 1973).

--- SENTENCE 138 ---
Acanthaster outbreaks around high islands, we can subject the data of Table 1 in Marsh and Tsuda (1973) to a chi-square test.

--- SENTENCE 139 ---
Marsh and Tsuda summarized findings of surveys and resurveys of various islands around Micronesia by University of Guam, Trust Territory, and Westinghouse teams.

--- SENTENCE 140 ---
They categorized the islands as high islands or atolls and categorized the reef conditions on a scale from 1 to 6.

## Influence of Rainfall on Larval Biology of Acanthaster

The main breeding season of <u>Acanthaster</u> south of the equator is

December-February (Lucas 1973).

--- SENTENCE 162 ---
The main breeding season of <u>Acanthaster</u>

north of the equator is June-July (Yamazato and Kiyan 1973).

--- SENTENCE 163 ---
In both

regions, these periods begin about a month after the beginning of the rainy season.

--- SENTENCE 164 ---
The larvae of Acanthaster appear to be adapted to surprisingly low salinity levels for an echinoderm.

--- SENTENCE 165 ---
In fact, laboratory experiments by Lucas (1973) demonstrate that the survival of larvae increases as the salinity is lowered, at least to  $30^{\circ}/\circ\circ$ .

--- SENTENCE 166 ---
According to the Advisory Committee on Research (A.C.R.), Pearson and Lucas identified a mechanism for the outbreaks of <u>Acanthaster</u> by combining the breeding season of <u>Acanthaster</u> with the increased survival of larvae at lower salinity levels.

--- SENTENCE 167 ---
"Thus, the right combination of heavy run-off (lowering salinity), light wind conditions (preventing mixing with more saline water) and an abundance of <u>A. planci</u> larvae could lead to a high survival rate in larvae and subsequent expansion of juvenile and adult populations" (A.C.R. 1975:16).

--- SENTENCE 168 ---
Pearson and co-workers found optimum salinity levels as a result of runoff in reef waters near the coast of Queensland where infestations were common.

--- SENTENCE 169 ---
We agree that heavy runoff is favorable for the survival of Acanthaster larvae, but we think the main benefit of the runoff is not the lower salinity itself (a proximate adaptation), but that it ultimately provides more nutrients (nitrate-nitrogen and reactive phosphotes) which stimulate phytoplankton blooms which, in turn, provide food for Acanthaster larvae.

--- SENTENCE 170 ---
The breeding season of Acanthaster coincides with the beginning of the rainy season and the larvae are adapted to relatively low salinities most likely as adaptations to place larvae in situations of maximum likelihood for ample nourishment.

--- SENTENCE 171 ---
Lucas (1974) determined the food requirements of <u>Acanthaster</u> larvae by raising larvae in aquaria with six different concentrations of a green falgellate and six concentrations of a diatom.

--- SENTENCE 172 ---
The greatest percentage of larvae survived to late brachiolaria stage at 5,000 diatom cells/ml.

--- SENTENCE 173 ---
Lucas referred to Marshall (1933) as the only comprehensive study of phytoplankton on the Great Barrier Reef.

--- SENTENCE 174 ---
Marshall sampled phytoplankton for almost a year and found most samples to contain phytoplankton at very low concentrations, about 2 cells/ml.

--- SENTENCE 175 ---
In a few samples, at irregular intervals, phytoplankton reached maximum concentrations of about 170 cells/ml, and these were mainly diatoms.

--- SENTENCE 176 ---
The maximum phytoplankton densities found in coral reef waters are a small fraction of the minimum densities required to support Acanthaster larvae.

--- SENTENCE 177 ---
For this reason, we suggest that Acanthaster plagues are initiated when extra heavy rainfall occurs, especially when breaking an extensive drought.

--- SENTENCE 178 ---
The heavy rains result in nutrients being washed into the sea, stimulating a relatively dense phytoplankton bloom.

--- SENTENCE 179 ---
When this coincides with the breeding season of Acanthaster, an extraordinary proportion of the larvae may survive starvation, resulting in an infestation three years later.

--- SENTENCE 180 ---
Moore (1978) pointed out that Acanthaster planci is a long-lived species with a very high reproductive potential.

--- SENTENCE 181 ---
The long life of adults allows the species to persist for long periods between times in which environmental conditions favor larval survival.

--- SENTENCE 182 ---
When the conditions favoring larval survival occur, the high reproductive potential of adults produces a massive settlement and large fluctuations in local populations.

--- SENTENCE 183 ---
We hypothesize that these conditions that favor larval survival include, in part, a large phytoplankton bloom resulting from extra heavy rainfall at the beginning of a

rainy season.

--- SENTENCE 184 ---
The breeding season of <u>Acanthaster</u> and the salinity tolerance of their larvae increase the chance of the larvae being present when the phytoplankton bloom occurs.

--- SENTENCE 185 ---
## Other Marine Species that Correlate Abundance with Runoff

Sutcliffe (1972, 1973) showed that correlations exist between the amounts of land drainage or river discharges and fish and lobster catches.

--- SENTENCE 186 ---
As with the 3-year lag between the terrestrial runoff that favors larval Acanthaster and the appearance of a plague of adult Acanthaster, the correlations of river discharges are found if lag periods are considered to account for age at maturity or age at commercially harvestable size.

--- SENTENCE 187 ---
The timing of the runoff is important.

--- SENTENCE 188 ---
He found that the runoff data to be analyzed for the correlation must be taken from early in the reproductive season for the species under consideration, just as we must remember that relevant rainfall data for the Acanthaster study are 6 months apart on the two sides of the equator in consideration of the different reproductive seasons in the two hemispheres.

--- SENTENCE 189 ---
Sutcliffe (1973) found that the major effect of land drainage was in increased production of larval stages, probably through increased primary production.

--- SENTENCE 190 ---

# Other Plagues Resulting from Changes in Rainfall and Added Nutrition for Larvae

White (1976) reviewed the literature on 7 species of locusts from around the world.

--- SENTENCE 192 ---
As with <u>Acanthaster</u>, he found that very young locusts usually have an inadequate or too thinly dispersed food supply and most die off before reaching maturity.

--- SENTENCE 193 ---

the very young locusts.

--- SENTENCE 194 ---
An increased survival of very young locusts later results in a plague.

--- SENTENCE 195 ---

## Fluctuating Densities of Marine Invertebrates with Planktotrophic Larvae

--- SENTENCE 197 ---
Although "plague" is a term reserved for invertebrates such as

Acanthaster and locusts that are harmful to man economically, many marine invertebrates are characterized by irregular outbreaks or swarms (Coe 1956).

--- SENTENCE 198 ---
Many marine invertebrates produce a tremendous "surplus" of larvae, the vast majority of which perish before metamorphosis.

--- SENTENCE 199 ---
White (1976) hypothesized that the major influence on the abundance of herbivorous insects is a relative shortage of food for the very young; that food is usually too thinly dispersed.

--- SENTENCE 200 ---
Both White (1976) and Wolda (1978a) present evidence that fluctuations in populations of tropical herbivorous insects are strongly affected by irregularities in rainfall.

--- SENTENCE 201 ---
We suggest that this situation is generally the same for shallow-water marine invertebrates with planktotrophic larvae.

--- SENTENCE 202 ---
Outbreaks occur when runoff of terrestrial nutrients coincide with the reproductive season of the marine invertebrates.

--- SENTENCE 203 ---
It is sometimes asserted that "pronounced short-term fluctuations in population densities are not features of the population ecology of specialized coral reef species and A. planci can be shown to be such a specialized coral reef species" (Endean 1977:188).

--- SENTENCE 204 ---
The definition used for the term "specialized" (Endean 1974) is not definitive enough to clearly include or exclude most species, but if we consider tropical marine invertebrates with planktotrophic larvae, we find that most species are characterized by great year-to-year fluctuations in population densities.

--- SENTENCE 205 ---
For example, while we

were on Tutuila, we found concentrations (about 50 seen per half hour) of small (6 to 9 cm total diameter) Linckia laevigata at two locations: one at Austin Lambert's study site near the Governor's house on Coconut Point north of the airport and the other between the Rainmaker Hotel and the big square cement block due east on the reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 206 ---
As Yamaguchi (1973) pointed out, Linckia laevigata is similar to Acanthaster in that it is long-lived and only large adults are usually found.

--- SENTENCE 207 ---
Recruitment is very important, probably because of low survival of pelagic stages.

--- SENTENCE 208 ---
While on Tutuila, we noted an abundant recruitment of small (ca. 10 cm) Stichopus chloronotus on the reef flat at Masefau Bay.

--- SENTENCE 209 ---

--- SENTENCE 210 ---
Marsh et al. (1977) noted that an extensive settlement of Diadema and Echinothrix occurred in 1973 and the abundant year class of 1973 was still prevalent in 1977 (and perhaps longer).

--- SENTENCE 211 ---
The prevalence of these year classes is because of the irregularity of heavy recruitment.

--- SENTENCE 212 ---
Peter Frank (1969) characterized tropical gastropods as being long-lived with irregular reproductive success.

--- SENTENCE 213 ---
We do not know of any tropical marine invertebrate with planktotrophic larvae that has regular and nonfluctuating patterns of recruitment.

--- SENTENCE 214 ---
Endean (1977:188) argues that A. planci infestations are not periodic or cyclic because coral reef systems are regarded as having "a particularly stable or predictable organization because they are biologically accommodated."

--- SENTENCE 215 ---
We suggest that coral reef systems might fluctuate as much as temporate marine systems, but the fluctuations of small motile invertebrates have been unnoticed and unstudied while the fluctuations in coral and sponge populations are on a long-term time scale because of the longevity of individuals.

--- SENTENCE 216 ---
Tropical insect populations were assumed to fluctuate less than temperate insect populations on the basis of the diversity-stability hypothesis.

--- SENTENCE 217 ---
Once comparative data were obtained from the tropical rain forest (Wolda 1977, 1978b), it was found that there was no empirical basis for the theoretical assumption of less fluctuation of invertebrates in tropical rain forests.

--- SENTENCE 218 ---
We predict that the same might hold for coral reef systems once data are obtained.

--- SENTENCE 219 ---
In summary, Acanthaster planci is typical of many large coral reef invertebrates with planktotrophic larvae in being long-lived with very irregular reproductive success.

--- SENTENCE 220 ---
Many tropical marine invertebrates with planktotrophic larvae are also characterized by irregular outbreaks but their effects on the coral reef system go unnoticed by man.

--- SENTENCE 221 ---
In this report, we hypothesize that the degree of larval survival depends mainly on the availability of sufficient phytoplankton of the proper quality and that this is correlated with amount of terrestrial runoff which is correlated with amount of rainfall at the breeding season.

--- SENTENCE 343 ---
Arborescent <u>Acropora</u> often spread by the reattachment of broken branches.

--- SENTENCE 344 ---
The widely spaced and lengthy branches readily wedge into crevices in the reef framework or between the branches of other arborescent, cespitose, or foliaceous corals.

--- SENTENCE 345 ---
They have two mechanisms for reattachment.

--- SENTENCE 346 ---
The <u>Acropora</u> recements itself by growing onto the barren substratum from one end of the branch or, if it has fallen onto a living coral, the coral into which it is wedged grows up onto the branch of the <u>Acropora</u> and cements the two corals together.

--- SENTENCE 347 ---
The easily broken, easily wedged, and reattached branches of arborescent Acropora may allow these species to gain a refuge in size when invading an area and to bypass some of the main problems of coral recruitment by planulation.

--- SENTENCE 348 ---
Planulae have a very difficult time recruiting into an algal turf because until the small coral reaches a refuge in size, it can easily be smothered by algae or by the sediment traps created by the filamentous algae (Birkeland 1977).

--- SENTENCE 349 ---
Planulae also have a difficult time settling on another coral or onto an extensive patch of living corals because the coral could eat the planulae and because a planula probably cannot attach to living tissue.

--- SENTENCE 350 ---
Acropora branches can invade these areas because they have a refuge in size from the sediment, the algae and other corals.

--- SENTENCE 351 ---
Although the lower surfaces or the parts of the branches that are wedged into the substrata die, the exposed surfaces live and continue to grow.

--- SENTENCE 352 ---
In cases where there is enough surge to provide a zone of refuge from Acanthaster, the Acropora appears to be the coral most rapid in reinvading the area if the surge zone refuge exists.

--- SENTENCE 353 ---
Of course there is no reason to interpret this as an adaptation by Acropora to Acanthaster.

--- SENTENCE 354 ---
Acanthaster outbreaks are irregular and Acanthaster is generally scarce.

--- SENTENCE 355 ---
The problems

planulae have with the algal turf, sediment and living corals are ubiquitous and the tendencies of Acropora to fragment and reattach are most likely to be adaptations to these factors.

--- SENTENCE 356 ---
The pocilloporids, poritids, and agariciids generally spread by planulation as evidenced by themoften showing up on settling plates.

--- SENTENCE 357 ---
When branches or nubbins of <u>Pocillopora damicornis</u> are attached to terra cotta bricks and transplanted to areas where the species is not normally found, planulae settle and groups of new colonies start on the sides of the bricks.

--- SENTENCE 358 ---
One hundred forty nine <u>P. damicornis</u> colonies started on the side of ten small (22 x 10 x 6 cm) terra cotta bricks in Ylig Bay on Guam. <u>P. damicornis</u> nubbins were attached to the upper surfaces of these bricks, but the species was absent or very scarce in the surrounding environment.

--- SENTENCE 359 ---
The relevance of this example is that very small surviving colonies are probably important as "seed" colonies.

--- SENTENCE 360 ---
Other than the <u>Acropora</u> branches, which probably fell from the surge zone after the <u>Acanthaster</u> left, less than 5% of the substratum was occupied by living coral colonies.

--- SENTENCE 361 ---
Most of these survivors were in crevices, at the tips of otherwise dead arborescent colonies, or on vertical surfaces.

--- SENTENCE 362 ---
We are not sure whether the importance of these "seed" colonies is in producing larvae, in attracting larvae of their own species from the plankton, or both, but the importance of these small survivors is probably disproportionate to their prevalence.

--- SENTENCE 363 ---
In order to test the importance of "seed" populations and Acropora branches for the speed of recovery of the coral communities, we transplanted branches of Pocillopora and Acropora into devastated areas.

--- SENTENCE 364 ---
The rate of

colonization of corals in the immediate area of transplants can then be compared with the rate of colonization into other nearly areas.

--- SENTENCE 365 ---
We might expect more <u>Pocillopora</u> planulae to settle in the immediate area of the transplanted <u>Pocillopora</u> branches.

--- SENTENCE 366 ---
We expect the <u>Acropora</u> to reattach and grow.

--- SENTENCE 367 ---
It might be important whether the corymbose Acropora land right side up or upside down so we transplanted some of them right side up and others upside down to see if this affects their survival and reattachment.

--- SENTENCE 368 ---
Transplant experiments were set up at Siufaga Point, at Alofau, and about halfway between Aualili Point and Leutu Point.

--- SENTENCE 369 ---
These sites are labeled "A", "B", and "C", respectively, in Figure 1 in Appendix C. At Siufaga Point, we transplanted 32 branches of Acropora acuminata, 11 pieces of A. hyacinthus right side up and 4 A. hyacinthus upside down.

--- SENTENCE 370 ---
At Alofau, we transplanted 38 branches of A. acuminata, 4 A. abrotanoides, 11 A. hyacinthus right side up, 4 A. hyacinthus upside down and 7 Pocillopora.

--- SENTENCE 371 ---
Between Aualili Point and Leutu Point, we transplanted 32 A. acuminata, 5 A. abrotanoides, 12 A. hyacinthus right side up, 6 A. hyacinthus upside down and 6 Pocillopora.

--- SENTENCE 372 ---
Unfortunately, a few <u>Acanthaster</u> could move through any of these sites again and wipe out the transplant experiments, so transplant experiments have been set up on Guam where they are relatively free of <u>Acanthaster</u> and can be kept under surveilance.

--- SENTENCE 373 ---
Transplant experiments have also been set up at three sites on Palau (Birkeland 1979).

## ARE THERE ADAPTATIONS TO ACANTHASTER INFESTATIONS?

--- SENTENCE 375 ---
Although Acropora is the preferred prey of Acanthaster, it reinvades a devastated area more rapidly than other corals if it has a refuge in the surge zone.

--- SENTENCE 376 ---
However, we wish to stress that we do not claim that this refuge or manner of reinvasion is an adaptation to Acanthaster infestations.

--- SENTENCE 377 ---
The invasion of areas below the surge zone by the breaking and reattaching of branches is probably an adaptation for invading an algal turf or a stand of other corals.

--- SENTENCE 378 ---
The algae and corals are a perpetual problem for planulae, and Acanthaster infestations are a very irregular and rare event that occurs mainly on high islands rather than atolls.

--- SENTENCE 379 ---
Acanthaster may be too rare or irregular to be a strong selective force.

--- SENTENCE 380 ---
The adaptations for invading an algal turf may provide a preadaptation for Acanthaster infestations.

--- SENTENCE 381 ---
Whether some species are adapted to <u>Acanthaster</u> to the extent that <u>Acanthaster</u> is depended upon for their survival is relevant to the question of whether we should let <u>Acanthaster</u> infestations run their course.

<u>Acanthaster</u> has many predators, but it is not the preferred prey for any of them.

--- SENTENCE 382 ---
<u>Charonia tritonis</u> prefers several other asteroids.

--- SENTENCE 383 ---
<u>Cassis cornuta</u> usually feeds on irregular urchins.

--- SENTENCE 384 ---
<u>Cymatorium lotorium and Murex sp. will</u> probably take other small echinoderms or molluscs if <u>Acanthaster</u> are scarce.

--- SENTENCE 385 ---
<u>Bursa rubeta</u> will probably take other small echinoderms or polychaetes if <u>Acanthaster</u> are scarce.

--- SENTENCE 386 ---
<u>Hymenocera picta</u> prefers smaller asteroids (Glynn 1977), especially ophidiasterids.

--- SENTENCE 387 ---
<u>Dromidiopsis dormia</u> can most likely survive on other small echinoderm prey.

--- SENTENCE 388 ---
<u>Kanthid crabs and Dardanus sp. are usually omnivorous</u>.

--- SENTENCE 389 ---
The grouper <u>Pomicrops lanceolatus</u> will probably take small fish and crustaceans if <u>Acanthaster</u> are scarce.

--- SENTENCE 390 ---
Although <u>Balistoides viridescens</u> and <u>Arothron hispidus</u> were observed near <u>Acanthaster</u> in Samoa,

we never saw one attack an <u>Acanthaster</u>.

--- SENTENCE 391 ---
Ormond and Campbell (1974) document the predation of <u>Arothron hispidus</u>, <u>Balistoides viridescens</u>, and <u>Psendobalistes flavimarginatus</u> on <u>Acanthaster</u>, but according to the information from Hiatt and Strasburg (1960), each of these predators can take other invertebrates.

--- SENTENCE 392 ---
<u>Cheilinus undulatus</u> will feed on crustaceans, molluscs, and other echinoderms as well as <u>Acanthaster</u> (Randall et al. 1978).

--- SENTENCE 393 ---
In summary, we do not know of any predator that depends on <u>Acanthaster</u> and, in fact, we do not know of any predator for which <u>Acanthaster</u> is the preferred prey.

--- SENTENCE 394 ---
Including predators, parasites and commensals, about 34 associates have been listed for <u>Acanthaster planci</u> (Eldredge, in Tsuda 1972:15-17; Cannon 1972).

--- SENTENCE 395 ---
Most of these are associates of other species as well, but there are protozoa (Cannon 1972), a copepod (Humes 1970), and a parasitic flatworm (Cannon 1974) that may be restricted to <u>Acanthaster planci</u>.

--- SENTENCE 396 ---
These associates still may not require an abundance of <u>Acanthaster</u> for survival.

--- SENTENCE 397 ---
Acanthaster, is the fastest growing coral, disruption of growth of Acropora patches by Acanthaster infestations might ultimately result in greater coral diversity.

--- SENTENCE 398 ---
Coral communities on atolls tend to be of lower diversity, larger monotypic patches, and more distinctly zoned than coral communities around high islands, and Acropora spp.

--- SENTENCE 399 ---
tend to be predominant on atolls.

--- SENTENCE 400 ---
Since Acanthaster infestations are more common around high islands, we might conclude the differences between high islands and atoll reef communities to be a result of previous infestations.

--- SENTENCE 401 ---
We doubt that this conclusion will hold up to further study.

--- SENTENCE 402 ---
Acropora is often predominant around high islands such as Tutuila, Palau and many others and the greater diversity of coral communities may just as likely be the result of more variable

habitats, coastlines, nutrient flow patterns, and other features of the physical environment of high islands.

--- SENTENCE 403 ---
Furthermore, atolls in the Marshalls tend to be further from the center of coral dispersion than high islands in Carolines and Melanesia.

--- SENTENCE 404 ---
Outlying high islands, such as the Marquesas, are relatively depauperate in corals (Chevalier 1978).

--- SENTENCE 405 ---
A lot of research should be undertaken to examine and explain differences in reef communities on high and low islands, but to date we have no convincing evidence that anything is dependent upon or adapted to Acanthaster infestations.

--- SENTENCE 406 ---
Lucas and Jones (1976) argue that Acanthaster planci is a relatively recently evolved species.

--- SENTENCE 407 ---
Our observations cannot refute this claim.

--- SENTENCE 408 ---
However, the lack of adaptations to Acanthaster may be because of its recent arrival on the scene or, instead, because of the irregularity of its abundance.

--- SENTENCE 409 ---
(There are probably very few organisms adapted to 17-year locusts for this latter reason.)

--- SENTENCE 410 ---
Walsh et al. (1971) suggest that because <u>Acanthaster planci</u> infestations are probably a natural phenomenon, then infestations might be an essential part of the system and, in the long run, contribute to the growth of the reef.

--- SENTENCE 411 ---
Therefore, they conclude, it might be best to let nature take its course and leave the infestations alone.

--- SENTENCE 412 ---
However, James A. Marsh (pers.

--- SENTENCE 413 ---
comm.) commented that much of the nutrients in a coral reef system are bound up in the corals and recycled.

--- SENTENCE 414 ---
If coral communities are devastated beyond a certain point, much of what had accumulated over years might be lost from the system, and recovery might be relatively slow.

--- SENTENCE 415 ---
We all agree that we need to learn more about the role of <u>Acanthaster</u> in the system.

--- SENTENCE 416 ---
It is possible that <u>Acanthaster</u> infestations are beneficial to coral reef communities through increasing the rate of turnover, but we are inclined

to question this hypothesis until more studies have been obtained because we can find no evidence of adaptations to <u>Acanthaster</u> infestations.

--- SENTENCE 417 ---
From the above considerations, we can see no reason why we should necessarily be required to let nature take its course if we have our own aethetic or economic reasons to reduce the abundance of <u>Acanthaster</u> in certain areas.

--- SENTENCE 418 ---
Studies on these matters should be undertaken, but as advised by Johannes (1975:42), until we know more about this matter, interim decisions should be based on what we know, rather than on how much we do not know.

#### RECOMMENDATIONS

The main conclusion of our study was that Acanthaster infestations are a natural phenomenon that recur suddenly at irregular intervals.

--- SENTENCE 419 ---
Extra heavy rains or strong rains following a drought are one of the major factors leading to the infestation.

--- SENTENCE 420 ---
We could consider ways to prevent future outbreaks if human activities were a major factor, but we cannot control the weather.

--- SENTENCE 421 ---
The known predators of Acanthaster all prefer other prey, so their introduction might do more harm than good.

--- SENTENCE 422 ---
Until we learn more about the system, we are limited to two courses of action, either manually reduce the size of the Acanthaster population or let nature take its course.

--- SENTENCE 423 ---
To decide which alternative to take is a very difficult decision that must be made by the local people or administration because they must weight the necessity of the task against the cost.

--- SENTENCE 424 ---
The task of significantly reducing the numbers of <u>Acanthaster</u> is usually overwhelming and expensive, yet if nature takes its course, there might be a relatively long wait for the coral reef community to return to its previous state or it might return to an alternate state.

--- SENTENCE 425 ---
Extensive efforts have previously been made to attempt to control the numbers of Acanthaster in Micronesia, but there is some doubt that these control measures had any significant effect.

--- SENTENCE 426 ---
When the Acanthaster left, they appeared to leave on their own.

--- SENTENCE 427 ---
For example, Richard C. Wass (in Tsuda 1972:25) testified that 68,793 Acanthaster were killed in Ponape but a subsequent survey found "that the density and locations of major concentrations have remained virtually unchanged".

--- SENTENCE 428 ---
James A. Marsh (In Tsuda 1972:24) reported that Acanthaster infestations had been apparently continuous in the Seventy Islands area of Palau to that time, despite longterm control efforts.

--- SENTENCE 429 ---
Although the total infestation of the greater Koror area of Palau in 1979 was estimated to consist of about 50,000 Acanthaster (Birkeland 1979), 57,000 were removed from one small area around the mouth of Malakal Channel (W. M. Hamner, pers.

--- SENTENCE 430 ---
The population was still there and the bounty money ran out.

--- SENTENCE 431 ---
The bounty system in American Samoa removed 486,933 Acanthaster but many thousand still remained during our visit.

--- SENTENCE 432 ---
Killing 486,933 <u>Acanthaster</u> at American Samoa and 57,000 in Palau in 1979 might have relieved some of the predation pressure from corals.

--- SENTENCE 433 ---
There was a general decline in <u>Acanthaster</u> populations in Micronesica between 1969 and 1972 and Marsh and Tsuda (1973) attributed this in part to control efforts; approximately 280,000 <u>Acanthaster</u> were killed by control teams.

--- SENTENCE 434 ---
However, <u>Acanthaster</u> also disappeared about as rapidly from areas in which no control efforts were ever carried out.

--- SENTENCE 435 ---
For example, no control efforts were made on Aguijan, yet the survey of August 1970 counted 3753 <u>Acanthaster</u> and the resurvey of October 1971 counted only a single starfish (Marsh and Tsuda 1973).

--- SENTENCE 436 ---
The control efforts at Ponape and at the Seventy Islands of Palau had little apparent effect, but the <u>Acanthaster</u> eventually left.

--- SENTENCE 437 ---
From these considerations, we would conclude that attempts to significantly reduce infestations of Acanthaster around an island by either control teams or a bounty system would be very expensive and the Acanthaster would probably eventually leave on their own after devastating the coral communities.

--- SENTENCE 438 ---
On the other hand, a devastated coral community will take several years to return to a similar state or it may develop into an alternate state.

--- SENTENCE 439 ---
The basic question here is whether the original coral community is worth the expense and effort to protect.

--- SENTENCE 440 ---
If a large hotel catered to spearfishermen, sport divers or underwater photographers, it would undoubtedly find it worth the expense to keep the main aggregations of Acanthaster away from its immediate area or preferred tourist diving or fishing sites.

--- SENTENCE 441 ---
For this reason, one of us recommended the bounty system for a recent outbreak in Palau (Birkeland 1979).

--- SENTENCE 442 ---
We must now bring up a delicate point that we cannot ignore.

--- SENTENCE 443 ---
The Samoans and Micronesians we have spoken with were mainly concerned with the bounty money rather than the state of the natural reef community.

--- SENTENCE 444 ---
Acanthaster outbreaks have occurred in the past, and the system eventually recovered.

--- SENTENCE 445 ---
A Palauan fisherman questioned the value of removing Acanthaster and described the value of the after-effects of the infestation (cf.

--- SENTENCE 446 ---
The Samoans we talked to were mainly concerned with territorial rights of families and villages in harvesting the Acanthaster for bounty.

--- SENTENCE 447 ---
Robert Owen, Chief Conservationist of Palau, described (pers.

--- SENTENCE 448 ---
how Palauans raised coconut rhinoceras beetles for bounty when the bounty system was initiated to protect the Palauan's coconut plantations.

--- SENTENCE 449 ---
One of us (C.E.B.) is concerned with the amount of time it takes for the coral community to recover (discussed in the paragraph below) and is convinced that reducing the abundance of Acanthaster will not harm the system

(cf.

--- SENTENCE 450 ---
preceding section of this report), but we acknowledge that an adequate control system would be expensive, and the effects of <u>Acanthaster</u> on the coral communities does not appear to be of concern to the residents of Samoa or Micronesia.

--- SENTENCE 451 ---
Since it appears that Acanthaster infestations are a recurring natural phenomenon, then we might predict that the coral communities will recover as they have in the past.

--- SENTENCE 452 ---
This may take a number of years and this delay may be economically harmful if a resort or hotel were involved.

--- SENTENCE 453 ---
On the other hand, if general environmental conditions have changed since the previous outbreak and recovery, the system may develop to an alternative stable community (Holling 1973, Sutherland 1974).

--- SENTENCE 454 ---
For example, if a change in runoff patterns favors an increased productivity of algae, this could inhibit recolonization by corals (Hedley 1925, Moorehouse 1936, Dawson 1959, Nishihira and Yamazato 1974, Johannes 1975).

--- SENTENCE 455 ---
After devastation of coral communities by Acanthaster predation, the devastated area is often replaced by algal communities.

--- SENTENCE 456 ---
Sometimes these alternative communities may dominate the area for considerable time, perhaps indefinitely (Nishihira and Yamazato 1974).

--- SENTENCE 457 ---
Only time will tell.

--- SENTENCE 458 ---
Examples of coral communities replaced by algal communities because of environmental changes are given by Dawson (1959) and Johannes (1975).

--- SENTENCE 459 ---
Examples are given by Johannes (1975) and Endean (1976) of reefs that showed little or no recovery of coral communities after 30 and 35 years.

--- SENTENCE 460 ---
Goreau (1969) hypothesized that the patchy distribution of reef communities may be a result of problems of recruitment.

--- SENTENCE 461 ---
The chance location of a reef may result when the coral community reaches a "critical mass", after which the community could perpetute itself until it developed into

a reef.

--- SENTENCE 462 ---
Those communities not reaching the "critical mass" soon enough might be set back or "damped out" by random events.

--- SENTENCE 463 ---
Reef recovery may not be such a problem on Samoa because the exposed fringing reefs have refuges for "seed populations" of corals from <u>Acanthaster</u> in the surge zone (cf. discussion in the section of this report on reef recovery).

--- SENTENCE 464 ---
If a control system is to be initiated, there are two methods that could be used: a bounty system or a control team.

--- SENTENCE 465 ---
With both methods, there has always been a problem with poor handling of daily kill records (Tsuda 1972).

--- SENTENCE 466 ---
In most bounty programs, there seems to have been a problem with inflated kill records.

--- SENTENCE 467 ---
As a typical example, we quote (from Tsuda 1972:33)

--- SENTENCE 468 ---
Dr. Gerald R. Allen from his written summary of the bounty program on Truk:

"I counted a total of 39 killed by a man I accompanied on one dive.

--- SENTENCE 469 ---
Upon surfacing this person reported a kill of 76 starfish.

--- SENTENCE 470 ---
The numbers that are turned in on the reports are probably indicative to a certain extent of the actual population, but may be inflated by as much as 20-50%."

Tsuda (1971) points out many inconsistencies in kill records.

--- SENTENCE 471 ---
This is a chronic problem.

--- SENTENCE 472 ---
We strongly recommend that if a bounty system is initiated, then the starfish killed for the bounty must always be counted by a fisheries officer or scientist.

--- SENTENCE 473 ---
If a control team is used, it should be remembered that enthusiasm for the project can wear off.

--- SENTENCE 474 ---
A responsible person must be in charge of control teams.

--- SENTENCE 475 ---
As an example, a Palauan control team spent about a month supposedly killing starfish on the small atoll of Kayangel.

--- SENTENCE 476 ---
An examination of kill records indicated that no starfish were ever killed there and the University of Guam survey team previously determined that Acanthaster was absent from

that atoll to begin with (Tsuda 1972:32).

--- SENTENCE 477 ---
However, if the above problems can be controlled with reasonable management procedures and if the island residents and administration consider the protection of their reef communities desirable enough to warrant the expense and effort, then the bounty system has a number of merits.

--- SENTENCE 478 ---
It is a self-regulating method of harvesting.

--- SENTENCE 479 ---
At \$0.15 per alamea, control will be most intense when alamea are abundant.

--- SENTENCE 480 ---
When or where the Acanthaster are scarce, searching for them would become impractical at that price.

--- SENTENCE 481 ---
Acanthaster are harmful only when they are abundant enough to be aggregated and exposed to view during the daytime, making collection by bounty hunters efficient.

--- SENTENCE 482 ---
When Acanthaster are aggregated, they more thoroughly devastate the reef, leaving larger patches in the community which take longer to recover.

--- SENTENCE 483 ---
Even small aggregations increase the probability of successful spawning by Acanthaster.

--- SENTENCE 484 ---
Most control effort could concentrate on aggregations and the bounty system promotes this approach.

--- SENTENCE 485 ---
Control teams lose interest and are expensive to maintain.

--- SENTENCE 486 ---
The bounty system only has a cost when expense is justified.

--- SENTENCE 487 ---
Another good aspect of the bounty system is that the money goes to interested local residents.

--- SENTENCE 488 ---
An alternative approach to the <u>Acanthaster</u> problem might be to let the <u>Acanthaster</u> go away on their own, then transplant seed populations of corals.

--- SENTENCE 489 ---
Whether this might be an effective method of increasing the speed of recovery might be indicated by the results of the transplant experiments described in a previous section of this report.

--- SENTENCE 490 ---
In summary, Acanthaster infestations are a naturally recurring phenomenon brought about in part by weather conditions that are indirectly favorable to Acanthaster larvae.

--- SENTENCE 491 ---
Controlling infestation of adult

Acanthaster is a very expensive and almost overwhelming project to attempt.

--- SENTENCE 492 ---
If managed reasonably carefully and if local residents and the administration consider the protection of their reef communities important enough to justify the expense and effort, then we would recommend the bounty system.

--- SENTENCE 493 ---
At \$0.15 per alamea, the bounty system would only be effective where Acanthaster are abundant enough to be a problem.

#### INTRODUCTION

Late in 1977 fishery biologists working for the Office of Marine Resources in American Samoa, reported that the "Crown-of-Thorns starfish" (Acanthaster planci (L.)) has caused severe damage to some local reefs around Tutuila Island.

--- SENTENCE 677 ---
This coral-eating starfish, known locally as "alalamea", was responsible for extensive coral kills on many reefs in the late nineteen-sixties throughout the tropical Pacific Ocean, and now appears to constitute a threat to the entire reef system of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 678 ---
Freshly killed corals and numerous Acanthaster planci starfish were first noticed in American Samoa during September 1977 on fringing reefs along the north coast at Fagatuitui Cove.

--- SENTENCE 679 ---
About 50 A. planci, ranging in size from 30-35 cm in diameter, were counted in November 1977 during a half-hour dive on the reef at this initial infestation site.

--- SENTENCE 680 ---
Severe damage to corals attributable to A. planci was next seen in December 1977 on the offshore Taema Bank along the southeast coast.

--- SENTENCE 681 ---
At this time, similar coral banks between Taema Bank and Anuu Island were reported to be free of A. planci infestation, although increased numbers of the starfish were being reported from the inshore fringing reefs along the south coast between Nuuuli and Aumi Villages.

--- SENTENCE 682 ---
These initial observations along with increasing reports of other reefs infested by A. planci prompted the Office of Marine Resources, Government of American Samoa, to conduct a field survey of reef areas to determine the distribution and abundance of the starfish and to assess the extent of the resulting coral damage around Tutuila Island and offshore coral banks.

#### **METHODS**

The principal field surveys used in this report were performed during a year-long period between January 1978 and January 1979.

--- SENTENCE 683 ---
The surveys were for the most part conducted by Dr. Richard C. Wass and Mr. Jan Swan of the Office of Marine Resources.

--- SENTENCE 735 ---
This total gives an average starfish density of  $1/28.7 \text{ m}^2$  for the three transects and on overall population for Sector II at 13000.

## Sector III

Sector III was surveyed on January 24, 1978 by running two parallel transects, 476 meters long, across Taema Bank on a bearing in line with Lepua Church and Breakers Point.

--- SENTENCE 736 ---
Corals on the backreef slope were mostly dead with less than 5 percent of them still living.

--- SENTENCE 737 ---
All the tabletop <u>Acropora</u> species, most of the arborescent <u>Acropora</u> patches, and all but a few scattered <u>Pocillopora</u> colonies were dead.

--- SENTENCE 738 ---
Only 27 A. planci were observed in this mostly dead coral zone.

--- SENTENCE 739 ---
Numbers of starfish increased dramatically on the shallower upper bank surface where 182 were counted along the two transects.

--- SENTENCE 740 ---
The

starfish were especially abundant along the seaward edge of the upper bank surface but upon reaching the forereef slope they were conspicously absent where a rubble substrate was encountered with few living or dead corals present.

--- SENTENCE 741 ---
A total of 209 A. planci were counted along the two transects of Sector III giving it an average starfish density of  $1/9.8 \text{ m}^2$  and on overall population of 68000.

## Sector IV

Sector IV was surveyed on the same date as Sector III.

--- SENTENCE 742 ---
A very large concentration of A. planci was found on the forereef slope of this sector.

--- SENTENCE 743 ---
The starfish were aggregated along the slope, parallel to the main axis of the bank reef, into a narrow band about 5-8 meters wide and 800 meters long similar to the classic "fronts" discribed by Chesher (1971).

--- SENTENCE 744 ---
Because of the concentration of starfish into a band, a quadrat method of measuring starfish density was used.

--- SENTENCE 745 ---
The starfish front extended from the eastern end of Taema Bank to a point in line with bearings between the television tower on Alava Mountain and the western tip of Breakers Point.

--- SENTENCE 746 ---
By plotting those bearings on a navigation chart the starfish front was estimated to be about 800 meters long.

--- SENTENCE 747 ---
The first quadrat survey was conducted on January 24, 1978 at a location along the starfish front in line with a bearing between the television tower on Alava Mountain and the navigation light on Breakers Point.

--- SENTENCE 748 ---
Two quadrats at this location averaged 400 starfish each.

--- SENTENCE 749 ---
On February 1, 1978 the starfish front was surveyed again at two more locations.

--- SENTENCE 750 ---
Two quadrats at the first of these two survey sites, located on a bearing in line with the television tower on Alava Mountain and the

top-most peak at Breakers Point, averaged 364 starfish inch.

--- SENTENCE 751 ---
At the second survey site, located on a bearing in line with the television tower on Alava Mountain and the middle part of Breakers Point, two quadrats averaged 340 starfish each.

--- SENTENCE 752 ---
Based upon densities from these three survey sites the average starfish density along the front was estimated at 15.9/m².

--- SENTENCE 753 ---
Using this average density value and on area 800 meters long by 6.5 meters wide, the total starfish front population was calculated to be about 80,000.

--- SENTENCE 754 ---
The A. planci front appeared to be moving up the reef slope and across the upper bank surface at Sector IV in a seaward to landward direction.

--- SENTENCE 755 ---
The white skeletons of the recently-eaten corals were obvious immediately behind the front.

--- SENTENCE 756 ---
The white coloration of the coral skeletons graded into yellow-green and finally brown-green as one proceeded seaward indicating increased algal growth and an increasing period of time since the starfish had passed.

--- SENTENCE 757 ---
Immediately landward of the front the corals were mostly living and free of starfish.

--- SENTENCE 758 ---
As the starfish front moved across the bank only about 80 percent of the corals were eaten, however, large numbers trailing behind were eating the remaining corals.

--- SENTENCE 759 ---
About 48,000 starfish were estimated to be trailing behind the actual front itself, which gives Sector IV a total population of 128,000.

--- SENTENCE 760 ---
At the time of the sector surveys the total A. planci population of Taema Bank was estimated to be about 212,000 with an estimated 80 to 90 percent of all the corals killed.

--- SENTENCE 761 ---
A resurvey of the eatern tip of Taema Bank (Sector IV) on April 18, 1978 revealed that the well established starfish front observed earlier in the year had broken up.

--- SENTENCE 762 ---
The breakup of the front was probably very recent as observers from Marine Environmental Research, Inc., reported numerous starfish there just three weeks earlier.

### Nafanua Bank Surveys

On February 6, 1978 divers were towed along the entire forereef slope of Nafanua Bank.

--- SENTENCE 780 ---
Most corals, including tabletop Acropora species, were alive.

--- SENTENCE 781 ---

--- SENTENCE 782 ---

--- SENTENCE 785 ---
Deeper parts of bank at this time were mostly dead, but regions shallower than 15 to 20 meters were still living and free of A. planci.

Taema Bank and patch reefs to the west.

--- SENTENCE 790 ---

--- SENTENCE 791 ---

--- SENTENCE 792 ---

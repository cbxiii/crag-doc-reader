The Impact of Acanthaster planci Coral Kills on the

Samoan Reef Fish Community

ABSTRACT-Effects of Acanthaster planci coral kills on the reef fish community was examined at Cape Larsen on Tutuila Island, American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 4 ---
Three areas that had been visually censused in the late 1970s by Richard Wass were chosen as study sights.

--- SENTENCE 5 ---
Cape Larsen was surveyed 3 times before A. planci reduced the live coral cover from 70% to less than 20% (visually estimated).

--- SENTENCE 6 ---
The pointquarter method was used and compared to the visual estimates made in the late 1970s at each location.

--- SENTENCE 7 ---
The visual estimates were approximately 40% higher than estimates by the point-quarter method.

--- SENTENCE 8 ---
The fish communities at Cape Larsen and Sita Bay were recensused by wass in 1985.

--- SENTENCE 9 ---
censused the 3 transects in 1987.

--- SENTENCE 10 ---
The pre A. planci data were pooled and compared to the pooled post A. planci data for Cape Larsen.

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
consequences of pooling the data are discussed.

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
Comparisons of 51 species were made.

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
Five species showed significant changes in abundance.

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
Two species closely associated with branching Acropora and pocillopora corals were reduced in abundance, one algal grazer increased in abundance, and two labrids changed (one up, one down) in abundance.

### INTRODUCTION

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
Acanthaster planci is the coralivorous starfish responsible for killing many live coral stands throughout the Pacific Ocean.

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
In November 1977, a dramatic population increase of A. planci was detected by Richard Wass on Tutuila Island, American Samoa (Birkeland 1982)(Figure 1).

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
Tutuila reefs are characteristically fringing reefs because of the steep topography.

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
Despite the removal of 486,933 crown-of-thorns starfish in less than one year after the outbreak (Birkeland 1982)(Figure 2), most of the live coral habitat surrounding Tutuila was destroyed.

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
Wass began assessing the reef fish community in American Samoa with transects in February 1977.

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
Some of the transects he established were repeated during and after the A. planci outbreak, providing a rare opportunity to assess the resultant changes in the reef fish community.

--- SENTENCE 22 ---
Wass (in Birkeland et al. 1985) found the coral associated damselfishes (pomacentridae) were reduced in American Samoa, and Williams (1986) found coralivorous butterflyfishes (chaetodontidae) of the Great Barrier Reef were reduced in number following A. planci perturbations.

--- SENTENCE 23 ---
This paper examines the influence of <u>A. planci</u> coral kills on the American Samoan reef fish community, after eight years, by comparing transect data taken before and after the 1978 disturbance.

--- SENTENCE 24 ---
Methods used in collecting and analyzing the data are scrutinized and discussed.

# METHODS AND MATERIALS

The transect locations are illustrated in Figure 3.

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
The Cape Larsen transect is located on a reef front along the 25 ft depth contour where the reef slope abruptly steepens.

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
The Sita Bay transect is located on the upper edge of the sloping reef front at a depth of 15-20 ft.

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
The Asili Point transect is located on a steeply sloping reef front along the 25 ft contour.

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
The transect corridor encompasses 1 m on either side of the depth contour and 2 m above the reef for 100 m.

Live coral cover was estimated for each transect.

--- SENTENCE 34 ---
In the transects Wass censused, the live coral cover and the genus composition were visually estimated.

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
In the transects I censused, the live coral cover and the genus composition was estimated by the point-quarter method recommended by Birkeland and Amesbury (Attachment A).

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
Twenty points (80 coral colonies) were sampled along each transect.

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
At Cape Larsen, along the 20 ft depth contour, I sampled at least 8 points (32 coral colonies) in each of two 30 m long replicates with the point-quarter method.

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
This location was previously sampled in April 1982 and April 1985 by Birkeland et al. (1985) with the same procedure, and is slightly different than the transect established by Wass.

--- SENTENCE 40 ---
Transect locations given by the numbers: 1. Cape Larsen, 2. Sita Bay, 3. Asili Point.

--- SENTENCE 41 ---
The fish cersusing for each 100 m X 2 m X 2 m transect was conducted by a scuba equipped observer following a weighted line laid about 10 minutes before along the depth contour.

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
Holes and crevices within the transect corridor were inspected for secretive and nocturnal species.

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
Approximately 30 minutes were required to enumerate the fishes on each transect.

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
Data were recorded on a tape recorder through a microphone in the mouthpiece of a regulator.

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
Wass waited 10-30 minutes, then spent 15-20 minutes seeking and listing species on an underwater slate that were not recorded during the census.

--- SENTENCE 46 ---
I immediately began a 15-20 minute search, recording additional species on the tape recorder.

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
Extremely diver wary species were recorded when entering the water.

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
The 15-20 minute searches were conducted within 20 m of the transect line and in the same depth range.

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
A. planci coral kill was compared using the Two Sample t' test because "the t' test is robust enough to stand considerable departures from its theoretical assumptions especially if the sample sizes are equal or nearly equal, and especially when two-tabled hypotheses are considered" (Zar 1974).

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
The data did not strictly meet the assumptions of the t' test - the variances were heterogenous and the data was not independent - but the sample sizes were equal and two-tabled hypotheses were tested.

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
Similar to Williams (1986), analyses were attempted only when a species occurs in the transect corridor at least 2 out of 3 times before or after the A. plancicoral kills.

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
If a species was being analyzed and it was not counted in the transect corridor, but was recorded as present ("P" in Tables 1, 2 and 3) in the following 15-20 minute search, then a value of 0.5 was given for each present status recorded.

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
The large number of comparisons led to a high probability of rejecting the hypothesis that the means were equal when they were equal (a Type I error)(Williams 1986).

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
The number of comparisons was multiplied by 0.05 to calculate the expected differences due to a Type I error and was subtracted from the observed differences to estimate the number of actual differences.

# RESULTS

The percent live coral cover for the 20 ft depth contour at Cape Larsen increases from 7.58% in April 1982 to 22.34% in April 1985 to 24.70% in October 1986.

--- SENTENCE 62 ---
The percent live coral cover visually estimated by Wass in the late 1970's compared to the point-quarter estimates in 1986 for each transect are respectively: Cape Larsen, 10-20% versus 7.8%; Sita Bay, 70% versus 52%; Asili Point, 70% versus 57%.

--- SENTENCE 63 ---
Cape Larsen's live coral cover is composed mostly of encrusting colonies of Montipora (21% by area) and Acropora (20%) with some Pocillopora (18%) colonies.

--- SENTENCE 64 ---
Sita Bay's live coral cover is 51% branching and table Acropora.

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
Asili Point's live coral cover is dominated by branching and table Acropora (93%).

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
The results from the fish censusing at Cape Larsen, Sita Bay and Asili Point are in Tables 1, 2 and 3 respectively.

--- SENTENCE 67 ---
There were 51 species abundant enough to be tested for changes occurring after the A. planci coral kill at Cape Larsen.

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
Five species (10% of the comparisons) differed significantly in abundance, but 2.5 differences were expected due to Type I errors (Table 4).

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
Only 2.5 differences are actual differences.

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
The point-quarter method was compared with 1m<sup>2</sup> plots for sampling live coral cover by Colgan (1981).

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
He demonstrated "sampling time was reduced without a reduction in the quality of the data."

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
The point-quarter method also yields consistent information when applied by different researchers.

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
The estimate I made in October1986 is consonant with the increase in live coral cover shown by Birkeland et al. (1985) with their estimates of April 1982 and April 1985 (Figure 4).

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
My point-quarter data is considerably lower than visual estimates made by Wass (Figure 5).

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
Even with a conservative assumption that the live coral cover has remained static over the last seven years, ther is a 40% inflation of the point-quarter data for percent live coral cover by the visual estimate used by Wass.

--- SENTENCE 76 ---
By duplicating the methods used by Wass when censusing fishes I was able to achieve a similar Total Number of Species and On-Transect Species at all three stations, but the On-Transect Individuals total is extremely low at Cape Larsen and Sita Bay.

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
By pooling my Cape Larsen transect data with the other post A. planci transect data, population reductions in a species would be more apparent and population increases would be less apparent.

--- SENTENCE 109 ---

--- SENTENCE 112 ---
Paracirrhites arcatus, a cirrhitid closely associated with branching Acropora, Pocillopora and Stylophora corals (Amesbury and Myers 1982), was significantly (P < 0.05) reduced in numbers.

--- SENTENCE 113 ---
At Sita Bay and Asili Point it appears there was no significant change.

--- SENTENCE 114 ---
Two labrids showed significant changes.

--- SENTENCE 115 ---
Bodianus axillaris significantly (P < 0.05) reduced in number, and Halichoeres hortulanus significantly increased in number.

--- SENTENCE 116 ---
B. axillaris feeds primarily on benthic invertebrates (Amesbury and Myers 1982), and it appears there was no significant change at Sita Bay (it was not recorded at Asili Point).

--- SENTENCE 117 ---
H. hortulanus primarily feeds on sand-dwelling invertebrates and small fishes (Amesbury and Myers 1982).

--- SENTENCE 118 ---
It does not appear to have significantly changed in number at Sita Bay or Asili Point.

--- SENTENCE 119 ---
Williams (1986) found no significant changes in population for H. hortulanus on A. planci affected reefs.

--- SENTENCE 120 ---
Naso lituratus, an algal grazing acanthurid (Amesbury and Myers 1982), increased in number significantly (P < 0.05).

--- SENTENCE 121 ---
Williams (1986) found a significant (P < 0.01) increase in N. lituratus populations on 1 of 2 reefs affected by A. planci.

--- SENTENCE 122 ---
Distinguishing between changes in a reef fish community because of a disturbance or because of temporal variations are made more difficult with a sampling technique that introduces more variability to the data.

--- SENTENCE 123 ---
The transect corridor in this study is too small.

--- SENTENCE 124 ---
Better quantifiable and analyzable data could be achieved by widening the transect corridor and by splitting the 100 m long transect into two 50 m replicate transects starting from the midpoint.

--- SENTENCE 125 ---
At the Office of Marine and Wildlife Resources in American Samoa, two replicate censuses are taken along a 50 m long, 10 m wide and 5 m high corridor starting at a common point and extending in opposite directions.

--- SENTENCE 126 ---
Transect locatins are censused several times a year in an attempt to detect seasonal Variations.

--- SENTENCE 127 ---
For tropical ecosystems where species are numerous and abundances are high, the waterproof tape recorder with the regulator microphone seems to work very well.

--- SENTENCE 128 ---
It is time efficient and frees the eyes for constant observation.

--- SENTENCE 129 ---
The exact impact of extensive A. planci coral kills on a reef fish community is difficult to assess.

--- SENTENCE 130 ---
More knowledge of spacial and temporal variations within a reef fish community is needed to detect changes that

are caused by <u>A. planci</u> coral destruction.

--- SENTENCE 131 ---
Generally, coralivorous fishes are reduced in number by starvation because their food source is destroyed (Sano et al.1984, Birkeland et al. 1985, Williams 1986), and algal grazers increase in number with the level of algae covered substrate (Birkeland et al. 1985).

--- SENTENCE 132 ---
The effects of A.planci coral kills, and subsequent algal increase, on the higher inverebrate community could explain some of the changes detected in the labrids.

--- SENTENCE 133 ---
Juvenile fish recruitment patterns may change post A. planci, and habitat changes may increase or decrease the chances of specific juveniles against predation following recruitment.

--- SENTENCE 134 ---
Many detailed studies of specific recruitment and feeding patterns would be needed to illuminate the less visible interactions.

--- SENTENCE 135 ---
Species like <u>paracirrhites arcatus</u> that are very territorial and habitat specific will increase competition for space.

--- SENTENCE 136 ---
Other species may have a resulting decrease in space competition.

--- SENTENCE 137 ---
The analyses show only a few species are detectably affected.

--- SENTENCE 138 ---
Species diversity remains high, and the reef fish community has changed only slightly.

--- SENTENCE 139 ---
Wass (in Birkeland et al. 1985) notes that fishes targeted by local fishermen appear as large and as plentiful as ever.

--- SENTENCE 140 ---
Though A. planci destroyed nearly all the live coral covering Tutuila reefs, this impact did not extend heavily into the reef fish community.

## Cross-Kingdom Amplification Using *Bacteria*-Specific Primers: Complications for Studies of Coral Microbial Ecology

Julia P. Galkiewicz1 and Christina A. Kellogg2 \*

*University of South Florida, College of Marine Science, 140 7th Ave. S., St. Petersburg, Florida 33701,*<sup>1</sup> *and U.S. Geological Survey, 600 4th St. S., St. Petersburg, Florida 33701*<sup>2</sup>

Received 9 June 2008/

--- SENTENCE 2 ---
Accepted 8 October 2008

**PCR amplification of pure bacterial DNA is vital to the study of bacterial interactions with corals.

--- SENTENCE 3 ---
Commonly used** *Bacteria***-specific primers 8F and 27F paired with the universal primer 1492R amplify both eukaryotic and prokaryotic rRNA genes.

--- SENTENCE 4 ---
An alternative primer set, 63F/1542R, is suggested to resolve this problem.

--- SENTENCE 5 ---
**

Coral microbial ecology focuses on the interactions between the coral animal and its associated microbial communities, including algae (31), bacteria (29), fungi (5), and archaea (18, 34).

--- SENTENCE 6 ---
The functions of the different microbial groups within the coral-associated community are still relatively uncharacterized, although a probiotic role has been hypothesized (25).

--- SENTENCE 7 ---
The identification of bacteria associated with different corals has been accomplished by both culture-based and culture-independent methods (6, 19, 20, 27–30).

--- SENTENCE 8 ---
However, because of the limitations of cultivation, culture-independent methods are more widely used to catalog microbial diversity (6, 9, 29, 30).

--- SENTENCE 9 ---
Since the bacteria are not isolated, identification depends on genetic comparisons.

--- SENTENCE 10 ---
The most commonly used DNA sequence for bacterial phylogenetics is the highly conserved 16S rRNA gene sequence, and primers have been designed to selectively amplify bacterial 16S rRNA genes (2, 35).

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
Differential amplification of mixed samples has been noted, indicating that special care must be used when interpreting these data for quantitative applications (14, 32).

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
In this paper, we describe the amplification of coral 18S rRNA genes by using primer sets that had previously been described as *Bacteria* specific.

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
Cross-kingdom amplification is not unknown for primer sets that are targeted toward bacteria (16) but has not been previously reported for the commonly used sets 8F/1492R and 27F/1492R (3, 6, 8, 29, 30).

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
In fact, 8F has been repeatedly reported as *Bacteria* specific (2, 7, 16, 26), and 27F is a single-base-pair modification of 8F (Table 1).

--- SENTENCE 15 ---
Branches of the Pacific scleractinian coral *Pocillopora damicornis* (Linnaeus 1758) were collected in two locations in American Samoa and one location in the northwestern Hawaiian Islands.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
Two samples per site were taken for a total of six samples, preserved in a dimethyl sulfoxide-EDTA-salt buffer.

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
DNA was extracted using a Mo Bio PowerSoil DNA isolation kit (29, 30).

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
Primer sets 8F/1492R and 27F/1492R were used in separate reactions to amplify extracted rRNA genes (Table 1).

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
PCR was performed on a GeneAmp PCR system 9600 (Applied Biosystems), using 12.5 l AmpliTaq Gold (Applied Biosystems), 1 l each of 10 pM concentrations of forward and reverse primers, 9.5 l sterile deionized water, and 1 l DNA template, for a total volume of 25 l. The temperature cycling program used was modified slightly from that of Bourne and Munn (6) by adding an initial hot start and was as follows: 1 cycle of 95°C for 15 min; 30 cycles of 95°C for 1 min, 54°C for 1 min, and 72°C for 2 min; and a final extension of 72°C for 10 min.

DNA extracted from the six coral samples was separately amplified using 8F/1492R and cloned, creating six clone libraries.

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
Because the sequencing of the first clone library (Tut2) revealed coral DNA amplification, none of the other five libraries were sequenced.

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
DNA amplified from the coral samples was purified using the QIAquick PCR purification kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA) and cloned using the Qiagen PCR cloning plus kit, in accordance with the manufacturer's protocols.

--- SENTENCE 22 ---
The rRNA genes were amplified from the clones using M13/pUC forward and reverse primers, and PCR products were visualized through electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel with ethidium bromide added directly to the gel for a final concentration of 10<sup>7</sup> g/ml.

--- SENTENCE 23 ---
Clones with correctly sized vectors (ca. 1.5 kbp) were sequenced unidirectionally using the forward primer by Northwoods DNA, Inc. (Solway, MN).

--- SENTENCE 91 ---
Although the primer combination of 63F/1542R was necessary to separate coral from bacterial rRNA genes in this experiment, these primers should be used with caution.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
Marchesi et al. developed 63F due to the failure of standard primers in amplifying environmental samples (23).

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
Early tests showed that 63F exhibited a bias in the species of bacteria amplified, but overall, it worked better than 27F in amplifying bacterial 16S rRNA genes in environmental samples.

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
However, Sipos et al. found that a significant bias was introduced into amplifications of mixed cultures when annealing temperatures (*Ta*) above 52°C were used (32): bacterial species with rRNA genes that matched 63F exactly were preferentially amplified over those with three mismatches at the 5 end of the primer.

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
If the mismatched bacterial 16S rRNA gene sequences had a relative abundance of less than 1:10 in the original sample, no PCR product could be detected.

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
This result showed that important bacterial community members may go undetected as a result of a slight primer mismatch.

--- SENTENCE 97 ---
To address this problem, Sipos et al. suggested keeping the *Ta* below 50°C.

--- SENTENCE 98 ---
Reducing *Ta* to the appropriate levels lowered the amplification bias of 63F to almost undetectable amounts.

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
The specific melting temperatures (*Tm*) of all primers used in this study can be found in Table 1 and were determined using the *Tm* calculations for oligonucleotides (Promega biomath calculator).

--- SENTENCE 100 ---
Common primer design and use protocols indicate that the *Ta* used during thermocycling should be at least 5°C lower than the *Tm* for optimal primer hybridization (17): for 8F/1492R, the *Ta* should be 47°C, and for 63F/1542R, the *Ta* should be 49°C.

--- SENTENCE 101 ---
The actual *Ta* used in this experiment was 54°C, decreasing the likelihood of primer attachment.

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
Rohwer et al. used a *Ta* of 62°C, which should lead to extremely specific binding of the primers to template DNA (29, 30).

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
When the 62°C *Ta* was applied to our samples, the "coral band" seen in the 63F/1542R amplification disappeared, indicating that coral DNA amplification was largely reduced or eliminated.

--- SENTENCE 104 ---
Attempts to amplify the extractions using 8F/ 1492R and a touch-down PCR protocol (65°C *Ta*, 0.5°C decrease every cycle) (F. Rohwer, personal communication) were unsuccessful, while faint amplification was detected using 63F/ 1542R.

Amplification of eukaryotic DNA using *Bacteria*-specific primers has previously been reported for systems other than the coral holobiont (22).

--- SENTENCE 105 ---
Lopez et al. found that several common primer sets used for testing bacterial populations in wine also amplified yeast, fungal, and plant DNA in a mixed-DNA extraction (22).

--- SENTENCE 106 ---
As suggested by Ben-Dov et al., periodic reassessments of common primers should be done because many primers were developed at a time when genetic databases were less comprehensive (4).

--- SENTENCE 107 ---
Based on *Tm* calculations, it is apparent why previous studies

(29, 30) using primers 8F and 1492R to amplify 16S rRNA genes from coral tissue did not result in PCR products of 18S rRNA genes: high *Ta* lead to extremely specific primer attachments, greatly reducing the probability that coral DNA would be amplified.

--- SENTENCE 108 ---
However, caution must be used when determining the ideal *Ta* for use in thermocycling protocols.

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
Since extremely high *Ta* lead to highly specific primer bindings, potentially valuable bacterial community members may be excluded from amplification because of several base pair mismatches to the primer.

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
To combat this problem, Frank et al. suggested using a combination of several slight variations of the forward primer 27F to amplify the true bacterial community ratios more accurately (15).

--- SENTENCE 111 ---
Increasing the diversity of forward primers may increase the diversity of the 16S rRNA gene sequences that are amplified.

--- SENTENCE 112 ---
In order to fully comprehend the multitude of interactions in the coral holobiont, the complete range of bacterial species present in samples must be analyzed.

--- SENTENCE 113 ---
Balance between primer specificity and the potentially mismatched novel bacterial sequences must be found.

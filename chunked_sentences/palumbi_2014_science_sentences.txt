## Mechanisms of Reef Coral Resistance to Future Climate Change

Stephen R. Palumbi,\* Daniel J. Barshis, † Nikki Traylor-Knowles, Rachael A. Bay

Department of Biology, Stanford University, Hopkins Marine Station, Pacific Grove, CA 93950, USA.

--- SENTENCE 2 ---
E-mail: spalumbi@stanford.edu

†Present address: Department of Biological Sciences, Old Dominion University, Norfolk, VA 23529, USA.

--- SENTENCE 3 ---
**Reef corals are highly sensitive to heat, yet populations resistant to climate change have recently been identified.

--- SENTENCE 4 ---
To determine the mechanisms of temperature tolerance, we reciprocally transplanted corals between reef sites experiencing distinct temperature regimes, and tested subsequent physiological and gene expression profiles.

--- SENTENCE 5 ---
Local acclimatization and fixed effects, such as adaptation, contributed about equally to heat tolerance and are reflected in patterns of gene expression.

--- SENTENCE 6 ---
In less than two years, acclimatization achieves the same heat tolerance that we would expect from strong natural selection over many generations for these long-lived organisms.

--- SENTENCE 7 ---
Our results show both short-term acclimatory and longerterm adaptive acquisition of climate resistance.

--- SENTENCE 8 ---
Adding these adaptive abilities to ecosystem models is likely to slow predictions of demise for coral reef ecosystems.

--- SENTENCE 9 ---
**

Reef-building corals have experienced global declines resulting from bleaching events sparked by pulses of warm water exposure (*1*–*4*).

--- SENTENCE 10 ---
However, corals in naturally warm environments can have high resistance to bleaching temperatures, and can survive heat exposure that would bleach conspecifics in cooler microclimates (*5*, *6*).

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
Similarly, recent discovery of populations of acidification-resistant corals show that physiological or evolutionary mechanisms of environmental accommodation exist (*7*, *8*).

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
Such populations are ideal test sites for research into the mechanisms of coral response to climate change.

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
Corals in adjacent backreef pools in the U.S. National Park of American Samoa on Ofu Island experience strong differences in temperature (*9*, *10*).

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
In the Highly Variable (HV) Pool, temperatures often exceed the local critical bleaching temperature of 30°C, reaching 35°C during strong noontime low tides (*6*).

--- SENTENCE 15 ---
By contrast, the Moderately Variable (MV) Pool rarely experiences temperatures above 32°C.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
Corals in the HV Pool have higher growth rates (*9*, *10*), higher survivorship and higher symbiont photosynthetic efficiency during experimental heat stress than conspecifics from the MV Pool (*6*).

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
These pools provide a powerful system to test the speed and extent of coral acclimatization and adaptation to warm water conditions in the context of future climate change.

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
To test corals in their native habitats for physiological resistance to heat stress, we collected branches of the table top coral *Acropora hyacinthus* (cryptic species E, (*11*)) and exposed them to experimental bleaching conditions.

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
*A. hyacinthus* is a cosmopolitan species that comprises a large percentage of hard coral cover on Pacific reefs and shows high levels of bleaching and mortality during large-scale bleaching events (*4*).

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
We chose *A. hyacinthus* for this study because it is a dominant reef-builder and is especially sensitive to environmental stress, making its relative ability to acclimate or adapt extremely important to the future of coral reef ecosystems as climate change proceeds.

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
We subjected branches of corals to a prescribed ramp in water temperature of 29 - 34°C for 3 hours, followed by an incubation for 3 hours at 34°C.

--- SENTENCE 22 ---
These conditions mimic the natural increase in temperature observed in the HV Pool during a tidal cycle.

--- SENTENCE 23 ---
Experiments on fragments of tagged and monitored colonies showed that individuals native to the HV Pool exhibit higher resistance to thermal stress, measured by retention of chlorophyll derived from photosynthetic symbionts, than corals from the MV Pool (Fig. 1).

--- SENTENCE 24 ---
The average retention of chlorophyll-a after experimental heat stress was 80% in HV Pool corals (Fig. 1C) but only 45% in MV Pool corals (Fig.

--- SENTENCE 25 ---
1A, *t* test, p<0.00001) compared with controls.

--- SENTENCE 26 ---
To test for acclimatization, we transplanted coral colonies of *A. hyacinthus* reciprocally from their native locations in the HV and MV pools to three transplant sites within each pool.

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
We transplanted six colonies from the HV Pool and twelve from the MV Pool.

--- SENTENCE 28 ---
After 12, 19, and 27 months, we tested transplanted colonies for thermal resistance.

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
For 11 separate colonies, 22 of 23 paired bleaching experiments show that corals acquired at least part of the heat sensitivity of the pool they were transplanted into.

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
The experiments showed higher chlorophyll-a retention during heat stress in colonies transplanted to the HV Pool than in the same colony transplanted to the MV Pool (p < 0.0001, paired *t* test, Fig. 2).

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
Bleaching resistance did not vary with the time

of transplant or season (p>0.80).

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
Although the MV Pool corals acquired heat resistance when moved to the HV Pool, they did not achieve the resistance of native HV corals.

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
MV pool corals transplanted to the HV Pool retained less chlorophyll in bleaching experiments than corals native to the HV pool (compare Fig.

--- SENTENCE 34 ---
1D; i.e., 67.5% and 80%, respectively.

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
Student's *t* test p<0.05).

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
By contrast, HV Pool corals transplanted into the MV Pool dropped their chlorophyll-a retention to the same level as the MV Pool natives (compare Fig.

--- SENTENCE 37 ---

--- SENTENCE 38 ---

--- SENTENCE 39 ---
To understand the physiological changes associated with acquired heat resistance, we investigated gene expression in reciprocally transplanted corals.

--- SENTENCE 40 ---
Six corals were sampled at noon on August 30, 2011, each from reciprocal transplant sites in the HV and MV Pools.

--- SENTENCE 41 ---
Reads from 12 mRNA extractions (one from each coral from each pool) were mapped to the *A. hyacinthus* assembly developed by Barshis *et al*. (*12*).

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
Data pipelines and statistical procedures followed De Wit *et al*.

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
Transcriptome profiles of transplanted colonies show strong evidence for acclimatization.

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
A 2-way ANOVA of 16,728 coral genes detected 74 that changed significantly when comparing genetically identical coral fragments between the two pools (average 3.4-fold expression difference, FDR-corrected p value <0.05, Table S3).

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
Among the 55 contigs with annotations, we found several transcription factors and cell signaling proteins (14 contigs), heat shock and chaperonin proteins (9 contigs), TRAF-type proteins, cytochrome P450 and fluorochromes (Fig.

--- SENTENCE 46 ---
3A) that appeared to be involved in heat acclimation.

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
The ANOVA test also highlights 71 contigs with differential expression depending on the origin of colonies (2-way ANOVA experimentwide FDR p<0.05, Table S2).

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
These genes showed differences in expression levels depending strictly on the pool of origin, not on the final transplant site.

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
In our previous work members of the tumor necrosis factor receptor (TNFR) superfamily, which is involved in eukaryotic immune function and apoptosis (*14*, *15*), were constitutively upregulated in native HV Pool corals (*13*).

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
In the current data set, one TNFR gene exhibited a 9.9 fold difference in expression in coral colonies native to the HV Pool, whether they were living in the HV or MV pools (Fig.

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
3B), when compared with corals native to the MV Pool.

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
These results indicated that mediators of coral thermal resistance have fixed constitutive expression levels in either pool, perhaps representing signs of genetically-based local adaptation.

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
Our transcriptome data also allowed us to test for changes in the proportions of symbiotic algae during coral acclimatization.

--- SENTENCE 54 ---
*Symbiodinium* clades C and D are common in corals in American Samoa and corals in warmer microclimates tend to have clade D symbionts (*16*).

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
Though most colonies are dominated by one or the other of these clades, all colonies we have tested host background populations of the other clade (*17*).

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
To test if coral host acclimation was accompanied by changes in symbiont proportion, we estimated the proportion of clade C and D by counting the transcriptome reads that mapped exclusively to small artificial test contigs of ITS1, ITS2 and the chloroplast 23S gene, which distinguish clade C and D *Symbiodinium* (*17*).

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
In our experiments, symbiont type explained a negligible fraction of the variation in bleaching resistance in common garden conditions (R2 = 0.15 and 0.06 in the HVP and the MVP transplants, respectively, p>0.30, Fig. S2).

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
Our data also showed little shift in C versus D proportions as a result of transplantation.

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
When clade C-dominated MV Pool corals are moved to the HV Pool their proportion of clade D shifts from <1% to about 2%.

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
Likewise D-dominated HV corals moved to the MV Pool maintain only about 4% clade-C symbionts (Fig. 4).

--- SENTENCE 61 ---
Additionally, there were no gene expression changes in *Symbiodinium* between transplants to the different pools, suggesting little acclimation by symbionts occurred in the altered environmental conditions (*18*).

--- SENTENCE 62 ---
These experiments showed that some corals are capable of broad acclimatization to microclimate and developed enhanced resistance to bleaching without changing symbionts.

--- SENTENCE 63 ---
We can place this capacity for acclimatization in an evolutionary context by comparing the natural phenotypic difference we find between pools with the shift due to acclimatization after transplantation.

--- SENTENCE 64 ---
Phenotypic change in evolutionary biology is often measured by the intensity parameter *I*, which is defined as the change in mean phenotype before versus after natural selection, divided by the standard deviation (*19*).

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
In our case, we applied this concept to the spatial differences between pools instead of to temporal differences.

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
The average phenotypic change from corals native to the HV Pool versus the MV Pool was 0.35 with a standard deviation of 0.14 (compare Fig.

--- SENTENCE 67 ---
1A and Fig. 1C).

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
Thus, the phenotypic shift between pools was 2.5 standard deviations.

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
Here, *I* measures the difference in phenotype between coral populations subjected to different environments, and is affected by phenotypic change caused by the fixed effects between the microclimates (denoted *IF*), as well as by the acclimatization of individuals (denoted *IA*), *I* = *IF*+*IA*.

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
In this case, fixed effects include evolutionary adaptation, shifts in symbiont type, developmental changes and epigenetics.

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
Our transplant experiment allowed us to measure *IA* as the average change in mean phenotype among acclimating individuals (0.214) divided by the standard deviation (0.137), resulting in *IA* = 1.56 (Fig.

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
1A vs B and C vs D).

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
This in turn allowed us to estimate *IF* to be 0.94 standard deviation units

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
(= *I* - *IA*).

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
These estimates indicated that these corals did acclimate to higher temperatures.

--- SENTENCE 76 ---
The change in phenotype due to acclimatization (1.56 sd units) was similar to, but higher than the change we estimated is caused by fixed effects between pools (0.94 sd units).

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
We have provisionally extended this analysis to gene expression to illustrate the dual roles of acclimation and adaptation in gene expression shifts.

--- SENTENCE 78 ---
There are 141 contigs for which corals native to the HV Pool showed expression levels that were more than one standard deviation different from corals native to the MV Pool, and that were significant in the above 2-way ANOVA analysis.

--- SENTENCE 79 ---
Considering gene expression as a phenotype, these loci had an intensity *I* >1.0.

--- SENTENCE 80 ---
We also measured the contribution of acclimation to this phenotypic change by measuring gene expression in MV Pool corals in the HV Pool and vice versa.

--- SENTENCE 81 ---
For these 141 contigs, the change caused by acclimation (*IA*) averaged 42% of the phenotypic difference (Fig. S4, Table S4), with the rest being caused by fixed effects (*IF*).

--- SENTENCE 82 ---
Loci with strong fixed effects included Tumor Necrosis Factor Receptor (as above) and a series of cellular transport loci with unknown function in corals.

--- SENTENCE 83 ---
As in the case of bleaching phenotypes, fixed effects could be due to adaptive evolution, but also affected by developmental changes, epigenetics and symbiont type.

--- SENTENCE 84 ---
It is also possible there are residual effects that might eventually be erased by longer residence time.

--- SENTENCE 85 ---
However, our experiments were conducted on branches that grew in situ after transplantation, and so our data are from tissues that never experienced the native pool environment.

--- SENTENCE 86 ---
Loci with strong components of acclimation include the Tumor Necrosis Factor Receptor-Associated Factors, the signaling transducers for TNFR, as well as *Ras* and *Rab* proteins, transcription factors and heat shock proteins.

--- SENTENCE 87 ---
The possibility that corals can acclimate to local differences has been suggested for decades: conspecific corals at different latitudes show bleaching temperatures 1-2°C above local mean summer maximum sea surface temperature (*20*, *21*), despite substantial differences in mean summer maximum temperatures.

--- SENTENCE 88 ---
However, the mechanism generating this pattern has never been strongly tested.

--- SENTENCE 89 ---
Early analyses of the threat to corals from climate change (*1*, *2*) emphasized the potential for coral acclimatization or local adaptation to alter predictions of climate change effects.

--- SENTENCE 90 ---
New models show that coral adaptation over a forty year time frame could substantially change predictions for coral reef demise (*22*).

--- SENTENCE 91 ---
But because future adaptation over many generations has been considered too slow for long-lived species such as corals, the role of individual acclimatization in coral environmental tolerance has been central to the debate on the future of reefs.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
The corals in our experiment achieved a larger bleaching phenotype shift (*IA* = 1.56) due to acclimatization within 15-24 months than the shift due to fixed effects between habitats, a substantial acceleration in the rate of local matching of coral phenotype to thermal environment.

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
Our results clearly show that acclimatization can allow corals to acquire significant high temperature resistance more quickly than strong natural selection would produce.

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
We do not yet know how many coral species can acclimate or evolve.

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
Several dozen coral species live and grow in the over-heated backreef pools of Ofu (*23*), but whether all individual colonies have equal acclimatization ability, or if there is an upper thermal limit to acclimatization or adaptation (*24*) remain unknown.

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
It is also probable that multiple stressors – from acidification and heat, for example – can reduce the ability of corals to respond (*25*).

--- SENTENCE 97 ---
Thus, acclimatization alone cannot be expected to completely overcome the threat to corals from widespread bleaching events, especially if the onset of high temperature stress is abrupt and sustained.

--- SENTENCE 98 ---
In this regard, the tempo and severity of heat anomalies will be critical for effective coral acclimatization.

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
Persistence of populations through climate change demands biogeographic shifts of species (*26*–*28*), evolutionary adaptation of populations (*29*) or local acclimatization of individuals (*2*).

--- SENTENCE 100 ---
For long-lived, sessile foundation species that create ecosystem habitat such as forest trees or reef building corals, range shifts and evolution are predicted to be slow (*2*, *30*).

--- SENTENCE 101 ---
As a consequence, the rate and scope of acclimatization in these species to future environmental conditions is central to understanding the impact of climate change.

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
For the fast-growing, shallow water species we studied, acclimatory andadaptive responses allowed them to inhabit reef areas with water temperatures far above their expected tolerances.

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
How well other corals can similarly respond, and what the limits of these responses are, will determine how well current models accurately predict the future demise of coral reefs.

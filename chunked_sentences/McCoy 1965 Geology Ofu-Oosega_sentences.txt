--- SENTENCE 184 ---
Abstract

The islands of Ofu and Olosega are in the Manu'a Group of the Samoan Archipelago, 65 miles east of Tutuila Island.

--- SENTENCE 185 ---
Together they comprise an area of 3-1/2 square miles.

--- SENTENCE 186 ---
The maximum elevation is 2095 feet on Olosega.

--- SENTENCE 187 ---
ofu and Olosega Islands are the remnants of a single shield volcano, the Ofu-Olosega volcano.

--- SENTENCE 188 ---
Hawaiian-type eruptions built a volcano about 2500 feet above present sea level, along the Manu'a Ridge of the regional Manu'a rift zone during the Pliocene.

--- SENTENCE 189 ---
Two rift sones radiated from the volcano, one N 40° E and another S 35° W.

--- SENTENCE 190 ---
Late in the development of the volcano, collapse at the intersection of the rift zones produced a caldera 3 miles long and 1-1/2 miles wide.

--- SENTENCE 191 ---
Four igneous rock units are recognized: 1) the Extracaldera Volcanics, 2) the Intrusive Volcanics, 3) the Intra-caldera Volcanics, and 4) the Late Volcanics.

--- SENTENCE 192 ---
The Extra-caldera Volcanics represent the numerous lava flows of an and pahoehoe types which built the shield prior to caldera collapse.

--- SENTENCE 193 ---
The Intrusive Volcanics are a peripheral dike complex which delineates the caldera.

--- SENTENCE 194 ---
Two volcanic plugs are also exposed.

--- SENTENCE 195 ---
The Intra-caldera Volcanics include the thick flows which ponded within a pit crater contiguous to the caldera, and the talus breccias which accumulated at the base of the southern caldera wall.

--- SENTENCE 196 ---
Following deep disection of the volcano, the Late Volcanics occurred as a

phreato-magmatic explosion, producing the Nu'utele tuff cone west of Ofu, and as a submarine eruption between Olosega and Ta'u Islands in the late 1860's.

--- SENTENCE 197 ---
Igneous rock types are predominately aphanitic and porphyritic olivine basalts.

--- SENTENCE 198 ---
Ankaramites and basaltic andesites also occur, indicating differentiation of the primary magma.

--- SENTENCE 199 ---
A relatively minor amount of pyroclastic rock is present.

--- SENTENCE 200 ---
No major erosional unconformity is exposed.

--- SENTENCE 201 ---
Development of the shield closely paralleled that of other volcances in the Samoan and the Hawaiian Islands.

--- SENTENCE 202 ---
Gravity data corroborates the surface geology.

--- SENTENCE 203 ---
A narrow fringe of calcareous beach deposits and a fringing coral reef surround both islands.

--- SENTENCE 204 ---
High cliffs around the islands are the product of marine abrasion.

--- SENTENCE 205 ---
Indications of a +15 and a +5 foot stand of sea level occur along the coastline as wave-cut benches.

### INTRODUCTION

### General Geographic Description of the Islands

Ofu and Olosega Islands are the two smallest islands of the Manu'a Group, the eastern-most group of islands in the Samoan Archipelago.

--- SENTENCE 206 ---
Ofu and Olosega are about 60 miles east of Tutuila Island, the administrative center of American Samoa, and 6-1/2 miles west of Ta'u Island, the largest island in the Manu'a Group.

--- SENTENCE 207 ---
Both islands are at 14° 11' South Latitude, Ofu at 169° 40' West Longitude and Olosega at 169° 37' West Longitude.

--- SENTENCE 208 ---
Together they comprise an area of 3-1/2 square miles.

--- SENTENCE 209 ---
The Manu's Group of islands trend N 70° W.

--- SENTENCE 210 ---
This is the same direction of trend for the larger islands of the Samoan Archipelago, Savai'i, and Upolu in Western Samoa (Kear and Wood, 1959), and Tutuila in eastern Samoa, but the two trends seem to be offset by about 25 miles along a northeast-southwest direction (Fig. 1).

--- SENTENCE 211 ---
Both Ofu and Olosega are triangularly shaped with their apexes barely separated by a narrow, shallow waterway.

--- SENTENCE 212 ---
Ofu rises to 1,621 feet and Olosega to 2,095 feet.

--- SENTENCE 213 ---

The first geological observation recorded was made by
La Perouse in 1798 who merely stated that Ofu and Olosega,
as well as the rest of Samoa, appeared volcanic and made
up of pieces of lava or basalts surrounded by coral.

--- SENTENCE 226 ---
Later,
Couthouy (1842) described the reefs in Samoa, mentioning
large fragments of coral 85 feet above sea level "at the
northwest end of Manu'a."

--- SENTENCE 227 ---
He was probably referring to
what is now called the island of Ta'u and the tuffs at its
northwestern point, which do carry coral fragments (Stice,
1965).

--- SENTENCE 228 ---
Evidently, the application of the name Manu'a to all

three islands is a more recent innovation.

--- SENTENCE 229 ---
Dana (1849) noted the separation between the Manu'a trend and the trend of the larger Samoan islands.

--- SENTENCE 230 ---
From information supplied by others in the Wilkes Expedition who visited the Manu'a Group, Dana thought that Ofu and Olosega were "once united."

--- SENTENCE 231 ---
Friedlander, according to Stearns (1944), believed Ofu and Olosega were the remnants of a single volcano, and that the embayments north and south of the islands resulted from collapse which formed two craters.

--- SENTENCE 232 ---
He also collected a sample of olivine basalt from Olosega which was first described by Weber in 1909 and later by Macdonald (1944).

--- SENTENCE 233 ---
Daly (1924, pp. 132-135) was the first American geologist to visit Ofu and Olosega.

--- SENTENCE 234 ---
His reconnaissance survey of only a few days contributed much to the geology of the two islands.

--- SENTENCE 235 ---
He recognized that both islands were merely the remnant of a larger basaltic volcano.

--- SENTENCE 236 ---

--- SENTENCE 237 ---

2) remnants of the wall of a collapse caldera, or 3) scars left by two enormous landslips involving much of the original island.

The first hypothesis he rejected.

--- SENTENCE 239 ---
He could not, however, decide between the second and third, and believed the decision could not be made until more detailed soundings were taken offshore.

--- SENTENCE 240 ---
Daly thought both islands were largely composed of pyroclastic material, with basaltic flows

predominating only at the northwestern end of Ofu and the southeastern end of Olosega.

--- SENTENCE 242 ---

### Volcanic Structure

### General Structure of the Shield.

--- SENTENCE 280 ---
Ofu and Olosega Islands are the deeply dissected remnants of the Ofu-Olosega volcano.

--- SENTENCE 281 ---
In much the same manner as other volcanoes in the Samoan islands (Stearns, 1944; Kear and Wood, 1959) and the Hawaiian Islands (Stearns, 1946; Stearns and Macdonald, 1946; Wentworth and Macdonald, 1953), the Ofu-Olosega volcano built a constructional lava dome, or shield, upon the broad ridge striking N 70° W through the Manu'a Group.

--- SENTENCE 282 ---
The Manu'a Ridge represents a major regional lineament on the Central Pacific sea floor.

--- SENTENCE 283 ---
The original dome probably stood approximately 2500 feet above present sea level, or about 500 feet higher than Piumafua Mountain on Olosega, the highest point of the volcano now.

--- SENTENCE 284 ---
Nearly all of the caldera was north of the present islands.

--- SENTENCE 285 ---
Two rift zones extend outwards from the caldera.

--- SENTENCE 286 ---
From the eastern edge of the caldera, one rift zone trends N 40° E, and the other known rift zone trends \$ 35° W from the southwestern edge.

--- SENTENCE 287 ---
Following the collapse of the summit to form the caldera, the southern portion of the volcano remained higher, as noted by Stearns (1944).

--- SENTENCE 288 ---
This high southern rim has been eroded to the present islands of Ofu and Olosega.

--- SENTENCE 289 ---
Near the caldera, the dips of lava flows are almost horizontal, but towards the periphery of the Ofu-Olosega shield they increase to as much as 30°.

--- SENTENCE 290 ---
The average dip is about 15°, and such

steep dips may indicate that the flows entered deep water close to shore (Stearns and Macdonald, 1942, p. 156).

--- SENTENCE 291 ---
Pyroclastics compose about 5 per cent of the known volume of the volcano, so that Daly's term "explosive type" probably would be considered a misnomer to later geologists (Stearns, 1944).

--- SENTENCE 292 ---
Nevertheless, pyroclastic rocks appear to be more abundant than described for the Hawaiian volcanoes in general (Wentworth and Macdonald, 1953).

--- SENTENCE 293 ---
This higher pyroclastic content is probably due to the caldera's position close to sea level, supporting the interpretation of a generally low elevation of the shield indicated by the steeper dips discussed above.

--- SENTENCE 294 ---
Under these conditions, normally quiet Hawaiian-type eruptions would be expected to produce a slightly higher amount of pyroclastics because of littoral and phreatomagmatic explosions, especially in the vicinity of the caldera and along the rift zones.

--- SENTENCE 295 ---
The resulting high concentration of pyroclastic debris would extend horizontally as the shield grows in diameter.

--- SENTENCE 296 ---
Thus, a vertical section would show a decreasing content of pyroclastic material upwards.

--- SENTENCE 297 ---
The island of Ofu is at the intersection of the N 70° W rift of the Manu'a Ridge, and the southwestern rift zone of the Ofu-Olosega volcano, with the southeastern corner of the Ofu-Olosega caldera.

--- SENTENCE 298 ---
Olosega Island lies on the intersection of the regional Manu'a rift zone and the northeastern rift zone of the volcano.

--- SENTENCE 299 ---
Both islands exist today because of their position along the higher southern rim of the caldera and the more resistant nature of the dike complexes passing

through the islands.

--- SENTENCE 300 ---
None of the original shield surface remains on either Ofu or Olosega.

--- SENTENCE 301 ---
The western slope of Ofu behind Ofu village and the eastern slope of Olosega are, however, slightly steepened and lowered surfaces which still partly reflect the original shield surface.

--- SENTENCE 302 ---
Elsewhere on Ofu, the topography is controlled by the underlying dikes along both the rift sones and the caldera boundary, and the eroded remnant of the pit crater which was adjacent to and part of the caldera.

--- SENTENCE 303 ---
Weathering along the eastern slope of Olosega directly reflects the underlying structure by proceeding in a series of 3- to 10-foot high steps, which approximately parallel the contours.

--- SENTENCE 304 ---
This manner of weathering evidently progresses flow by flow.

--- SENTENCE 305 ---
A similar pattern has developed on portions of the western slope of Ofu, but in not nearly as extensive a pattern as on the eastern slope of Olosega.

--- SENTENCE 306 ---
The bench protruding from the eastern slope of Olosega and forming Le'ala Point, is the result of a series of thicker flows exposed here, the denser material being more resistant to weathering.

--- SENTENCE 307 ---
Nu'utele and Nu'usilaelae islands west of Ofu are the eroded remnants of a tuff cone that erupted late in the history of the volcano.

--- SENTENCE 308 ---
Collapse of the summit of the Ofu-Olosega volcano, late in the development of the shield, produced a caldera 3 miles long and 1-1/2 miles wide.

--- SENTENCE 309 ---
In size, it is similar to the calderas of the Hawaiian volcanoes.

--- SENTENCE 310 ---
Calderas in Hawaii are

!

--- SENTENCE 311 ---
[](_page_18_Figure_0.jpeg)

believed to have been produced by subsidence, resulting in the formation and eventual coalescence of numerous pit craters.

--- SENTENCE 312 ---
They are classified as calderas of the Glen Coe type (Stearns and Macdonald, 1946; Macdonald, 1964).

--- SENTENCE 313 ---
The Ofu-Olosega caldera probably originated through subsidence also, and would be classified as a caldera of the Glen Coe type.

--- SENTENCE 314 ---
A pit crater that was not entirely incorporated into the general elliptical pattern of the caldera, remains as a depression on Ofu.

--- SENTENCE 315 ---
The floor of the caldera was probably quite close to sea level; it may even have been open to the sea along its northern boundary, allowing marine erosion to occur later within the caldera.

### Gravity Measurements

A gravimetric survey of Ofu and Olosega Islands was conducted during the summer of 1964 as part of a gravimetric survey of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 316 ---
The absolute Bouguer gravity anomalies of Ofu and Olosega range from a low of plus 260 milligals at Alaufau on the west coast of Ofu, to a high of plus 312 milligals on the beach at Sunu'itao, also on Ofu (Machesky, 1964).

--- SENTENCE 317 ---
A surface density of 2.3 grams per cubic centimeter is assumed.

--- SENTENCE 318 ---
Contouring of the Bouguer anomaly for the islands shows a distinct anomalous positive area in the vicinity of Vainu'ulua Peak on Ofu.

--- SENTENCE 319 ---
Similar patterns of the contoured Bouguer anomaly have been related to the calderas and the rift zones of the Hawaiian volcanoes (Woollard, 1951; Strange, Malahoff, and Woollard, 1964).

--- SENTENCE 320 ---
For the Ofu-Olosega volcano, the Bouguer gravity anomaly corroborates the present geologic

mapping, also establishing the position of the caldera to the north of the islands.

--- SENTENCE 321 ---
The westward bulge of the Bouguer anomaly contours on Ofu indicates the southwest rift zone of the volcano.

--- SENTENCE 322 ---
The regional Manu's rift zone appears as an eastward bulge of the Bouguer anomaly contours on Olosega.

# Features Associated with the Caldera Topographic features.

--- SENTENCE 324 ---
Two prominent topographic features are associated with the caldera on Ofu, the depression on Ofu and the ridge partly surrounding the depression.

--- SENTENCE 325 ---
The semi-circular depression of north-central Ofu is slightly over 1 mile in diameter along the northern coastline, and more than 1000 feet deep there.

--- SENTENCE 326 ---
It represents a pit crater which was adjacent to and part of the caldera, possibly in the same relationship as North Pit is to Mokuaweoweo, the caldera of Mauna Loa on the island of Hawaii (Stearns and Macdonald, 1946).

--- SENTENCE 327 ---
The collapse forming the crater occurred where the Manu'a regional rift zone and the southwestern rift zone of the Ofu-Olosega volcano intersected the caldera wall.

--- SENTENCE 328 ---
The high ridge bordering the depression on Ofu includes

Mato Ridge.

--- SENTENCE 329 ---
Tumu Mountain, and Le'olo Ridge.

--- SENTENCE 330 ---
The entire southern coastline of Ofu parallels this ridge.

--- SENTENCE 331 ---
Structurally, it
represents a portion of the peripheral dike complex which
intruded along the bounding faults of the pit crater.

--- SENTENCE 332 ---
The only topographic feature on Olosega associated with the caldera is the high cliff along the northwest coast of island, from Tamatupu Point to Fiava.

--- SENTENCE 333 ---
It is the fault-line

scarp of the caldera's southeastern rim.

--- SENTENCE 334 ---
Numerous dikes rose through the talus breccias within the caldera and the lava flows both within and without the caldera, to feed the numerous eruptions that built the shield volcano.

--- SENTENCE 335 ---
Because the orientation of these dikes were controlled by the caldera boundary faults, as noted by Stearns (1944, p. 1316), a ring pattern concentric to the caldera resulted.

--- SENTENCE 336 ---
This arrangement of dikes is herein referred to as the peripheral dike complex.

--- SENTENCE 337 ---
Farther from the caldera, rift-aligned faults controlled dike alignment.

--- SENTENCE 338 ---
These peripheral dikes are identical in pattern, and probably similar in origin, to the thinner, near-surface continuations of the ring dikes which surround the subsidence calderas of the shield volcances of Scotland, as given by Rickey, MacGregor and Anderson (1961, Fig. 24, p. 57).

--- SENTENCE 339 ---
Implications of similar peripheral dike patterns have been noted on other volcances in the Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 340 ---
Macdonald (1945; also Wentworth and Macdonald, 1953, p. 12) suggests that a pattern of cones on Mauna Kea, on the island of Hawaii, might possibly reflect a ring dike pattern at depth.

--- SENTENCE 341 ---
Pissures of historical eruptions on Niusfo'ou Island in northern Tonga also form a ring pattern around one side of the caldera, in the center of the island (Macdonald, 1948).

--- SENTENCE 342 ---
At its widest exposure on Ofu, between Agaputuputu on the north coast and To'aga on the southeastern coast, this peripheral dike complex is 3000 feet wide.

--- SENTENCE 343 ---
The general trend of the dikes in this area is N 850 W.

To the west of Agaputuputu on Ofu, the collapse of the pit crater along the southeastern boundary fault of the caldera, has truncated almost half of the peripheral dike complex.

--- SENTENCE 344 ---
The remaining dikes continue around the pit crater, forming the high ridge of Tumu Mountain and Le'olo and Mato Ridges.

--- SENTENCE 345 ---
At Samo'i, on the north coast of Ofu beneath Mato Ridge, these remaining dikes outcrop as a dike complex about 1800 feet wide.

--- SENTENCE 346 ---
The trend here is generally N 180 W.

Eastwards from its widest exposure on Ofu between Agaputuputu and To'aga, a considerably narrower section is exposed along the spectacular razorback ridge and Vainu'ulua Peak.

--- SENTENCE 347 ---
Here the complex is only about 900 feet wide.

--- SENTENCE 348 ---
The trend of the dikes forming the ridge and the peak is generally eastwest.

--- SENTENCE 349 ---
On the western point of Olosega, between Piumafua Mountain and Asaga Strait, the dike complex again becomes over 3000 feet wide.

--- SENTENCE 350 ---
At Tamatupu Point, the westernmost tip of Olosega in Asaga Strait, the trend is still east-west, but in less than 1000 feet to the east, it changes northward to N 82° E.

--- SENTENCE 351 ---
Then, in the vicinity of Nu'utoa Peak, the trend abruptly turns to the north again, to N 45° E, then once again to N 18° E in the cliffs behind Fo'iupolu and Sili.

--- SENTENCE 352 ---
Scattered dikes striking northeast cut through flows behind Olosega village, indicating that the complex may be over 3500 feet wide in this area.

--- SENTENCE 353 ---
The dikes exposed in this complex dip steeply to the

east at Samo'i on Ofu, to the north on the rest of Ofu, and to the northwest on Olosega.

--- SENTENCE 354 ---
That is, they all are essentially vertical or have very steep dips towards the center of the caldera.

--- SENTENCE 355 ---
Near the fringes of the complex, or father from the actual caldera boundary, the dips decrease.

--- SENTENCE 356 ---
This is particularly striking in the cliff exposures behind Olosega village, where the dikes vary from vertical at the northern edge of the complex to dips as low as 58° at the southern edge.

--- SENTENCE 357 ---
It is also noticeable in the cliffs behind Faiava on Olosega.

--- SENTENCE 358 ---
The abundance of dikes also decreases towards the fringes of the dike complex.

--- SENTENCE 359 ---
Nearer the caldera's boundary, the dikes are so closely spaced that little of the original country rock remains as, for example, in the vicinity of Nu'utoa Peak on Olosega.

--- SENTENCE 360 ---
Dikes also decrease in number upsection, as noted by Stearns (1944, p. 1316).

--- SENTENCE 361 ---
Dikes vary in thicknesses from a few inches to more than 70 feet, but average only 2 to 4 feet thick.

--- SENTENCE 362 ---
The only large fault clearly exposed on Ofu is part of the boundary fault of the pit crater.

--- SENTENCE 363 ---
At the outcrop of the fault, 300 feet east of Lelua Point on the north coast, it is a nearly vertical, normal fault striking 8 34° W.

--- SENTENCE 364 ---
The southeastern block is downthrown, but the magnitude of displacement cannot be measured.

--- SENTENCE 365 ---
The sea has cut a narrow, vertical recess into the sea cliff along the fault trace at sea level.

--- SENTENCE 366 ---
Elsewhere, numerous small, normal faults occur within the dike complexes, as at Samo'i on Ofu and in the cliffs behind Sili on Olosega, but with displacements of only a few feet.

--- SENTENCE 367 ---
Because of their small displacements, they have not been included on the geologic map.

--- SENTENCE 368 ---
The remainder of the caldera-boundary faults are either effectively masked by vegetation or have been avenues of intrusion for later dikes, and so cannot be distinguished.

Unconformities.

--- SENTENCE 369 ---
The unconformities described by Stearns (1944, p. 1315), as bounding the east and west sides of the Ofu caldera, are angular unconformities between older, thinner-bedded, extracaldera flows and the younger caldera-filling flows and talus breccias.

--- SENTENCE 370 ---
Because faulting produced the wall of the pit crater on Ofu, which was part of the caldera, the unconformity is also a buried fault-line scarp.

--- SENTENCE 371 ---
As noted by Stearns (1944, p. 1316), the eastern unconformity (and fault), west of Agaputuputu on Ofu, truncates the peripheral dike complex which lies to the east of this unconformity.

--- SENTENCE 372 ---
Small angular unconformities are exposed in the cliffs behind Sili on Olosega, and are probably the result of minor faulting near the calders.

### Streams and Valleys

All of the streams on Ofu and Olosega are ephemeral or intermittant

--- SENTENCE 395 ---
consequent streams, which have cut youthful steep-sided valleys.

--- SENTENCE 396 ---
There are two distinct drainage basins on Ofu.

--- SENTENCE 397 ---
One is along the western slope where six streams flow to the west, and the other is within the topographic depression of the pit crater where four streams flow to the north.

--- SENTENCE 398 ---
Three of these streams along the west coast, Matasina, Tufu, and

Malaeti'a streams, have cut valleys as deep as 160 feet.

--- SENTENCE 399 ---
The rest of the streams on Ofu have cut only shallow valleys about 40 feet below the surrounding land surface.

--- SENTENCE 400 ---
In all cases, valleys are cut into bedrock with little sediment in the stream channel.

--- SENTENCE 401 ---
Value and Sinapoto streams have both cut valleys about 120 feet below the general slope, but the remainder have cut only shallow valleys ranging from 40 to 90 feet deep.

--- SENTENCE 402 ---
Those streams at the southern end of the island are diverted by the bench at Le'ala Point.

### Description.

--- SENTENCE 410 ---
Bordering both islands are sea cliffs of varying heights.

--- SENTENCE 411 ---
Along the western coast of Ofu, particularly behind Ofu village and Nu'utele Island, the sea cliffs are only about 80 feet high.

--- SENTENCE 412 ---
Elsewhere around Ofu, however, the sea cliffs are much higher.

--- SENTENCE 413 ---
The highest cliffs are along the south coast from Va'oto Point to Vainu'ulua Peak.

--- SENTENCE 414 ---
They rise to more than 1500 feet near the Ofu triangulation station on Le'olo Ridge.

--- SENTENCE 415 ---
Along the northern coast of Ofu they are much lower, partly because of the downfaulted depression of the pit crater, but increase in height to the east at the razor-back ridge.

--- SENTENCE 416 ---
Behind Olosega village the sea cliffs reach a spectacular height of 2095 feet--directly to the sumit of the island.

--- SENTENCE 417 ---
From this maximum height the cliffs decrease in accordance
with the eastern slope of the island, along both the northwest and the southwest coasts.

--- SENTENCE 418 ---
Along the eastern coastline,
the cliffs are generally much lower.

--- SENTENCE 419 ---
They are, for example,
200 feet high at Le'ala Point.

--- SENTENCE 420 ---
At Maga Point, a buried tuff

--- SENTENCE 421 ---
Daly was uncertain about the origin of the cliffs along the southern coasts of both islands, but until more detailed soundings offshore were available, he decided that they may be the result of either collapse to the south, which formed a caldera, or the result of enormous landslips which eliminated

large portions of the island.

--- SENTENCE 422 ---
Stearns (1944) thought they might have been formed by marine erosion along the rift zones during a submergence of 400 feet.

--- SENTENCE 423 ---
As for the cliffs along the northern and western coasts, both Daly and Stearns agreed that they were the product of marine abrasion.

--- SENTENCE 424 ---
Soundings available now off the southern coasts of both islands do not indicate a caldera to the south (Fig. 3).

--- SENTENCE 425 ---
On-shore geology also does not indicate a caldera to the south.

--- SENTENCE 426 ---
Thus, the cliffs are probably not caldera boundary fault-line scarps.

--- SENTENCE 427 ---
It is still difficult to test Daly's landslip hypothesis.

--- SENTENCE 428 ---
It is believed, however, that the cliffs along the southern coasts, as well as the rest of the cliffs around both islands, have been cut by the sea.

--- SENTENCE 429 ---
Le'olo Ridge on Ofu and the high cliff behind Olosega village are also paralleling rift zones.

--- SENTENCE 430 ---
This is also true for the cliffs at Samo'i on Ofu and north of Lotofaga, near Sili, on Olosega.

--- SENTENCE 431 ---
It, therefore, is likely that the initial cliff was formed by normal faulting along the rift zones.

--- SENTENCE 432 ---
Faulting along rift zones is common on Hawaiian volcances, where they have been termed lateral faults by Wentworth and Macdonald (1953, p. 14 and 21).

--- SENTENCE 433 ---
This type of faulting was probably common along the rift zones of the Ofu-Olosega volcano, also.

--- SENTENCE 434 ---
The cliff extending from about three-fourths of a mile west of Agaputuputu to Asaga Strait on the north coast of Ofu, and the cliff extending along the north coast of Olesega

from Asaga Strait to Faiava in Sili were formed by faulting that produced the southern wall of the caldera.

--- SENTENCE 435 ---
Subsequent marine erosion has modified them to fault-line scarps.

--- SENTENCE 436 ---
Prior to the eruption of the Nu'utele tuff cone, the cliffs along the western coast of Ofu were actively being cut by the sea.

--- SENTENCE 437 ---
Sea cliffs being cut today usually do not have large, continuous talus deposits at their bases - sea cliffs in Hawaii are an example of this (Moberly and Chamberlain, p. 20-23).

--- SENTENCE 438 ---
With the development of coral reefs offshore, like the present fringing reef around both islands, active cliff-cutting would probably cease, as wave energy would now be expended upon the offshore reaf.

--- SENTENCE 439 ---
Consequently, deposits of talus and landslide material would accumulate, as, for example, along the southern Ofu and Olosega coastlines where large talus and landslide deposits have accumulated at the base of the cliffs.

--- SENTENCE 440 ---
Various stages in this sequence occur along the northern coastlines.

--- SENTENCE 441 ---
Long continued wave attack can form extremely high cliffs.

--- SENTENCE 442 ---
For example, Stearns (1947, p. 11-12) believes that the high cliffs which are over 3000 feet high along the northern coast of Molokai, in Hawaii, have been cut by the sea.

### IGNEOUS ROCKS

### General Statement

Igneous rocks comprise the entire mass of Ofu and Olosega islands, except for a fringe of recent calcareous sediment along the coastline.

--- SENTENCE 521 ---
They occur as thin lava flows predominantly, but also as intrusive wocks in the form of dikes and plugs as pyroclastic rocks.

--- SENTENCE 522 ---
The igneous rocks are divided into four series: (1) the Extra-caldera Volcanics of lava flows and pyroclastics which belong to the pre-caldera or shield-building stage, (2) the Intrusive Volcanics of dikes and plugs which fed numerous flows late in the development of the volcano, particularly during the formation of the caldera, (3) the Intra-caldera Volcanics of flows which ponded within the caldera and partially filled it, and (4) the Late Volcanics of tuff and submarine volcanism, which occurred after extensive erosion of the volcano.

--- SENTENCE 523 ---
Rock descriptions that follow are based on specimen identification of texture and mineralogy using a 20-power hand-lens.

--- SENTENCE 524 ---
Thus, mineralogical and petrological terms dependent on optical, chemical, or other more precise analytical methods generally cannot be used.

--- SENTENCE 525 ---
Nevertheless, certain associations that are common elsewhere are suspected and should be tested by a detailed petrographic study.

--- SENTENCE 526 ---
For instance, the pyroxene of the ankaramite flows is probably augite in most cases, and alteration products of olivine are

probably iddingsite.

--- SENTENCE 527 ---
Macdonald (1944) has described iddingsite from samples collected by Stearns on Ofu.

### Extra-caldera Volcanics

### Definition

The Extra-caldera Volcanics are the igneous rocks which built the main mass of the Ofu-Olssega volcane by repeated accumulations to form a shield.

--- SENTENCE 528 ---
The series extends down to the broad ridge of the regional Manu'a rift zone, the Manu'a Ridge, and is thus over 9,000 feet thick.

--- SENTENCE 529 ---
It is intruded by the numerous dikes which comprise the Intrusive Volcanics and was later faulted by the formation of the caldera.

--- SENTENCE 530 ---
Subsequent accumulation within the caldera, the Intra-caldera Volcanics, are in unconformable contact with the Extra-caldera Volcanics along this fault.

--- SENTENCE 531 ---
The Late Volcanics are separated from the Extra-caldera Volcanics by an erosional unconformity.

--- SENTENCE 532 ---
Stearns (1944) named this series the pre-caldera volcanics.

--- SENTENCE 533 ---
Whereas most of these flows were erupted before the formation of the caldera, that term is not applied here because it now also carries in Hawaii the implication that olivine-bearing basalts (Stearns, 1940, 1946; Macdonald, 1949), or tholeiites (Macdonald and Katsura, 1961, 1962), are the predominant rock type.

--- SENTENCE 534 ---
Olivine basalts do occur extensively, as do picrite-basalts of the ankaramite type whose presence is generally uncommon within the pre-caldera series of the Hawaiian Islands (Stearns, 1940, 1946; Macdonald, 1949).

--- SENTENCE 535 ---
Alkaline basalts, rather than tholeiltes, are probably predominant among the rocks exposed above sea level in the extra-caldera volcanic series.

--- SENTENCE 536 ---
As in Hawaii, tholeiltic basalts may comprise the lower 95% of the shield, however.

### Lava Flows

General character.

--- SENTENCE 537 ---
The lava flows of the Extra-caldera Volcanics are of both as and pahoehoe types, as defined by Macdonald (1953).

--- SENTENCE 538 ---
As in Hawaii (Macdonald, 1953), the pahoehoe flows form numerous thin flows containing many small lava tubes, forming a billowy pattern in cross-section and with a vesicular structure.

--- SENTENCE 539 ---
As flows are characterized by a massive central phase and a layer of clinker both above and beneath the central layer.

--- SENTENCE 540 ---
Vesicles are not as prevalent as they are in pahoehoe flows, and may not be present at all in the central massive layer.

--- SENTENCE 541 ---
In addition, the vesicles of as flows are very irregular in outline.

--- SENTENCE 542 ---
Only rarely are they filled with secondary minerals in either as or pahoehoe flows.

--- SENTENCE 543 ---
Pahoehoe lava flows predominate in the lower flows of the Extra-caldera Volcanics.

--- SENTENCE 544 ---
These lavas, especially when fresh exposures are encountered, have the characteristics of tholeitic basalts in hand specimen.

--- SENTENCE 545 ---
In the upper flows, pahoehoe and as flows occur in about equal abundance.

--- SENTENCE 546 ---
As mentioned above, it is probable that these rocks belong to the alkalic suite.

--- SENTENCE 547 ---
Flows generally increase in thickness upsection.

### Intrusive Volcanics

### Definition

The Intrusive Volcanics are composed of the dikes and plugs which intruded the Extra-caldera and the Intra-caldera Volcanics.

--- SENTENCE 713 ---
Only a single dike, however, appears to have intruded the Intra-caldera Volcanics.

--- SENTENCE 714 ---
This would indicate that the Intrusive Volcanics are generally older than the Intra-caldera Volcanics, but are younger than the Extra-caldera Volcanics.

--- SENTENCE 715 ---
Both Daly (1924) and Stearns (1944) made note of the dikes.

--- SENTENCE 716 ---
Stearns referred to the numerous dikes as the "dike" complex."

--- SENTENCE 717 ---
It is named here the Intrusive Volcanics to also include two plugs.

### Dikes

General character.

--- SENTENCE 718 ---
The general character of the dikes has been discussed earlier in this report.

--- SENTENCE 719 ---
The dike complex which they form is about 3000 feet wide in its present exposure, and is in a concentric peripheral pattern outlining the caldera boundary.

--- SENTENCE 720 ---
A few dikes are aligned along the rift mones.

--- SENTENCE 721 ---
Wearly all of the dikes are vertical; a few within the peripheral dike complex dip steeply towards the center of the

calders.

--- SENTENCE 722 ---
On the average, the dikes are about 2 to 4 feet thick, but vary from only a few inches to over 70 feet thick, Several of the dikes have horizontal, polygonal jointing, like that reported in Hawaii (Wentworth and Macdonald, 1953, p. 86-7).

--- SENTENCE 723 ---
The jointing is better developed in thicker dikes.

--- SENTENCE 724 ---
Glassy selvage is common along the margins of the dikes, but attains only an inch thickness in even the widest dike.

--- SENTENCE 725 ---
Many of the dikes are very dense.

--- SENTENCE 726 ---
Many, however, are moderately vesicular with bands of vesicles aligned parallel to the contact with the surrounding rock.

--- SENTENCE 727 ---
These dikes which are moderately vesicular most likely were intruded at shallow depths of less than 200 feet (Wentworth and Macdonald, 1953, p. 87-8).

--- SENTENCE 728 ---
The dikes of the peripheral dike complex are closely spaced.

--- SENTENCE 729 ---
Often, the intruded material has been disturbed.

--- SENTENCE 730 ---
Disruption, however, is probably the result of faulting during the time of the formation of the caldera, rather than the result of the intrusion of dikes.

--- SENTENCE 731 ---
Only rarely is a lava flow seen bent upwards by a dike.

--- SENTENCE 732 ---
Composite dikes are not unusual, and a few multiple dikes are exposed.

--- SENTENCE 733 ---
Along the rift zones, dikes are widely spaced.

--- SENTENCE 734 ---
Non-porphyritic basalts, picrite-basalts, and feldspar-phyric basalts are the most common rock types forming dikes.

--- SENTENCE 735 ---
Granularity in the dikes varies from aphanitic to holocrystalline.

--- SENTENCE 736 ---
Dikes, however, are accessible for sampling at only a few areas.

At Samo'i on Ofu, an aphanitic, dense basaltic dike contains scattered dunite inclusions 9 millimeters in diameter.

### Intra-caldera Volcanics

### Definition

The lava flows, pyroclastic rocks, and breccias which accumulated within the caldera, are defined here as the Intra-caldera Volcanics.

--- SENTENCE 776 ---
They crop out only within the depression of the north-central part of Ofu and in the Vainu'ulua Peak area.

--- SENTENCE 777 ---
Their contact with the Extra-caldera Volcanics marks the boundary of the caldera, which is so vague and broad that it might better be called a caldera boundary zone.

--- SENTENCE 778 ---
### Lava flows and pyroclastics

The lava flows of the Intra-caldera Volcanics represent the flows that accumulated within the caldera, the only remaining expression of which is the depression of north-central Ofu.

--- SENTENCE 779 ---
They are essentially horizontal, with possibly a slight dip of not more than 2° into the center of the depression.

--- SENTENCE 780 ---
Some of the flows are considerably thicker than the flows of the Extra-caldera Volcanics, due to pending.

--- SENTENCE 781 ---
Otherwise, the flows are very similar to the flows of the Extra-caldera Volcanics.

--- SENTENCE 782 ---
Pyroclastic rocks are almost entirely cinder, ash, and agglutinate, and is present only in minor amounts.

### Breccias

Vainu'ulua Peak, at the eastern tip of Ofu, is a mass of volcanic breccia cut by hundreds of dikes and the two plugs of the Intrusive Volcanics.

--- SENTENCE 802 ---
Bedding within the breccia is

poorly developed, but in some areas the beds appear to be dipping north.

--- SENTENCE 803 ---
Blocks of the breccia along the coastline are an angular, poorly-sorted accumulation of fresh basalt, ankaramite, and diabase blocks mixed with cinder and agglutinate.

--- SENTENCE 804 ---
Very little ash is present.

--- SENTENCE 805 ---
Blocks range in size from less than a foot to about 3 feet.

--- SENTENCE 806 ---
At the top of the sea cliff along the northern coast of Ofu, at the western end of the razor-back ridge near Agaputuputu, breccies crop out between the hundreds of dikes forming the peripheral dike complex.

--- SENTENCE 807 ---
The breccies are dipping steeply to the north.

--- SENTENCE 808 ---
Bedding within the breccies here is much better developed than in the breccies of the Vainu'ulua Peak area.

--- SENTENCE 809 ---
The breccie, sampled from large talus blocks at sea level, is very similar to the breccies in the Vainu'ulua Peak area, but contains more cinder and has an open structure.

--- SENTENCE 810 ---
The breccias on Ofu are typical of talus breccias described by numerous workers in the Hawaiian Islands as having accumulated at the base of escarpments (Stearns and Vaksvik, 1935, p. 84-6; Stearns and Macdonald, 1946, p. 19-20; Macdonald, Davis and Cox, 1960, p. 41-2, 34-7).

--- SENTENCE 811 ---
On Ofu, these breccias are also probably talus breccias which accumulated at the base of the southern wall of the caldera.

### Late Volcanics

### Definition

Following the cessation of the frequent shield-building and caldera-filling eruptions, two eruptions occurred on the

Ofu-Olosega volcano following a long period of erosion: the eruption of the Nu'usilaelae Tuff and one known submarine eruption.

--- SENTENCE 812 ---
These two eruptions comprise the Late Volcanics.

--- SENTENCE 813 ---
### Nu usilaelae Tuff

A phreatomagmatic explosion on the submarine ledge off the western coast of Ofu built a tuff cone about 3800 feet in diameter and probably not much higher than 300 feet.

--- SENTENCE 814 ---
All that remains of the tuff cone now are the islands of Nu'usilaelae and Nu'utele.

--- SENTENCE 815 ---
The tuff produced by the eruption is here named the Nu'usilaelae Tuff, after the small island of the same name (Kear and Wood (1959) have applied the name "Nu'utele" to a deposit of sand in Western Samoa).

--- SENTENCE 816 ---
Dips average 30° on the cone.

--- SENTENCE 817 ---
The gradient at the edge of the bench approximately 3200 feet offshore in this area steepens rapidly, indicating that the tuff cone may have originally been open to the sea.

--- SENTENCE 818 ---
It has been cut by the +5-foot stand of the sea.

--- SENTENCE 819 ---
The tuff is a dark red to yellow-brown, palagonitized, vitric-crystal-lithic lapilli tuff.

--- SENTENCE 820 ---
Individual beds vary in thickness from 1 inch to over 4 feet.

--- SENTENCE 821 ---
The crystals are too small to distinguish in hand specimen but are both clear (feldspar?) and dark (augite?).

--- SENTENCE 822 ---
Lithic fragments, especially on Nu usilaelae Island, are of angular dense basalt varying from less than an inch to nearly 1 foot in size.

--- SENTENCE 823 ---
The vitric material appears to be palagonitized lapilli-sized fragments of pumice studded with minute clear crystals.

--- SENTENCE 824 ---
No ceral

fragments occur in the tuff.

--- SENTENCE 825 ---
Interstices of the tuff are coated with a white amorphous-appearing mineral (chalcedony?) and a clear, needle-shaped mineral (zeolite?).

### Submarine volcanism

--- SENTENCE 826 ---
According to Coulter (1941, p. 13), a 1921 report by Thompson on the geology Western Samoa describes a submarine eruption between Olosega and Tabu in 1866.

--- SENTENCE 827 ---
Steams (1944, p. 1313, 1314) mentions a submarine eruption about 2.2 miles southeast of Maga Point on Olosega, along the regional Manu'a rift zone also in 1866.

--- SENTENCE 828 ---
Kear and Wood make note of such an eruption, but give its date as 1866 in one section (p. 15), then in another section give the date as 1867-8 (p. 61).

--- SENTENCE 829 ---
The USC and GS chart for eastern Samoan waters (No. 4190) identifies a submarine volcano also along the regional Manu'a rift zone, 5.2 miles southeast of Olosega, close to Tatu Island.

--- SENTENCE 830 ---
The position and actual date of such an eruption, then, is indefinite, but evidently a submarine eruption did occur between Olosega and Ta'u Islands in the late 1860's.

--- SENTENCE 831 ---
If the vent is actually where Stearns locates it, the eruption may have been related to the Ofu-Olosega volcano.

### SEDIMENTARY DEPOSITS

### Beachrock

Beachrock, a cemented calcareous sandstone or conglomerate, occurs within the intertidal zone along beaches and on the 15 feet bench.

--- SENTENCE 832 ---
The beachrock within the intertidal zone is composed of thin beds which are usually less than a foot thick.

--- SENTENCE 833 ---
Where considerable beach erosion has taken place, as at the head of the Olosega village reef channel, over 6 feet of thin-bedded beachrock sections are exposed.

--- SENTENCE 834 ---
It always has a seaward dip, generally slightly less than the beach foreslope.

--- SENTENCE 835 ---
The grains are sub-angular to rounded remains of calcareous marine organisms, which have been cemented by calcium carbonate.

--- SENTENCE 836 ---
They are predominately of sand size on the Wentworth scale.

--- SENTENCE 837 ---
Only minor amounts of volcanic detritus are present in the beachrock.

--- SENTENCE 838 ---
Along the north coast of Ofu, however, from Sinapoto to Oneonetele, and along the northern portion of the eastern coast of Olosega, large subrounded basalt talus boulders have been cemented into the beach rock.

--- SENTENCE 839 ---
All deposits of beach rock on both islands are being eroded today.

--- SENTENCE 840 ---
Beachrock also forms part of the 12 to 15 foot bench on Tauga Point, Ofu, and Leaumasili Point, 'Imoa Point, and Le'ala

--- SENTENCE 841 ---
It also possibly occurs in some other areas along the northern portion of the eastern coast.

--- SENTENCE 842 ---
No bedding in the beachrock is evident.

--- SENTENCE 843 ---
It appears to be a deposit on a benched surface of igneous rock.

--- SENTENCE 844 ---
It is almost

entirely composed of poorly sorted, angular to sub-angular volcanic particles, some large coral fragments, and other smaller calcareous marine organic fragments.

--- SENTENCE 845 ---
All are cemented by calcium carbonate.

--- SENTENCE 846 ---
Grain size varies from gravel to sand size on the Wentworth scale.

--- SENTENCE 847 ---
The volcanic grains are olivine basalts, ankaramites, feldspar-rich basalts, and tuffs.

--- SENTENCE 848 ---
Grains of augite and olivine, some altered to iddingsite, are also present.

--- SENTENCE 849 ---
### Unconsolidated Calcareous Deposits

Unconsolidated calcareous deposits form beaches which nearly surround both islands, particularly where the fringing reef is well developed.

--- SENTENCE 850 ---
These beaches vary in width from only a few feet, as at Tafalau on Olosega, to 100 feet, as along most of the southern coast of Ofu in the vicinity of To'aga.

--- SENTENCE 851 ---
Usually the beaches are about 40 feet wide.

--- SENTENCE 852 ---
Beach-rock very often occurs at sea level.

--- SENTENCE 853 ---
Beach foreslopes average 9°.

--- SENTENCE 854 ---
Only two slight changes in slope occur on the foreslopes, one at about the mid-tide level and another at the low-tide level.

--- SENTENCE 855 ---
The sand extends only to the low-tide level on the reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 856 ---
Very little seasonal variation in the beaches occurs, probably because of the fringing reef off-shore.

--- SENTENCE 857 ---
The sand is composed of calcareous fragments of marine organisms with only minor amounts of volcanic material.

--- SENTENCE 858 ---
The median grain size at sea level is a coarse to medium sandsize on the Wentworth scale, as computed from sand samples collected around both islands and analyzed by sieving.

--- SENTENCE 859 ---
Table V lists the various attributes of the beach sand as related to the coastline exposure.

--- SENTENCE 860 ---
The similarity in grain size attributes for the north, south, and west coastlines, as listed in Table V, is the result of the fringing reef offshore and equal periods of rough water during the year on both the north and south coasts.

--- SENTENCE 861 ---
Calcareous gravel deposits are also common along the beaches, especially at sea level.

--- SENTENCE 862 ---
The northern section of the beach at Oge, on the east coast of Olosega, is composed of disk-shaped boulders of coral averaging perhaps 1/2 foot in diameter.

--- SENTENCE 863 ---
These form a very steep foreslope and a high berm.

--- SENTENCE 864 ---
Sand samples were collected from the reef flat off Ofu village, using a short plastic tube with a sample bag attached at one end.

--- SENTENCE 865 ---
The average size parameters for these samples, as given In Table V, are very similar to those of the beaches along the western coastline.

--- SENTENCE 866 ---
Two sand samples were taken with a pipe dredge, one at a depth of 45 feet off the mouth of the northern reef channel of Ofu village, and the other at a depth of 55 feet off the mouth of the reef channel at Olosega village.

--- SENTENCE 867 ---
These sands are also very similar in size to the beach sands.

--- SENTENCE 868 ---
Small longitudinal dunes only about 20 feet high, bank against the cliffs at Agaputuputu on Ofu.

--- SENTENCE 869 ---
Their sedimentary attributes are listed in Table V.

--- SENTENCE 870 ---
No other dunes occur.

### Landslide and Talus Deposits

Landslide and talus deposits occur at the base of cliffs

around both islands.

--- SENTENCE 871 ---
They are differentiated on Ofu and Olosega by the characteristic fan-shaped bulge of the land-slide deposit as compared to the much more uniform deposits of talus.

--- SENTENCE 872 ---
Some particularly large landslide deposits occur from Va'oto Point to To'aga along the southeast coast of Ofu, and behind Olosega village.

--- SENTENCE 873 ---
At Sili, large boulders on the reef flat, scars on the cliffs, and Samoan tales testify to very recent cascades of material down the cliffs.

--- SENTENCE 874 ---
Both types of deposits are unconsolidated angular to subangular accumulations of volcanic boulders and gravel (on
the Wentworth scale).

--- SENTENCE 875 ---
In the landslide deposits, finer
material is common; in talus deposits, it is uncommon.

--- SENTENCE 876 ---
The "incongrous outcrop" described by Stearns (1944, p. 1316) as "a smooth steep black rocky slope extending up the cliff of the north shore 1 mile west of the east end of Ofu," and thought by him to possibly be a heavy intrusive or a late lava flow, is a talus slope of angular dike rock beneath the dike complex in this area.

### GEOLOGIC HISTORY

A fissure opened along the Manu's rift zone during the latter part of the Tertiary period, near the close of the Pliocene epoch or possibly during the earliest Pleistocene epoch (Stearns, 1944), and volcanic eruptions from the fissure began building the Ofu-Olosega volcano from the broad rise of the Manu's Ridge.

--- SENTENCE 895 ---
Later, as the volcano approached sea level, hydro-magnatic explosions produced a high amount of pyroclastic material.

--- SENTENCE 896 ---
Once the shield rose above sea level, though, Hawaiian-type eruptions, along concentrated rift zones, produced a shield-shaped volcano rising to approximately 3,000 feet above present sea level.

--- SENTENCE 897 ---
The early lava flows were predominately thin-bedded pahoehoe flows of non-porphyritic basalts and porphyritic olivine basalts.

--- SENTENCE 898 ---
Repeated collapse of numerous pit craters on the summit eventually produced a caldera 3 miles long and 1-1/2 miles wide.

--- SENTENCE 899 ---
Lava flows ponded within the caldera and partly filled it.

--- SENTENCE 900 ---
Total collapse within the caldera was probably about 3,000 feet.

--- SENTENCE 901 ---
It may also have been flooded by the sea periodically.

--- SENTENCE 902 ---
Talus breccias accumulated along the base of the caldera wall.

--- SENTENCE 903 ---
Flank eruptions along the rift zones continued, although the frequency of eruptions decreased as compared to the frequency of the earlier shield-building eruptions.

--- SENTENCE 904 ---
The decreased frequency of eruptions allowed the weathering of the upper surfaces of the flows, but did not allow the formation of soil.

--- SENTENCE 905 ---
The dips of the flows were steepened because the flows entered deep water close to shore.

--- SENTENCE 906 ---
Ankaramites and basaltic andesite flows were erupted, indicating differentiation of the lavas.

--- SENTENCE 907 ---
Because this differentiation proceeded without a long erosional period, the Ofu-Olosega volcano might be compared to the "Haleakala type" in the Hawaiian Islands (Mac-donald and Katsura, 1962).

--- SENTENCE 908 ---
Dikes which fed these flows rose along the caldera boundary faults, and resulted in a peripheral dike complex.

--- SENTENCE 909 ---
Other dikes followed rift zone faults.

--- SENTENCE 910 ---
A pit crater about a mile in diameter collapsed at the intersection of the southwest rift zone and the caldera wall, enlarging the caldera.

--- SENTENCE 911 ---
Later dikes rising in this area followed the faults bounding the pit crater.

--- SENTENCE 912 ---
Lava flows partly filled the crater.

--- SENTENCE 913 ---
The Pliocene dates given by Stearns (1944) for this period of development of the volcano, compare favorably with other volcanoes in both the Samoan Islands (Stearns, 1944; Kear and Wood, 1959) and the Hawaiian Islands (Macdonald, Davis and Cox, 1960, or any other bulletin in this series).

--- SENTENCE 914 ---
Stearns' (1944) suggestion of a 600-foot submergence of the volcano during the middle and late Pleistocene, might be indicated by the ledge which extends to a 600-foot depth.

--- SENTENCE 915 ---
A 15-foot submergence of the volcano is indicated by a wave-cut bench at this elevation.

--- SENTENCE 916 ---
The high cliffs were cut during this higher stand of sea level.

--- SENTENCE 917 ---
Marine erosion also removed much of the northern portion of the volcano.

--- SENTENCE 918 ---
A phreatomagnetic explosion produced the Mu'usilaelae Tuff.

--- SENTENCE 919 ---
An emergence of 10 feet followed.

--- SENTENCE 920 ---
Waves cut a new bench into the Nutusilaelse Tuff cone, about 5 feet above present sea level.

--- SENTENCE 921 ---
A fringing reef developed.

--- SENTENCE 922 ---
Further emergence of 5 feet brought sea level to its presentposition.

--- SENTENCE 923 ---
During Historic time, a submarine eruption took place along the Manu's Ridge between Olosega and Ta'u Island.

--- SENTENCE 924 ---
The eruption appears to have occurred about 1866.

--- SENTENCE 925 ---
In summary, the geologic history of the Ofu-Olosega volcano is as follows:

- 1) Formation of a shield-shaped basaltic volcano from a fissure along the Manu's rift zone, during the Pliocene or early Pleistocene.
- 2) Through repeated collapse of the summit, a caldera 3 miles long and 1-1/2 miles wide was produced.

--- SENTENCE 926 ---
Flows partly filled the caldera.
- 3)

--- SENTENCE 927 ---
Formation of a peripheral dike complex by numerous dikes rising along the caldera boundary faults.

--- SENTENCE 928 ---
Collapse
  produced a pit crater enlarging the caldera, and flows partly
  filled the crater.
- 4) Submergence of a maximum of perhaps 600 feet; a submergence of 15 feet cut high sea cliffs.
- 5) Emergence of 10 feet, growth of a fringing coral reef.
  - 6) Emergence of about 5 feet to present sea level.
- 7) Submarine eruption, about 1866, between Olosega and Ta'u Islands.

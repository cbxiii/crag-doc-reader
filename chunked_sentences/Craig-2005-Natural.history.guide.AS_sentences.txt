--- SENTENCE 73 ---
Local facts and maps

**Samoan Archipelago**.

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
This chain of 13 Pacific islands (9 inhabited) plus two remote atolls is located 14<sup>0</sup> south of the equator near the international date line.

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
It is divided into two political entities, the US Territory of American Samoa and the neighboring independent country of Samoa, formerly known as Western Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 76 ---
The youngest islands in this chain lie towards the east (Ta'u).

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
The land area of (western) Samoa (2934 km², 1132 mi²) is approximately 15 times larger than the land area of American Samoa (197.1 km², 76.1 mi²).

--- SENTENCE 78 ---
**Territory of American Samoa**.

--- SENTENCE 79 ---
The total land area of 76.1 square miles (197.1 km<sup>2</sup>) includes five volcanic islands (Tutuila, Aunu'u, Ofu, Olosega, Ta'u) and two remote atolls (Rose, Swains).

Population.

--- SENTENCE 80 ---
Polynesians arrived here about 3,000 years ago.

--- SENTENCE 81 ---
The current population (62,000 in 2005) is growing rapidly at about 1.8% per year, which equates to about 1,100 additional babies and immigrants per year.

--- SENTENCE 82 ---
Most people (96%) live on Tutuila Island.

--- SENTENCE 83 ---
The ethnic composition is approximately 90% Samoan, 4% Tongan, 2% Caucasian, and 4% others.

!

--- SENTENCE 84 ---
The main islands are steep mountains that 1900 19 emerge from the ocean floor 2-3 miles below the sea surface.

--- SENTENCE 85 ---
Peak elevations are 3,170 feet on Ta'u Island (Lata Mountain) and 2,142 feet on Tutuila Island (Matafao Peak).

--- SENTENCE 86 ---
Hot, humid and rainy year-round, but there is a long, wet summer season (October - May) and a slightly cooler and drier season (June - September).

--- SENTENCE 87 ---
Total rainfall is 125 inches at the Tafuna airport and 200+ inches in mountainous areas.

--- SENTENCE 88 ---
While very destructive, cyclones are a natural but erratic feature of the environment in this part of the world.

--- SENTENCE 89 ---
The most recent ones in American Samoa have occurred at intervals of 1-13 years: 1981 Esau, 1987 Tusi, 1990 Ofa, 1991 Val, 2004 Heta, 2005 Olaf.

Native flora and fauna.

--- SENTENCE 90 ---
Most native species here are closely related to those in Indonesia, but due to our remote location in the Pacific Ocean, the diversity of terrestrial species here is low: 343 flowering plants, 135 ferns, 25 resident or migratory land and water birds, 20 resident seabirds, 3 mammals (all bats), 7 skinks, 4 geckos, 2 sea turtles, 1 snake, and other occasional visitors.

--- SENTENCE 91 ---
In contrast, the diversity of marine species here is high: 890 coral reef fishes, over 200 corals, and several whales and dolphins.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
Insects and other invertebrate species here are not well known.

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
Species found only in the Samoan Archipelago include one bird (Samoan starling), one stream fish, several land snails, and about 30% of local plant species.

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
Five flowering plant species are endemic to American Samoa itself.

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
Marine endemics have not been identified, but a thorough investigation has not been conducted.

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
**Threatened or endangered species**.

--- SENTENCE 97 ---
Federally listed species here include humpback and sperm whales, and the green and hawksbill sea turtles.

--- SENTENCE 98 ---
Additional species 'of

concern' in the Territory include the sheath-tailed bat, 3 birds (Spotless Crake, Friendly Ground Dove, Many-colored Fruit Dove), several land snails, and others.

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
**Pests and weeds (invasive non-native species)**.

--- SENTENCE 100 ---
There are many: rats, 3 bird species (2 mynas and bulbuls), feral pigs, dogs, cats, toads, house gecko, tilapia and molly fishes, African and predatory snails, various insects, about 250 alien species of vascular plants (many of them weed species), and others.

--- SENTENCE 101 ---
On land, no poisonous snakes, but a bite from a large 8-inch centipede can be extremely painful.

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
In the ocean, sharks are generally not a problem but stepping on the poisonous spine of a stonefish can be a serious medical emergency although this rarely happens.

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
Consumers of fish

!

--- SENTENCE 104 ---
[](_page_7_Picture_4.jpeg)

should note that ciguatera poisoning has been found (infrequently) in several snappers (locally called *mu*) and a few other fish species.

--- SENTENCE 105 ---
Also, avoid eating any fish or invertebrate caught in Pago Pago Harbor, because they are contaminated with heavy metals and other pollutants.

--- SENTENCE 106 ---
Malaria is not present, but two other mosquito-transmitted diseases can occur here: dengue fever (caused by a virus) and filariasis (elephantiasis, caused by a parasitic nematode worm).

--- SENTENCE 107 ---
However, the chance of getting either by a visitor is slight (see chapter on mosquitoes).

--- SENTENCE 108 ---
Leptospirosis, a common tropical bacterial disease spread through contact with the urine of infected animals (pigs, dogs, cats, rats), is fairly common among local residents (17% recent infection history); most people have mild flu-like symptoms but it can be fatal.

--- SENTENCE 120 ---
Seasons and cyclones

Samoa's weather may seem warm, wet and humid all year, but we do have two seasons.

--- SENTENCE 121 ---
Not exactly summer and winter, because Tutuila is a small dot in a vast tropical ocean, so the ocean moderates our weather year-round.

--- SENTENCE 122 ---
Our seasons are sometimes referred to as tropical wet and dry periods, although you may wonder, when is it ever dry here?

October through May is our 8-month hot and wet summer.

--- SENTENCE 123 ---
Rainfall at the Tafuna airport weather station is about 11-14 inches per month and air temperature (averaged over a 24-hour period) is about 82-83<sup>0</sup> F (for the period 1990-2002).

--- SENTENCE 124 ---
Our cold spell occurs from June through September when averaged air temperatures plummet to 81<sup>0</sup> F and rainfall is reduced by half.

--- SENTENCE 125 ---
Actual rainfall can vary much more than this, as we all know.

--- SENTENCE 126 ---
Minimum and maximum records for rainfall over a one-month period are 0.3 and 32.7 inches.

--- SENTENCE 127 ---
The amount of rainfall we receive depends largely on where we are standing.

--- SENTENCE 128 ---
[](_page_8_Figure_5.jpeg)

harbor area is very wet -- it receives about 200 inches of rainfall per year.

--- SENTENCE 129 ---
Lata Mountain on Ta'u receives over 300 inches (25 feet) per year.

--- SENTENCE 130 ---
The Tafuna plains are much drier and receive a mere 125 inches per year.

--- SENTENCE 131 ---
If it seems like it rains every day here, it almost does.

--- SENTENCE 132 ---
In most years, the airport weather station detects at least a trace of rain 300 days of the year.

--- SENTENCE 133 ---
But a noticeable amount of rain (at least one tenth of an inch) occurs on about 50% of the days of the year.

--- SENTENCE 134 ---
But remember, the airport (where these measurements are taken) is the driest part of our island.

--- SENTENCE 135 ---
Relative humidity at the airport fluctuates in the 80s (daily range 73-90%).

--- SENTENCE 136 ---
Air temperatures in recent years typically fluctuate from nighttime lows around  $77^{0}$  F to afternoon highs in the upper 80s.

--- SENTENCE 137 ---
We now average 60 days per year at  $90^{0}$  or above.

--- SENTENCE 138 ---
Record highs and lows here are  $96^{0}$  and  $62^{0}$  F.

!

--- SENTENCE 139 ---
But if it feels like our climate it is getting warmer, it is, but the picture is a little complicated.

--- SENTENCE 140 ---
We have two long-term records of air temperature in American Samoa, the NOAA weather stations at the Tafuna airport and at Tula.

--- SENTENCE 141 ---
Temperature trends at these two sites are quite different, probably due to local conditions where the temperatures are actually measured.

--- SENTENCE 142 ---
Temperatures measured at the airport weather station have risen steadily over the past 20 years (see graph), but no such increase has been recorded at the NOAA station in Tula.

--- SENTENCE 143 ---
The airport station measures air temperatures at a height of about 5.5 feet above the ground, and it is located on the Tafuna Plains which is rapidly being built up with buildings and roads, so it is probably detecting a nearground increase in temperature that is felt by us.

--- SENTENCE 144 ---
Whether other parts of Tutuila are warming in a similar fashion is not known.

--- SENTENCE 145 ---
In contrast, the Tula

!

--- SENTENCE 146 ---
[](_page_9_Figure_2.jpeg)

station is on the upwind side of the island and it measures air temperatures at 50 feet above the ground, so it is less affected by near-ground conditions.

--- SENTENCE 147 ---
It shows no increase in temperature, as would be

expected at our remote oceanic location.

--- SENTENCE 148 ---
As summer progresses, the temperature of the ocean's surface waters also increases by about 3<sup>0</sup> F. Warmer ocean temperatures, in turn, help provide the energy to start tropical cyclones, thus the chance of a cyclone is greatest between November and April.

--- SENTENCE 149 ---
Tropical cyclones have hit American Samoa at intervals of 1-13 years during the past 24 years: 1981 Esau, 1987 Tusi, 1990 Ofa, 1991 Val, 2004

Heta and 2005 Olaf.

--- SENTENCE 150 ---
Their severity varied -- Tusi was particularly damaging to Manu'a, the back-to-back cyclones

--- SENTENCE 151 ---
Ofa and Val hit Tutuila hard, Heta's overall impact was moderate, Olaf slammed Manu'a hard.

--- SENTENCE 152 ---
A world map of cyclone tracks (next page) helps put some perspective on American Samoa's susceptibility to tropical cyclones.

--- SENTENCE 153 ---
This map shows the tracks of all cyclones greater than 74 mph during the 10-year period 1994-2003.

--- SENTENCE 154 ---
It shows that cyclones are generated primarily in two bands that lie north and south of the earth's equator at latitudes of about 10-30°.

--- SENTENCE 155 ---
American Samoa lies near the edge of the cyclone band in the southern hemisphere where cyclone activity begins to fade.

!

--- SENTENCE 156 ---
[](_page_9_Picture_9.jpeg)

Damage from Cyclone Val in 1991.

--- SENTENCE 157 ---
Note loss of forest foods for wildlife.

--- SENTENCE 158 ---
It is curious that there are no cyclones along the equator or around South America.

--- SENTENCE 159 ---
This occurs because cyclones require two general conditions to develop: (1) warm ocean waters at least  $80^{\circ}$  F ( $26.5^{\circ}$  C) to fuel the cyclone, and (2) enough Coriolis force (caused by the earth's rotation) to provide stability and spin to a developing storm.

--- SENTENCE 160 ---
Coriolis force is weakest along the equator, so developing storms there remain unstable and do not develop into cyclones.

--- SENTENCE 161 ---
South America has few cyclones due to cool water temperatures (upwelling along the west coast) or other climatic conditions (on the east coast) that are not conducive for cyclone development.

!

--- SENTENCE 162 ---
[](_page_10_Picture_2.jpeg)

Tracks of tropical cyclones during the 10-year period 1994-2003.

--- SENTENCE 163 ---
Cyclones are a regular but erratic feature of the environment in our part of the world, so it is likely that native species inhabiting American Samoa are able to cope with cyclone impacts and recover, given enough time and assuming that their recovery is not jeopardized by human-related conditions such as habitat loss due to human population increases, competition by invasive alien species, pollution, or changing environmental conditions due to global climate change.

--- SENTENCE 164 ---
Returning to the topic of seasons, some plants and animals in Samoa schedule their activities, such as flowering or spawning, according to the seasons.

--- SENTENCE 165 ---
These biological events tend to occur over a much longer period in the tropics compared to colder regions of the world, because the distinction between our two seasons is not great and growing conditions here are generally good year-round.

--- SENTENCE 166 ---
Nevertheless, seasonal patterns of flowering and fruiting mean times of abundance or scarcity for some of our native wildlife, particularly the fruit bats (pe'a) and Pacific pigeon (lupe).

--- SENTENCE 167 ---
Some animals schedule their seasonal migrations to our islands to avoid winters elsewhere in the world.

--- SENTENCE 168 ---
We see more golden plovers (*tuli*) and tourists during the October to March period when they flee winter in the northern hemisphere, while humpback whales (*tofola*) visit us in August-October when they escape winter in the southern hemisphere.

# **3.

--- SENTENCE 169 ---
Biodiversity in our rainforests and coral reefs**

There's a certain mystique about the word 'biodiversity' that seems to be associated with images of steamy jungles or wondrous new medicines, but the word more specifically refers to the number of species or 'species richness' of an area.

--- SENTENCE 170 ---
One reason why tropical areas are so fascinating is that they contain the highest numbers of plant and animal species found anywhere on earth.

--- SENTENCE 171 ---
American Samoa sits squarely in the tropics, so we should have a high biological diversity here, but we do and we don't.

--- SENTENCE 172 ---
There is a sharp contrast between the number of plant and animal species that live on land here (few) versus those that live in our coastal waters (many).

--- SENTENCE 173 ---
Most small islands in the South Pacific share this characteristic.

--- SENTENCE 174 ---
To start at the beginning, when our islands emerged as fiery volcanoes from the depths of the sea, they were devoid of plants or animals.

--- SENTENCE 175 ---
As time passed and the terrain became more hospitable, life for organisms became possible, but the plants and animals still had to cross vast ocean distances to get here from someplace else.

!

--- SENTENCE 176 ---
[](_page_11_Picture_5.jpeg)

A quick look at a map will show one reason why few land species got here.

--- SENTENCE 177 ---
We are really quite isolated in the Pacific Ocean, far from potential sources of plants and animals.

--- SENTENCE 178 ---
To reach our shores, organisms would either have to blow in on the wind, drift for hundreds or thousands of miles on some piece of floating debris, or be carried in by another organism like plant seeds in a bird's stomach.

--- SENTENCE 179 ---
The species that were successful probably got here by 'island hopping' across the Pacific, spreading from island to island over the course of many thousands or millions of years.

--- SENTENCE 180 ---
The difficulty in getting here is best illustrated by the sparse representation of native mammal species.

--- SENTENCE 181 ---
Over the past 1.3 million years that Tutuila Island has existed, only 3 mammal species (all bats) got here and established viable populations.

--- SENTENCE 182 ---
Our native species list also includes about 478 flowering plants and ferns, 25 resident land and water birds, 20 resident seabirds, 7 skinks, 4 geckos, 2 sea turtles, 1 snake, and occasional other visitors (this list does not include all the introduced non-native species like rats, dogs, pigs, toads, myna birds, and many weeds).

--- SENTENCE 183 ---
There's a second reason for our low diversity on land -- the small size of our islands.

--- SENTENCE 184 ---
In general, the smaller the island, the fewer the species on it.

--- SENTENCE 185 ---
For example, tiny Rose Atoll (0.4 sq mi) supports only 5 native plant species, 21 birds (virtually all seabirds), 2 geckos, and 2 sea turtles.

--- SENTENCE 186 ---
So, although American Samoa technically has 'tropical rainforests' due to our high level of rainfall (200- 300 inches per year in some mountainous areas), we lack the high species richness found in the jungle rainforests of Indonesia, Africa or South America that are filled with hooting monkeys, poison dart frogs, pythons and flesh-eating piranhas.

--- SENTENCE 187 ---
On the other hand, because of our isolation, some terrestrial species in Samoa have evolved over many thousands of years to such an extent that they have become distinctly different species found nowhere else but here.

--- SENTENCE 188 ---
For example, 1% of our plant species occur only in American Samoa; 30% of our plant species and the Samoan starling (*fuia*) occur only in the Samoan archipelago (which includes western

Samoa); and the Samoan fruit bat occurs only in the Samoan and Fijian islands.

--- SENTENCE 189 ---
So, our rainforest may lack diversity, but it contains some species found nowhere else on earth.

--- SENTENCE 190 ---
Turning to our marine environment, we find the opposite situation.

--- SENTENCE 191 ---
There is an incredibly diverse ecosystem just beneath the waves.

--- SENTENCE 192 ---
Coral reefs are among the most species-rich ecosystems in the world.

--- SENTENCE 193 ---
We have, for example, 890 nearshore fish species which is an amazingly high number compared to many other coastal areas.

--- SENTENCE 194 ---
To get a sense of this species-rich environment, if you were to dive on our reefs once a week, you could in theory see a new fish species on every dive for 17 years.

--- SENTENCE 195 ---
Although coral reefs are limited to shallow waters, usually around the fringes of islands, most coral reef species have eggs and larvae that can survive for weeks or months in the open ocean and get dispersed by ocean currents to new locations.

--- SENTENCE 196 ---
As a result of this genetic exchange of marine organisms between islands, there are probably few marine species that are unique to the Samoan islands.

--- SENTENCE 197 ---
Finally, superimposed over the South Pacific region is a larger-scale pattern of species distributions.

--- SENTENCE 198 ---
Most of our marine and land species can be traced back to the same or related species inhabiting mainland and insular southeast Asia.

--- SENTENCE 199 ---
From that center of remarkably high diversity, rainforest and coral reef species radiated out, spreading eastward across the South Pacific islands.

--- SENTENCE 200 ---
But like ripples in a pond, the farther away one gets from that 'center', the fewer the species (see map below).

--- SENTENCE 201 ---
This same pattern applies to corals, fishes, sea turtles, seagrasses, mangroves, land birds and plants.

--- SENTENCE 202 ---
Very few species reached here from the opposite direction (South America) probably due to the much greater distance and fewer islands in that direction to facilitate 'island hopping'.

!

--- SENTENCE 203 ---
Contours represent the number of tropical fish species (reef, inshore and epipelagic species).

--- SENTENCE 204 ---
This volcano we live on**

The geology of the Samoan islands is surprisingly interesting.

--- SENTENCE 205 ---
First, we are living on a volcano, which is resting quietly at the moment.

--- SENTENCE 206 ---
Second, our volcano is on the move -- it's traveling towards China with us on it.

--- SENTENCE 207 ---
And, finally and most unfortunately, our volcano is doomed and it will eventually sink back into the dark ocean depths.

--- SENTENCE 208 ---
About 1.3 million years ago, our volcano spewed forth enough lava to rise up out of the ocean and become "Tutuila Island".

--- SENTENCE 209 ---
Actually, just the tip of the volcano is visible to us -- most of the mountain is underwater.

--- SENTENCE 210 ---
While the tallest mountain peak on Tutuila is about one half mile high, the mountain extends another 2 miles below the sea surface.

--- SENTENCE 211 ---
It is not really an exaggeration to call the Samoan islands 'active volcanoes'.

--- SENTENCE 212 ---
These islands were indeed formed by volcanism, and the volcanoes are still active, in a geologic timeframe of course, and due to some unusual circumstances as described below.

--- SENTENCE 213 ---
The most recent volcanic eruptions were a lot more recent than many people realize.

--- SENTENCE 214 ---
In western Samoa, major eruptions occurred in 1905 when lava flows destroyed a village.

--- SENTENCE 215 ---
In the Manu'a islands, subsurface volcanic eruptions and earthquakes occurred in 1866, causing dense clouds of smoke and pumice to erupt from the ocean surface for several months.

--- SENTENCE 216 ---
One hundred years ago is just a blink of the eye to a volcano, which measures time in the millions of years.

--- SENTENCE 217 ---
We humans tend to forget how briefly people have lived upon these shores.

--- SENTENCE 218 ---
Human habitation on Tutuila, even considering the whole 3000 year period that Samoans have dwelt here, represents a mere 0.2% of the time since the sun first shone on this new land.

--- SENTENCE 219 ---
To explain our volcano's slow-motion march towards China, we first need to review the nature of the earth's surface or crust.

--- SENTENCE 220 ---
The earth's outer layer, the one we live on, is several miles thick, but that is a thin skin compared to the total size of the earth.

--- SENTENCE 221 ---
This outer layer is made up of many separate sections that seemingly float on top of the earth's molten core and move about in very slow motion.

--- SENTENCE 222 ---
Geologists call these outer sections "plates".

--- SENTENCE 223 ---
You may recall, for example, that the continents of Africa and South America were once joined together when the earth first formed, but the two continents slowly drifted apart to where they are today.

--- SENTENCE 224 ---
The same process applies to the plates under the Pacific Ocean.

--- SENTENCE 225 ---
The plate we're on is called the Pacific Plate and it is moving westward (towards China) at a leisurely speed of about 3 inches per year.

--- SENTENCE 226 ---
At this rate, in one million years we will be 50 miles closer to China.

--- SENTENCE 227 ---
It is not accidental that the islands of American Samoa and western Samoa lie roughly in a straight line.

--- SENTENCE 228 ---
Directly underneath us is what geologists call a "hot spot" of thermal activity in the earth's core.

--- SENTENCE 229 ---
It's a volcano just waiting to happen.

--- SENTENCE 230 ---
When the pressure builds up at the hot spot, molten magma bursts up through the Pacific Plate and forms a volcanic island.

--- SENTENCE 231 ---
Then the hot spot calms down for awhile, perhaps a million years or so.

--- SENTENCE 232 ---
During this peaceful interval, the Pacific Plate keeps marching onward, so when the hot spot acts up again, it forms a new volcanic island rather than build upon the previous one.

--- SENTENCE 233 ---
In other words, the hot spot stays in one place but the plate above it keeps moving.

!

--- SENTENCE 234 ---
The islands formed generally lie in a straight line that is oriented in the direction the plate is moving.

--- SENTENCE 235 ---
The new islands form on the eastern end of the chain, so the islands become progressively older as you move from east to west.

--- SENTENCE 236 ---
For that reason, the islands in western Samoa are about 1 million years older than the islands in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 237 ---
(Early geologists got this direction of movement backwards.)

--- SENTENCE 238 ---
The newest volcanic eruption in our island chain is forming about 30 miles east of Ta'u Island, but it will probably be another few hundred years before this sub-surface volcano, named Vailulu'u, breaks the sea surface (in 2005 it was 1800 feet below the surface but growing rapidly).

--- SENTENCE 239 ---
But something else really exciting also happens in our area.

--- SENTENCE 240 ---
As Tutuila Island glides westward, a part of our plate collides with another plate to the west of us (the Australian Plate), and our plate actually rips in two at this point (see diagram).

--- SENTENCE 241 ---
One piece of our plate continues moving towards China, but the other piece slides down into the 6-mile deep Tongan Trench and under the Australian Plate, never to be seen again.

--- SENTENCE 242 ---
The collision of these two colossal pieces of the earth's surface causes the seafloor to bend and rip, which in turn causes some earthquakes.

--- SENTENCE 243 ---
This is shown on the map at right which pinpoints all earthquakes (magnitude 6.0 or greater) in the

Pacific region in 2004.

--- SENTENCE 244 ---
Samoa and the Tongan Trench are a geologically active area and this is presumably the cause of renewed volcanic activity in our archipelago.

--- SENTENCE 245 ---
Recent surveys suggest how this might be happening.

--- SENTENCE 246 ---
Long cracks in the seafloor have been discovered between the Samoan islands and the Tongan Trench.

--- SENTENCE 247 ---
The cracks are oriented in an east-west direction and seem to be formed as the seafloor bends southward down into the top of the Tongan Trench.

--- SENTENCE 248 ---
These cracks may make it easier for the hot magma beneath the crust to spew upward and emerge as young lava on top of our old islands.

--- SENTENCE 249 ---
And all this is happening a mere 100 miles south of Tutuila Island.

--- SENTENCE 250 ---
We live in a very unique area.

--- SENTENCE 251 ---
Finally, most oceanic volcanoes eventually disappear.

--- SENTENCE 252 ---
As time passes, two things happen.

--- SENTENCE 253 ---
Volcanoes erode continuously as ocean waves attack its shorelines and rivers gouge into its terrain.

--- SENTENCE 254 ---
In addition, the weight of a newly formed volcano is so heavy that it causes the volcano to sink slowly back down into the sea.

--- SENTENCE 255 ---
Rose Atoll and Swains Island are good examples of sunken volcanoes.

--- SENTENCE 256 ---
In the distant past, Rose and Swains may have been magnificent mountainous islands with beautiful rainforests and coral reefs.

--- SENTENCE 257 ---
But that's ancient history now, because those islands, over a period of several million years, eventually sank out of sight.

--- SENTENCE 258 ---
All that remains are tiny amounts of coral that grew up from the peaks of the mountains as they slipped below the sea surface.

--- SENTENCE 259 ---
Tutuila Island should be around for a few more million years.

Our deep blue ocean

American Samoa is much larger than you might think it is.

--- SENTENCE 357 ---
The whole Territory covers 117,500 square miles, which is about the size of New Zealand or the state of Oregon.

--- SENTENCE 358 ---
The Territory is big because we claim jurisdiction of the ocean that surrounds American Samoa, from the shoreline out to 200 miles offshore.

--- SENTENCE 359 ---
That is standard procedure throughout the world (each country with marine coasts wants to protect its coastline and marine resources from others).

--- SENTENCE 360 ---
To be more precise, American Samoa has jurisdiction over territorial waters out to 3 miles, while the US federal government maintains control of the zone from 3 to 200 miles from shore.

!

--- SENTENCE 361 ---
[](_page_17_Picture_3.jpeg)

Most of the Territory is open ocean, of course.

--- SENTENCE 362 ---
Only a minuscule 0.1% of the area consists of dry land (all 7 islands total only 76.1 square miles).

--- SENTENCE 363 ---
The other 99.9% marine portion consists of two main habitats -- the shallow coastal waters adjacent to the islands (a'au, aloalo) and the deep waters offshore (vasa).

--- SENTENCE 364 ---
Shallow coastal habitats, with their

coral reefs and colorful fish, are quite limited in total area because our islands slope steeply down into deeper water and depths of 2000 feet are reached within 0.5-2 miles from shore.

--- SENTENCE 365 ---
So, most of our coral reefs are restricted to a narrow ring around each island.

--- SENTENCE 366 ---
There is also some coral on the tops of several offshore seamounts in the Territory.

--- SENTENCE 367 ---
The rest of our marine environment consists of deep blue ocean with a fairly flat seafloor 2-3 miles below the sea surface.

--- SENTENCE 368 ---
The reason for the blue color of the ocean is an interesting one and it is a key factor to understanding our ocean ecosystem, so we need to get technical for a moment.

--- SENTENCE 369 ---
Water by itself is highly transparent with a bluish tinge.

--- SENTENCE 370 ---
What adds other colors to the ocean are, in large part, small marine plant-like cells called phytoplankton.

--- SENTENCE 371 ---
The more phytoplankton in the water, the greener the water becomes.

--- SENTENCE 372 ---
Phytoplankton require two main ingredients to grow well: sunlight and nutrients (fertilizers).

--- SENTENCE 373 ---
If they have both, they grow in abundance.

--- SENTENCE 374 ---
This, in turn, supports a productive food web: phytoplankton provide food for the microscopic shrimp-like animals (zooplankton), and the zooplankton provide food for the fish to eat.

--- SENTENCE 375 ---
Tropical oceans are not green because conditions are generally not good for phytoplankton growth.

--- SENTENCE 376 ---
Although phytoplankton have all the sunlight they could ever want in the surface layer, nutrient levels there are too low to support much plant growth.

--- SENTENCE 377 ---
This occurs because the deep tropical ocean is typically stratified into two layers with very different temperatures.

--- SENTENCE 378 ---
The sun heats up the surface layer, which is about 300 feet deep, to a pleasant 84° F, but the deeper layer remains a chilly 42° F. Because warm water is lighter than cold water, the warm ocean water generally stays on top, the cold water stays on the bottom.

--- SENTENCE 379 ---
The two layers do not mix.

--- SENTENCE 380 ---
The bottom layer is where the nutrients are, but because of the 2-layer stratification, the nutrients can't get up to the surface layer where they are needed to combine with sunlight for plant growth.

--- SENTENCE 381 ---
So, conditions for phytoplankton are not very good in the tropical ocean.

--- SENTENCE 382 ---
The surface layer has lots of light but few nutrients, while the bottom layer has lots of nutrients but no light.

--- SENTENCE 383 ---
It's pitch black down there.

--- SENTENCE 384 ---
This arrangement doesn't support a very productive foodweb, so there are generally fewer fish in tropical oceans than in non-tropical oceans.

--- SENTENCE 385 ---
You might wonder, how is it that non-tropical oceans are much more productive?

--- SENTENCE 386 ---
The answer is, again, temperature.

--- SENTENCE 387 ---
Away from the tropics, seasonal changes in water temperature cause the water to mix.

--- SENTENCE 388 ---
Winter temperatures cool the upper layer causing it to sink and mix with the bottom layer, and when this occurs, some nutrients are brought up to the surface.

--- SENTENCE 389 ---
The nutrients in shallow sunlit waters stimulate phytoplankton growth, thus fueling a more productive foodweb.

--- SENTENCE 390 ---
In the tropics, the 2-layer stratification of the ocean persists year-round because hot sun keeps the surface layer warm.

--- SENTENCE 391 ---
The tropical ocean has been called a 'biological desert' for this reason.

--- SENTENCE 392 ---
That's an exaggeration, of course, because all the tuna out there are finding something to eat.

--- SENTENCE 393 ---
And, many other species live out there as well, from an occasional whale, dolphin, sea turtle or seabird, to numerous species of fish and invertebrates such as jellyfish and shrimp-like crustaceans.

!

--- SENTENCE 394 ---
What are some of the marine resources in our offshore waters?

--- SENTENCE 395 ---
Three ocean resources of potential interest to American Samoa are fish, minerals and the water itself.

--- SENTENCE 396 ---
Several kinds of food and sport fish are present in modest numbers: tuna, *masimasi*, marlin, wahoo, sharks, and flying fish.

--- SENTENCE 397 ---
Surveys indicate, however, that the abundance of oceanic fishes within our 200-mile limit is probably not high enough to warrant significant commercial development of offshore fisheries.

--- SENTENCE 398 ---
That's the main reason

why the big tuna boats that deliver to American Samoa's canneries travel far beyond our 200-mile zone to other locations where the tuna are more abundant (the canneries are located here only to gain duty-free access to US markets).

--- SENTENCE 399 ---
Another resource mentioned from time to time are mineral deposits, such as manganese nodules, that lie on the seafloor.

--- SENTENCE 400 ---
However, these nodules, even if present in our waters, are too deep for economic extraction by current technologies.

--- SENTENCE 401 ---
Perhaps a more exploitable resource in the future involves the temperature of the ocean's cold bottom layer.

--- SENTENCE 402 ---
Scientists are working on a technology that extracts energy (to produce electricity) through a heat-exchange mechanism that is made possible by the large temperature difference between the tropical ocean's warm surface and cold bottom layers.

--- SENTENCE 403 ---
A demonstration facility for this technology has been operating in Hawaii since the 1970's, but it has been an uneconomical venture so far.

--- SENTENCE 404 ---
The two requirements for this technology -- a large temperature differential in the ocean, and easy access to this temperature difference by land-based facilities -- are met in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 405 ---
Will our future electricity needs be powered by our own blue ocean?

!

--- SENTENCE 406 ---
**

The islands of American Samoa are blessed with an abundance of coral (about 200 species), so this article presents an introduction to these unusual organisms.

--- SENTENCE 407 ---
Corals are animals like ourselves, although that may not be readily apparent because many look like whitish rocks, especially those washed up on the beach.

--- SENTENCE 408 ---
In a sense, corals are indeed partly rock, because only the outer thin layer of the coral is inhabited by the coral animal itself.

--- SENTENCE 409 ---
In that way, corals are like large trees – the inner part is hard and provides structural support, the outer part is the living, growing organism.

--- SENTENCE 410 ---
And, like trees, most coral animals are permanently attached to one spot on the reef.

!

--- SENTENCE 411 ---
[](_page_19_Picture_4.jpeg)

Seven general kinds of coral growth forms.

--- SENTENCE 412 ---
The coral rubble that Samoans traditionally spread outside their houses, and the coral rocks along our beaches, are old, dead pieces that broke off the reef during a storm, got tumbled around and tossed up on the beach.

--- SENTENCE 413 ---
Living corals grow primarily on the outer reef flat and in deeper water.

--- SENTENCE 414 ---
Although they take varied shapes, the coral animals inhabiting their surfaces are similar.

--- SENTENCE 415 ---
They look somewhat like miniature sea anemones (*matamalu*, *ulumane*) or upside-down jellyfish (*alualu*) with short tentacles that give the coral a slightly fuzzy appearance when the tentacles are extended.

--- SENTENCE 416 ---
Each single coral animal is called a polyp, but the coral branch or block we see on the reef is actually not a single animal but a colony of hundreds or thousands of tiny polyps living side by side, giving the appearance of being a single "coral".

--- SENTENCE 417 ---
The coral's short tentacles can be pulled back into the hard part of the coral when the animal is disturbed or when the coral is exposed at low tide, so even a live coral can look like a rock at such times.

!

--- SENTENCE 418 ---
In the coral shown at left, a single polyp lives in each hole, but all the polyps have withdrawn into their skeleton (the polyps of many coral species emerge only at night).

--- SENTENCE 419 ---
In the coral at right, many fuzzy-looking polyps have emerged to feed.

--- SENTENCE 420 ---
It seems inconceivable that these tiny coral polyps can build the hard coral 'rocks' that we see on the reef.

--- SENTENCE 421 ---
They do this by secreting layers of a hard substance (calcium carbonate) beneath their living cells.

!

--- SENTENCE 422 ---
It's as if each tiny polyp built a rock-solid house for itself but then, as it grows bigger, it decides to closeoff the bottom rooms in its house.

--- SENTENCE 423 ---
Then it grows some more and closes-off another layer of bottom rooms, and so on.

--- SENTENCE 424 ---
In this way, the coral polyp always lives in the outer, top layer which has been built upon layers and layers of rooms below.

--- SENTENCE 425 ---
Each polyp also cements its house to those of its adjacent neighbors which strengthens the whole structure, resembling a solidly built high-rise apartment complex.

--- SENTENCE 426 ---
Adding on these new rooms is a slow process.

--- SENTENCE 427 ---
Growth varies from about 0.5-3 inches per year depending on the species.

--- SENTENCE 428 ---
The photo on the right shows this nicely (it was part of a science project).

--- SENTENCE 429 ---
When this particular coral was living on the reef, it was stained with a harmless red die that was

absorbed only by the outside layer of living tissue.

--- SENTENCE 430 ---
Then, as the coral grew, it added the red stain to the new skeleton it was making, thus marking the size of the coral at the time it was stained.

--- SENTENCE 431 ---
After the stain was used up, the newly laid skeleton was again white.

--- SENTENCE 432 ---
After a one-year period, the coral was sawed in half and we can clearly see the pink band inside the coral.

--- SENTENCE 433 ---
The distance from the pink band to the new outer edge of the coral is the amount that this coral grew in one year (about 0.3 inches in this case, as measured on the actual sample).

!

--- SENTENCE 434 ---
[](_page_20_Picture_8.jpeg)

Over very long time periods, these corals grow into massively strong reef structures that can bear the brunt of powerful waves that crash upon them day after day.

--- SENTENCE 435 ---
The very largest corals on our reefs may be hundreds of years old.

--- SENTENCE 436 ---
Corals are one of the few organisms on earth that continually build on top of their old 'houses', forming such large solid structures.

--- SENTENCE 437 ---
This is not like a bird that might build its nest on top of another nest, because both of these nests decay and disappear in a short time.

--- SENTENCE 438 ---
In fact, most organisms on earth leave little trace after they die as their bones or shells disintegrate (dust to dust).

--- SENTENCE 439 ---
They build structures much larger and longer-lasting than the Egyptian pyramids.

--- SENTENCE 440 ---
What other organism can do this (except modern man with his steel and cement)?

--- SENTENCE 441 ---
Consider Swains Island or Rose Atoll, for example.

--- SENTENCE 442 ---
Both are the remnants of old volcanoes that, after millions of years, finally sank back down beneath the ocean's surface and disappeared altogether as volcanic islands usually do (see Chapter 4).

--- SENTENCE 443 ---
But as they slowly sank, the coral continued to grow on top of the submerged mountain tops, layer by layer, keeping pace with the sinking rate of the mountain.

--- SENTENCE 444 ---
The thickness of the coral there now is probably hundreds or thousands of feet thick on top of the old mountain peak and it's all that's left poking above the ocean surface.

--- SENTENCE 445 ---
Were it not for this thick coral formation on top of these old mountains, Swains Island and Rose Atoll would not exist today.

--- SENTENCE 446 ---
When corals turn white and die**

Coral reefs in American Samoa have turned pure white on several occasions in recent years.

--- SENTENCE 447 ---

--- SENTENCE 448 ---
Two very different kinds of stress cause corals to turn white: (1) clorox bleach, and (2) warm water temperatures.

--- SENTENCE 449 ---
Clorox bleaching happens from time to time when a foolish fisherman dumps clorox onto the reef to kill fish.

--- SENTENCE 450 ---

--- SENTENCE 451 ---

--- SENTENCE 452 ---
It only takes a slight increase above normal water temperature to bleach the coral.

--- SENTENCE 453 ---
Bleaching can be caused by a short-term exposure (1-2 days) at temperature elevations of 3-4 degrees, or by long-term exposure (weeks) at elevations of only 1-2 degrees.

--- SENTENCE 454 ---
To a diver, this may look like a pretty snowfall on the reef, but it indicates that the reef is seriously stressed.

--- SENTENCE 455 ---

--- SENTENCE 456 ---
They live close to the hottest temperature that they can tolerate, so it doesn't take much to push them over the limit.

#### **9.

--- SENTENCE 501 ---
Palolo swarming**

Once or twice a year, *palolo* swarm to the surface of the sea in great numbers.

--- SENTENCE 502 ---
Samoans eagerly await this night and scoop up large amounts of this delicacy along the shoreline with hand nets.

--- SENTENCE 503 ---
This gift from the sea was traditionally greeted with necklaces made from the fragrant *moso'oi* flower and the night of the *palolo* was and still remains a happy time of celebration.

--- SENTENCE 504 ---
The rich taste of *palolo* is enjoyed raw or fried with butter, onions or eggs, or spread on toast.

--- SENTENCE 505 ---
*Palolo* is the edible portion of a polychaete worm (*Eunice viridis*) that lives in shallow coral reefs throughout the south central Pacific, although they do not swarm at all of these locations.

--- SENTENCE 506 ---
This phenomenon is well known in Samoa, Rarotonga, Tonga, Fiji, the Solomon Islands and Vanuatu.

--- SENTENCE 507 ---
*Palolo* are about 12 inches long and live in burrows dug into the coral pavement on the outer reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 508 ---
They are composed of two distinct sections (see drawing).

--- SENTENCE 509 ---
The front section is the basic segmented polychaete with eyes, mouth, etc., followed by a string of segments called the "epitoke" that contain reproductive gametes colored bluegreen (females) or tan (males).

--- SENTENCE 510 ---
Each epitoke segments bear a tiny eyespot that can sense light (that's why islanders are able to use a lantern to attract the *palolo* to their nets).

!

--- SENTENCE 511 ---
[](_page_23_Picture_5.jpeg)

When it comes time to spawn, *palolo* will back out of their burrows and release the epitoke section from their body.

--- SENTENCE 512 ---
The epitokes then twirl around in the water in vast numbers and look like dancing spaghetti.

!

--- SENTENCE 513 ---
Around daybreak, the segments dissolve and release the eggs and sperm that they contain.

--- SENTENCE 514 ---
The fertilized eggs hatch into small larvae that drift with the plankton until settling on a coral reef to begin life anew.

--- SENTENCE 515 ---
The swarming of *palolo* is a classic example of the coordinated mass spawning of a simple marine organism.

--- SENTENCE 516 ---
The worms emerge from their burrows during a specific phase of the moon, but the actual date is a bit complicated.

--- SENTENCE 517 ---
The swarms occur on the evenings of the last quarter moon of spring or early summer.

--- SENTENCE 518 ---
In Samoa, this is seven days after the full moon in October or November.

--- SENTENCE 519 ---
Swarming occurs for two or three consecutive nights with the second night usually having the strongest showing.

--- SENTENCE 520 ---
*Palolo* usually appear here in October, but sometimes in November or sometimes during both months.

--- SENTENCE 521 ---
This difference is due to the fact that there are approximately thirteen lunar months in one calendar year and the *palolo* use primarily the moon to time their spawning activity.

--- SENTENCE 522 ---
If instead they always spawned every twelve lunar months, their time of spawning would occur earlier every year.

--- SENTENCE 523 ---
After a few years, they would be spawning in August or July.

--- SENTENCE 524 ---
In order to make up for this difference, the *palolo* will delay spawning in some years to the thirteen lunar month.

--- SENTENCE 525 ---
The fact that *palolo* adjust their spawning time means that there are other factors beside the moon that determine the time of year they begin to mature and are ready to release their epitokes.

--- SENTENCE 526 ---
Several studies on this matter have suggested that rising seawater temperatures, tides, weather, moonlight or other biological signals may play a role in starting the maturation and release of the epitokes.

--- SENTENCE 527 ---
Once the swarming begins, the presence of the *palolo* spawn in the water probably stimulates other *palolo* to release their mature epitokes.

Rules For Predicting Emergence.

--- SENTENCE 528 ---
Everyone seems to have their own methods for predicting when the best *palolo* rising will occur.

--- SENTENCE 529 ---
Several natural clues that preceded the *palolo* rising enabled islanders to predict the correct timing for *palolo* swarming.

--- SENTENCE 530 ---
These included the flowering of the *moso'oi* tree, the closing of the *palulu* flower (a morning glory), a strong smell from the reef, brown foamy scum (from coral spawn) on the ocean, toxins occurring in reef fish, and abrupt weather changes or bad weather such as thunderstorms or lightning.

--- SENTENCE 531 ---
So, will *palolo* swarm seven days after the full moon in October or November?

--- SENTENCE 532 ---

--- SENTENCE 544 ---
Giant clams (***faisua***)**

It's always a pleasure to see one of these beauties on the reef.

--- SENTENCE 545 ---
Giant clams (*faisua*) are large, colorful and, surprisingly, they are part animal and part "plant".

--- SENTENCE 546 ---
That's because giant clams, like corals, have plant-like cells (zooxanthellae) in their tissues that produce free food for the clams.

--- SENTENCE 547 ---
When a clam opens its shell and spreads out its pretty mantle, it exposes these solar panels of zooxanthellae to the sun to make food, like a plant unfolding its leaves.

!

--- SENTENCE 548 ---
[](_page_25_Picture_3.jpeg)

At the same time, the clam also gets some food by drawing water through its siphon and filtering out any tiny food particles (zooplankton).

--- SENTENCE 549 ---
Perhaps that's why giant clams grow so large – they have two very different ways to get food.

--- SENTENCE 550 ---
We have two native species of giant clams in our local waters, *Tridacna maxima* and *T. squamosa,* which look fairly similar.

--- SENTENCE 551 ---
They grow to about 12-15 inches in shell length, although most found today are smaller because the larger ones have been over-harvested.

--- SENTENCE 552 ---
The largest and most famous species of giant clam (*T. gigas*), which grows as big as a large suitcase, is not native here but has been recently imported.

--- SENTENCE 553 ---
Over the past 15 years,

mariculture efforts by DMWR have introduced *T. gigas* and *T. derasa,* and re-introduced *Hippopus hippopus*, a shallow-water species that was formerly present.

--- SENTENCE 554 ---
Because giant clams need sunlight, they inhabit shallow, clear waters down to about 60 feet deep.

--- SENTENCE 555 ---
They grow very slowly; one local clam was 18 years old.

--- SENTENCE 556 ---
They spawn repeatedly over their life span and release millions of eggs each time they spawn, but most young clams do not survive, so the adults have to live a long time and spawn many times to insure that the population survives.

--- SENTENCE 557 ---
Larval clams swim in the water for about a week, then settle permanently onto the reef to grow.

--- SENTENCE 558 ---
The clams are a favorite food item throughout the South Pacific and their accessibility in shallow waters and slow growth make them susceptible to overfishing.

--- SENTENCE 559 ---
That is very much the case in American Samoa, where few remain on many of our reefs near populated areas.

--- SENTENCE 560 ---
There is a growing concern that our population of giant clams may be getting too few and far between to spawn successfully.

--- SENTENCE 561 ---
Partly for that reason, there has been an interest in growing these clams in hatcheries to supply markets for food and the aquarium trade.

--- SENTENCE 562 ---
DMWR has operated a hatchery here for many years and tried to encourage local production by supplying small clams for local 'farmers' to grow them on their reefs.

--- SENTENCE 563 ---
That effort has met with limited success for several reasons.

--- SENTENCE 564 ---
Considerable dedication is needed because it may take several years to grow the clams to a commercial size, and the clams have to be protected from poachers and predators.

--- SENTENCE 565 ---
And there are always *fa'alavelave* events that call for contributions of giant clams if anyone has some.

--- SENTENCE 566 ---
In general, giant clam mariculture here has usually supplemented family needs rather than create a commercial business.

--- SENTENCE 567 ---
Periodically, the idea of raising the clams to a small size for the aquarium trade is talked about as a possible market.

--- SENTENCE 568 ---
Harvest regulations in American Samoa (in 2005) are: giant clams taken for personal consumption must be at least 6 inches in shell length, or if sold, a license is required and giant clams must be at least 7 inches in shell length and sold with the clam still in its shell.

The *alogo* is a popular Samoan food fish and it is one the most important species of reef fish caught, accounting in some years for up to 30% (by weight) of all reef fish caught in the nearshore subsistence fishery.

--- SENTENCE 669 ---
Most are caught by spear fishermen, particularly at night when the fish are sleeping in reef crevices.

--- SENTENCE 670 ---
Daytime spear fishermen have a much harder time catching them, because the *alogo* tend to stay just out of spearing range.

** *Manini* **and** *pone* **-- two favorite reef fish**

*Manini* and *pone* are two favorite food fishes found just about everywhere in shallow waters around the islands of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 672 ---
Like *alogo*, they belong to the family of fishes called surgeonfish because of their sharp knife blades that fit into grooves near their tail.

--- SENTENCE 673 ---
*Manini* and *pone* are rather meek fishes, however, and they do not seem to wield their weapons much.

--- SENTENCE 674 ---
The *manini* (*Acanthurus triostegus*) is a small fish about 5 inches long.

--- SENTENCE 675 ---
Its coloration is yellow with vertical black bars, which looks a bit like a prisoner's uniform and that's why this fish is also called the convict tang.

!

--- SENTENCE 676 ---
[](_page_30_Picture_4.jpeg)

*Manini* often swim in large schools containing hundreds or thousands of individuals.

--- SENTENCE 677 ---
There are two good reasons for this schooling behavior -- it helps them escape predators and it also helps them get access to food.

--- SENTENCE 678 ---
First, when a large fish attacks a school of *manini*, the *manini* scatter in all directions like a shotgun blast.

--- SENTENCE 679 ---
This commotion momentarily confuses the predator and the *manini* get away.

--- SENTENCE 680 ---
Each *manini* thus has a better chance of not getting eaten if it stays in a group.

--- SENTENCE 681 ---
The *manini* also cleverly use their schooling behavior to get food.

--- SENTENCE 682 ---
They like to feed on the thin green algae turf that grows on reef rocks, but these algae patches are usually guarded fiercely by *alogo* surgeonfish and damselfishes (*tu'u'u*) who are nasty to intruders.

--- SENTENCE 683 ---
Just the sight of a *manini* gets them livid with rage.

--- SENTENCE 684 ---
A single *manini* would not stand a chance to get by these guards, but a large group of *manini* can succeed.

--- SENTENCE 685 ---
The *alogo* and damselfishes are simply overwhelmed when hundreds of *manini* descend into their territory to feed.

--- SENTENCE 686 ---
While the *alogo* futilely

!

*Manini* spawning is a spectacular event.

--- SENTENCE 689 ---
When conditions are right, thousands will assemble to spawn at a particular time and place.

--- SENTENCE 690 ---
They often spawn in or near the reef channel (*ava*) at dusk when the tide is high.

--- SENTENCE 691 ---
Their behavior and coloration are noticeably different at this time, as they swim around in a agitated fashion and change color to white with wider black bars.

--- SENTENCE 692 ---
This seething mass of fish mills about until they can't take the excitement any longer.

--- SENTENCE 693 ---
A group of them will suddenly burst upwards in the water column, spawn, and return to the seafloor again, all in a split second.

--- SENTENCE 694 ---
Although this happens fast, you can tell that the fish actually spawned because the milt expelled by the male fish looks like a puff of smoke from a gun.

--- SENTENCE 695 ---
When the spawning action really gets going, it looks like an underwater version of popcorn popping.

--- SENTENCE 696 ---
After spawning, the fertilized eggs drift away with the currents.

--- SENTENCE 697 ---
About one month later, the small *manini* that survived this larval stage are ready to settle back onto the reef.

--- SENTENCE 698 ---
*Pone* (*Ctenochaetus striatus*, or the lined bristletooth surgeonfish) are a different type of surgeonfish in two respects.

--- SENTENCE 699 ---
They are a dull brown color and they have funny teeth.

--- SENTENCE 700 ---
Their lack of spectacular coloration is somewhat of an embarrassment in tropical waters which are renown for brightly colored fish.

--- SENTENCE 701 ---
*Pone* are, however, one of the most abundant fishes on the reefs, so they must be doing something right.

--- SENTENCE 702 ---
Their teeth have evolved very differently from other surgeonfishes because what they feed upon is quite different.

--- SENTENCE 703 ---
Instead of having actual teeth to bite off algae the way that *alogo* and *manini* do, *pone* have a mouthful of bristles which they use as a comb or brush to collect the detritus that lies on reef surfaces.

--- SENTENCE 704 ---
The detritus they eat includes all the small bits and pieces of formerly living plants and animals.

--- SENTENCE 705 ---
The detritus in your backyard, for example, might include grass clippings, old *ulu* leaves, decaying coconut husks, rotting papayas, and numerous unseen dead insects.

--- SENTENCE 706 ---
Not a pretty meal, to be sure.

Detritus is found everywhere, but few large animals can make a meal out of it.

--- SENTENCE 708 ---
*Pone* can, and that may account for their abundance and widespread distribution on coral reefs.

--- SENTENCE 709 ---
Every several years or so, *pone* have a very successful spawning event, and uncountable numbers of their young (*pala'ia*) settle onto the reef.

--- SENTENCE 710 ---
*Pala'ia* are very pretty and look like small dark *alogo*.

--- SENTENCE 711 ---

This is hard to believe -- an 18 year old *alogo* was caught in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 714 ---
That's old enough to get a drivers license.

--- SENTENCE 715 ---
We're talking about the *alogo*, also known as the blue-lined surgeonfish *Acanthurus lineatus*.

--- SENTENCE 716 ---
Nothing unusual about its size -- it was the typical 8-inch *alogo* you see in the stores.

--- SENTENCE 717 ---
And I ate it.

--- SENTENCE 718 ---
All that remained were the little bones that scientists use to determine the age of the fish.

--- SENTENCE 719 ---
These bones, when looked at under a microscope, have concentric circles, one for each year, just like tree rings.

--- SENTENCE 720 ---
You just count the rings and that's how old the fish is.

[](_page_32_Picture_4.jpeg)

What's even more astonishing is that an *alogo* caught in Australia's Great Barrier Reef was 44 years old.

--- SENTENCE 722 ---
I am not kidding.

--- SENTENCE 723 ---
There were also other *alogo* in the Australian sample that were 20-40 years old, so the record age of 44 is believable.

--- SENTENCE 724 ---
These old fish were not unusually large, just the standard size *alogo*.

--- SENTENCE 725 ---
It turns out that this is not unusual for coral reef fish.

--- SENTENCE 726 ---
Recent studies show that several other species of

surgeonfish (*pone*), unicornfish (*ume*), groupers (*gatala*) and snappers (*mu*) can also live up to 20-40 years.

--- SENTENCE 727 ---
These findings are beginning to reshape our understanding about the ecology of coral reef fishes and their vulnerability to overfishing.

--- SENTENCE 728 ---
The occurrence of many long-lived fish in a population indicates that the coral reef fish community is quite stable, with a low replacement of individual fish.

--- SENTENCE 729 ---
Once a young fish gets to the reef, it may be there for decades.

--- SENTENCE 730 ---
Why would these fish live so long?

--- SENTENCE 731 ---
The answer provides some key information about the environment that the fish live in.

--- SENTENCE 732 ---
Fish typically exhibit this type of life cycle (long life span and repeat spawning) when few of their young survive.

--- SENTENCE 733 ---
That's certainly the case for coral reef fish – their thousands of eggs and larvae drift around with the ocean currents for weeks or months.

--- SENTENCE 734 ---
Very few of them make it back to the reefs.

--- SENTENCE 735 ---
So, if all their young usually die, the adult fish need to live a long time and spawn repeatedly

to make sure that at least some young survive.

--- SENTENCE 736 ---
If the adults lived only a short time, the population might disappear altogether.

--- SENTENCE 737 ---
Every so often however, during years when the ocean currents and conditions are just right, massive numbers of young fish survive and appear on our reefs, like *pala'ia* (young *pone* surgeonfish) or *i'asina* (young goatfish).

--- SENTENCE 738 ---
While this is all very interesting of course, there is an important takehome message.

--- SENTENCE 739 ---
It is easy to overfish populations with these life history characteristics.

--- SENTENCE 740 ---
Having a long life span and spawning repeatedly may be a successful strategy under natural conditions, but it also makes these fish extra vulnerable to overfishing.

--- SENTENCE 741 ---
That's because fishermen tend to harvest the larger (older) fish in the population.

--- SENTENCE 742 ---
Under heavy fishing pressure, all the old fish may be taken, leaving only the smaller, younger fish.

--- SENTENCE 743 ---
That would put the population in a very precarious situation, because the younger fish left may not yet be old enough to spawn.

--- SENTENCE 744 ---
*I'asina* in a basket trap (*enu*)

--- SENTENCE 745 ---
[](_page_32_Picture_12.jpeg)

That appears to be exactly what has happened in American Samoa because there are relatively few and/or small sizes of the species commonly taken for food left on our reefs.

--- SENTENCE 746 ---
The graph at right shows the pooled lengths of all surgeonfish, unicornfish, parrotfish, snappers, emperors, groupers, jacks and sharks sighted during extensive scientific surveys on the reef slope at the 30-ft depth.

--- SENTENCE 747 ---
Few fish were 16 inches (40 cm) or larger.

--- SENTENCE 748 ---
This depressing picture is not a sudden event – surveys in 1996 and 2004 show similar results.

!

--- SENTENCE 749 ---
Even though current levels of fishing do not seem excessive, the area of our reefs is rather small and consequently it is easily fished out.

--- SENTENCE 750 ---
Fish stocks may well have been depleted years ago – knowledgeable locals and elder Samoans recall seeing far more fish on our reefs 25 years ago.

--- SENTENCE 751 ---
A meaningful recovery will require nothing short of a territory-wide reduction in the harvest of coral reef fishes for at least 10 years, with specific protection for the larger fish.

--- SENTENCE 752 ---
That's what the long life span of the fish is telling us.

--- SENTENCE 753 ---
There is no quick fix.

--- SENTENCE 754 ---
Additionally, setting aside some areas as marine protected areas is an essential component for the recovery of fish stocks.

--- SENTENCE 755 ---
To be effective, such areas must provide long-term protection by being permanently closed to all fishing.

--- SENTENCE 756 ---
Marine protected areas that are periodically opened up to fishing may satisfy short-term goals, but they do not allow the fish to grow big and old, and it is these old-timers that produce the most fish eggs.

--- SENTENCE 757 ---
For example, one large snapper (*mu*) 24 inches long releases as many eggs as 212 snappers that are 17 inches long.

--- SENTENCE 758 ---
So, that 18 year old *alogo* was not just an unusual trophy – it was also a vital member of the fish community that spawned year after year, thereby insuring that some young fish survived to maintain a healthy population of *alogo* on our reefs.

--- SENTENCE 759 ---
Two fisheries in American Samoa currently target coral reef fish -- the subsistence fishery and the artisanal (small-scale commercial) fishery.

--- SENTENCE 760 ---
Subsistence fishing includes the multiple ways that Samoans have always caught nearshore fish for food.

--- SENTENCE 761 ---
In modern times, this includes rod and reel, bamboo pole and line, free-diving, throw nets, gillnets, and gleaning (handpicking clams and octopus).

--- SENTENCE 762 ---
Stone weirs for atule and enu baskets for juvenile goatfish (*i'asina*) are still used in Manu'a.

--- SENTENCE 763 ---
On Tutuila, subsistence catches appear to be modest in size and have been declining steadily over the past 20 years due to lifestyle changes (more people have regular jobs).

--- SENTENCE 764 ---
The artisanal fishery that sells reef fish to local stores is a relatively recent development.

--- SENTENCE 765 ---
It is conducted by teams of night divers who use underwater flashlights and spears to catch sleeping fish.

--- SENTENCE 766 ---
In the mid 1990s, many of these divers switched from free-diving to scuba diving, which greatly increased the number of fish they caught.

--- SENTENCE 767 ---
This type of heavy fishing had a significant overfishing impact on our reef fish populations, so the use of scuba gear while spear fishing was banned in the Territory in 2001.

Tuna**

The ocean around us supports a variety of offshore fishes, such as *masimasi* (mahimahi), swordfish, wahoo and marlin, but by far the most commercially important of these pelagic fishes are the tunas.

--- SENTENCE 770 ---
The most common in our local waters are albacore (*apakoa*), yellowfin (*asiasi*), skipjack (*atu*) and dogtooth tuna (*tagi*) .

--- SENTENCE 771 ---
Dogtooth tuna are occasionally seen near shore, but tuna prefer the open ocean and are wide-ranging species.

--- SENTENCE 772 ---
These ultra-streamlined fish undertake impressive oceanic travels - one skipjack caught here had been tagged near Australia over 2000 miles away.

--- SENTENCE 773 ---
But the general movements of tuna in our area are not known.

--- SENTENCE 774 ---
Local fishermen catch tuna in our area by trolling at FADs (fish aggregation devices), offshore seamounts, or wherever seabird flocks are feeding (the flocks indicate the presence of baitfish that the tuna are probably also feeding upon).

--- SENTENCE 775 ---
In recent years, commercial catches of locally-caught tuna have increased in the longline fishery that targets albacore using long lengths (extending 5-40 miles) of monofilament longline with baited hooks.

--- SENTENCE 776 ---
Given that American Samoa has two major tuna canneries and we are the No. 1 port in the United States in terms of value of fish landed (about \$200,000,000 per year), it is somewhat surprising to realize that few of the fish canned here are actually caught within American Samoa's waters.

--- SENTENCE 777 ---
That's because tuna are not particularly abundant in our area, so local catches delivered to the canneries are a small fraction of the 200,000 tons of tuna processed at the canneries each year.

--- SENTENCE 778 ---
Commercial quantities of tuna are generally located 1000s of miles away from American Samoa, so the big purse seiners and foreign longliners that you see docked in Pago Pago Harbor generally do not fish locally.

--- SENTENCE 779 ---
Instead, they must travel for about 1 week just to reach their distant fishing grounds.

--- SENTENCE 780 ---
The reason why these boats deliver their catch to the canneries here is simply because the tuna canned in American Samoa can enter US markets tariff-free as "Made in USA", and the US is one of the largest consumers of tuna.

!

--- SENTENCE 781 ---
A rather enjoyable feature about tuna is that their meat generally lacks parasites, so people eat raw tuna in a variety of forms (*oka*, sashimi).

Turtles In trouble**

In Samoan folklore, sea turtles were believed to have the power to save fishermen who were lost at sea by bringing them safely to shore.

--- SENTENCE 783 ---
The Samoan word for sea turtle, "*I'a sa*," translates literally to "sacred fish", presumably because of this ability.

--- SENTENCE 784 ---
Samoans have traditionally harvested sea turtles for food, and the shell was often made into bracelets, combs, fishing hooks, and also was used in the headpiece worn by a princess during important dance ceremonies.

--- SENTENCE 785 ---
Turtles were incorporated into Samoan songs and art, and there are even turtle petroglyphs (rock carvings) in Faga'itua and Leone.

--- SENTENCE 786 ---
And, of course, there's the legend about the Turtle and Shark that appear in the sea at Vaitogi when villagers sing a special song.

--- SENTENCE 787 ---
It therefore seems extra unfortunate that turtle numbers in Samoa have declined so much that they are now considered endangered species.

--- SENTENCE 788 ---
Although it is difficult to determine how many are left, it is clear that

!

--- SENTENCE 789 ---
[](_page_35_Picture_5.jpeg)

few females lay eggs each year in the Territory.

--- SENTENCE 790 ---
This drop parallels the worldwide decline of sea turtles due to overharvest, loss of nesting beaches, and incidental kills in fishing gear.

--- SENTENCE 791 ---
Pacific populations of one of our species (hawksbills) are "rapidly approaching extinction" according to a recent scientific review.

--- SENTENCE 792 ---
Two turtle species, the green and hawksbill, are the most frequently found turtles in our local waters.

--- SENTENCE 793 ---
The hawksbill or "*laumei uga*" (*Eretmochelys imbricata*) is usually the species that nests on Tutuila beaches.

--- SENTENCE 794 ---
This is a solitary nester, and perhaps only 1 or 2 hawksbill females now use a suitable beach.

--- SENTENCE 795 ---
The hawksbill is occasionally poisonous -- in the late 1950s, people in Aunu'u got very sick after eating one.

--- SENTENCE 796 ---
Our other species is the green sea turtle (*Chelonia mydas*), named after the color of its fat.

--- SENTENCE 797 ---
It is also found around our islands, but it nests primarily at Rose Atoll.

--- SENTENCE 798 ---
These long-lived turtles have rather

!

--- SENTENCE 799 ---
[](_page_35_Picture_10.jpeg)

complicated life cycles that involve repeated long-distance migrations to and from American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 800 ---
They start life as eggs buried in beach sand.

--- SENTENCE 801 ---
Once a female has laid her first group of about 100 eggs, she will return at 2-week intervals to lay more.

--- SENTENCE 802 ---
In about 60 days, the eggs hatch and the little turtles dart into the ocean.

--- SENTENCE 803 ---
Where they go is not known, but eventually they take up residence at some feeding area that may be far away from American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 804 ---
There they remain for some 20-25 years until they become sexually mature, at which time they return to the very same beach where they

came from.

--- SENTENCE 805 ---
After laying eggs there, the adult females then turn around and go back to their distant feeding grounds.

--- SENTENCE 806 ---
That's the basic pattern for most sea turtle species throughout the world.

--- SENTENCE 807 ---
Swim far away to some nesting beach, then swim back to their feeding area, back and forth every few years thereafter.

--- SENTENCE 808 ---
We have some very interesting migration data for green sea turtles at Rose Atoll (see map below), where a tagging study was conducted in the mid-1990s.

--- SENTENCE 809 ---
In all, 10 tagged turtles were recovered after nesting at the atoll.

--- SENTENCE 810 ---
Eight swam 800 miles directly to Fiji (unfortunately two of them were eaten when they got there).

--- SENTENCE 811 ---
Another went past Fiji to Vanuatu, and the last one went in the completely opposite direction to French Polynesia near Tahiti.

!

--- SENTENCE 812 ---
[](_page_36_Figure_2.jpeg)

It's understandable why the adult turtles do not stay at Rose Atoll after nesting, because their favorite food (seagrass) is absent there.

--- SENTENCE 813 ---
But I wonder why don't they just stay in Fiji where they have both seagrass and nesting beaches.

--- SENTENCE 814 ---
Anyway, this pattern of large-scale movements between a turtle's nesting area and feeding area means that turtle stocks in the South Pacific Ocean are all mixed together.

--- SENTENCE 815 ---
While some of "our" turtles were caught in Fiji, the reciprocal is also true -- turtles that feed in our waters probably originated from islands elsewhere in the South Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 816 ---
This mixing greatly complicates conservation efforts.

--- SENTENCE 817 ---
It means that region-wide cooperation among the island countries of the South Pacific is essential; otherwise, while we try to protect turtles in American Samoa, our turtles may be killed later when they migrate to other islands.

--- SENTENCE 818 ---
Tough federal and territorial laws exist in American Samoa to protect turtles and their eggs, because they are an endangered species.

--- SENTENCE 819 ---
Depending on the circumstances, there is a \$500 to \$250,000 penalty and up to one year in jail, for killing a turtle or importing any turtle product into the Territory (shells, stuffed turtles, turtle combs, etc.).

--- SENTENCE 820 ---
Fortunately fewer turtles seem to be taken in American Samoa, probably due to their scarcity but also due to outreach programs that inform children and villagers about the endangered status of the turtles.

--- SENTENCE 821 ---
In addition to education efforts, we must protect both the turtles and their habitat.

--- SENTENCE 822 ---
Sandy beaches are essential for turtle nesting areas, so hauling sand away from our beaches results in the loss of critical habitat for these species.

--- SENTENCE 823 ---
No beaches, no nesting turtles.

--- SENTENCE 824 ---
Although some villagers may still like to eat turtle meat and eggs, the point to remember is that turtles are a disappearing resource in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 825 ---
They are a part of Samoa's heritage and need to be vigorously protected, or they may be lost altogether.

--- SENTENCE 826 ---
It is a sad commentary that many young Samoans have never even seen a live sea turtle.

Baby turtles: look but don't touch!

Baby sea turtles.

--- SENTENCE 829 ---
They're so neat - perfectly formed miniatures, all racing down the beach together to get into the water.

--- SENTENCE 830 ---
Kids think they're cute and they want to keep them as pets, and plenty of parents think baby turtles make great pets.

--- SENTENCE 831 ---
After all, they don't bark or fight, and they're so tiny so they don't take up much space.

They don't eat much, either.

--- SENTENCE 833 ---
Also, some people in American Samoa believe that when a nest of turtles hatches and all the babies run to the sea, their mothers are waiting out on the reef to eat them!

--- SENTENCE 834 ---
Because of this misguided belief,

!

--- SENTENCE 835 ---
[](_page_37_Picture_4.jpeg)

some people collect baby turtles and keep them at home or release them on a different beach.

--- SENTENCE 836 ---
But the very best thing we can do for baby turtles is: LEAVE THEM ALONE!

--- SENTENCE 837 ---
Many years of scientific research on sea turtles have taught us that mother turtles do not eat their babies.

--- SENTENCE 838 ---
Adult turtles eat mostly seagrass, algae (seaweed), and sea sponges (the living kind, NOT the kitchen kind).

--- SENTENCE 839 ---
In fact, after the female turtle has laid eggs (sometimes two or three times in the space of a few weeks), she goes back out to the sea and leaves the area.

--- SENTENCE 840 ---
For example, after nesting at Rose Atoll, some of American Samoa's green sea turtles swam to Fiji – over 800 miles away!So, those of you with good intentions out there, rest assured that mom will not be eating her babies, and you do not need to collect the baby turtles from the beach to save them from her.

--- SENTENCE 841 ---
It is true that baby turtles do have predators, such as large fishes and sharks.

--- SENTENCE 842 ---
Nature provides the turtles with some protection however: (1) most turtles hatch at night when predators might have difficulty

!

--- SENTENCE 843 ---
[](_page_37_Picture_8.jpeg)

seeing them, (2) baby turtles are dark-colored, and this "cryptic coloration" enables them to be camouflaged as they swim over the reef, (3) female turtles can lay over 100 eggs in a single nest — when this many turtles hatch at the same time, a big jack (*ulua*) or shark (*malie*) can't possibly catch and eat all of them, so some have a chance to hide in the reef.

--- SENTENCE 844 ---
This is called "predator swamping" and is common among reptiles (lizards, snakes, turtles, etc.).

--- SENTENCE 845 ---
For this reason it is a bad idea to take "just a few" baby turtles away, and release them later.

--- SENTENCE 846 ---
The "predator swamping" effect is lost and those baby turtles will probably end up as some fish's lunch.

--- SENTENCE 847 ---
A critical reason to leave baby turtles on the beach is that THAT EXACT BEACH is very important to them.

--- SENTENCE 848 ---
Baby turtles remember or "imprint on" the beach where they hatched.

--- SENTENCE 849 ---
Like many animals, turtles have little natural magnets in their brains that allow them to home-in on their native beach.

--- SENTENCE 850 ---
Years later when it's time for them to reproduce, adult turtles, with their internal "homing devices", will seek that same beach.

--- SENTENCE 851 ---
If hatchling turtles are removed from their home beaches and kept in someone's bathtub or

bucket, chances are they will become confused and have little chances of surviving, let alone finding their home beach later (that is, if they don't just die in the bucket).

--- SENTENCE 852 ---
Sea turtles have been around for millions of years and survived just fine before humans started taking their babies off the beach, whether to "protect" them or otherwise.

--- SENTENCE 853 ---
Newly-hatched turtles, just like their gigantic moms and dads, are wild animals and are not meant to be kept as pets.

--- SENTENCE 854 ---
The ocean provides better food and a cleaner, healthier environment for turtles than humans can.

!

--- SENTENCE 855 ---
Turtles are far-ranging animals that swim thousands and thousands of miles in the sea during their lifetimes.

--- SENTENCE 856 ---
When they go to their nesting islands, they meet with other turtles, mate, lay eggs, and so keep their species alive.

--- SENTENCE 857 ---
Every time a female sea turtle finds her way back to the beach where she was born and nests there, she completes the ancient, natural cycle that keeps sea turtles alive on Earth.

--- SENTENCE 858 ---
Imagine a turtle imprisoned in a bucket, tub, or garbage can, swimming endlessly in tiny circles, never to see the open ocean, never to meet another turtle, never to help its species survive.

--- SENTENCE 859 ---
Sea turtles all over the world are dwindling in number.

--- SENTENCE 860 ---
All species are listed as threatened or endangered under the U.S. Endangered Species Act, including our own green and hawksbill sea turtles.

--- SENTENCE 861 ---
If we want our children and grandchildren to be able to see these huge, magnificent sea turtles swimming alive and free in the ocean, where they belong, we have to do our best to protect them now.

--- SENTENCE 862 ---
The very best way we can to do that is by protecting the beaches where they nest, letting the baby turtles find their own way into the sea, and not hunting the big turtles.

--- SENTENCE 863 ---
Future generations of turtles (and people) will thank us.

September and October are the peak months when humpback whales (*tafola*) visit our balmy waters.

--- SENTENCE 866 ---
It's almost impossible not to get excited when one is spotted.

--- SENTENCE 867 ---
Most of us feel inexplicably privileged for a brief glimpse into their mysterious world, and there's an uncontrollable urge to shout 'thar she blows'.

Part of our fascination with whales is their huge size, of course.

--- SENTENCE 869 ---

--- SENTENCE 870 ---
We rarely get a chance to see the whole whale, except when they make a spectacular leap ("breach") out of the water.

--- SENTENCE 871 ---

--- SENTENCE 872 ---
Humpbacks are air-breathing mammals (like ourselves) that live their complete lives in the ocean.

--- SENTENCE 873 ---
They reproduce like all land mammals, but they do it underwater.

--- SENTENCE 874 ---

--- SENTENCE 875 ---

--- SENTENCE 876 ---
These whales eat small schooling fishes and krill (small shrimps).

--- SENTENCE 877 ---

--- SENTENCE 878 ---

--- SENTENCE 879 ---
Humpbacks lack real teeth because they have no need to bite or chew their tiny food items.

--- SENTENCE 880 ---
It seems odd that the whale, which is one of the world's largest animals ever, feeds on such small things.

--- SENTENCE 881 ---
The appearance of humpbacks in Samoa is an important segment of their grand migration up and down the South Pacific Ocean.

--- SENTENCE 882 ---
During the warm months of the southern hemisphere, our whales feed in the rich waters of Antarctica, located 3,200 miles to the south of us (see map).

--- SENTENCE 883 ---
Biologists call this particular group of whales "Stock-E" (formerly called "Group-5").

--- SENTENCE 884 ---
When Antarctic's bitter winter sets in, Stock-E whales seek warmer waters.

--- SENTENCE 885 ---
They migrate northward, with some going towards Australia and others

migrating towards Tonga.

--- SENTENCE 886 ---
Apparently most of this latter group remains near Tonga, but at least some migrate onward to Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 887 ---
However, one of our whales was sighted near Tahiti, so their migration patterns still hold some surprises for us.

--- SENTENCE 888 ---
A few humpbacks might arrive in Samoa as early as July or leave here as late as December, but they are most common here in September and October.

--- SENTENCE 889 ---
They occur in small groups of adults or in mother-calf pairs.

--- SENTENCE 890 ---
Humpbacks have been sighted around all 7 of the islands in the Territory, but we don't know how many are actually here.

--- SENTENCE 891 ---
They migrate here to mate and give birth to their young.

--- SENTENCE 892 ---
And, interestingly, they stop feeding while here -- only when they return to the Antarctic do they resume feeding.

!

--- SENTENCE 893 ---
[](_page_39_Picture_10.jpeg)

While an occasional spout of whale-breath can be seen in our local waters, you can also hear the whales if you stick your head in the water.

--- SENTENCE 894 ---
Humpbacks are famous for their unique "songs".

--- SENTENCE 895 ---
During mating season, male humpbacks sing to either attract females or to defend their territory from other male whales, much like birds do with their own songs.

!

--- SENTENCE 896 ---
[](_page_40_Picture_2.jpeg)

The whale's song is haunting and complex.

--- SENTENCE 897 ---
It sounds like a eerie series of chirps,

squeaks, whistles and grunts.

--- SENTENCE 898 ---
I know that "grunts" don't seem like they could make much of a song, but you just have to take my word for it.

--- SENTENCE 899 ---
It is unlike anything you have ever heard.

--- SENTENCE 900 ---
Scuba divers can hear the singing if the they hold their breath and pay attention.

--- SENTENCE 901 ---
Snorkelers can also hear the songs, but they have to dive about 10 feet deep to get below the noisy surface layer of water.

--- SENTENCE 902 ---
The song lasts about 10- 20 minutes, it has a beginning, middle and end, and all males of the same stock sing the same song.

--- SENTENCE 903 ---
Biologists can therefore identify where a whale comes from by listening to its song.

--- SENTENCE 904 ---
For example, Hawaiian humpbacks sing a different song (than the Samoan humpbacks), because they belong to a different stock of whales that migrates between Hawaii and Alaska.

--- SENTENCE 905 ---
The whales' song is one of the world's wonders of nature and it's at our doorstep.

--- SENTENCE 906 ---
Humpback whales are currently listed as an endangered species because their world-wide populations were decimated by whalers in the 1800's and 1900's.

--- SENTENCE 907 ---
By the time commercial whaling was stopped in 1966, 95% of our stock (Stock-E) had been killed.

--- SENTENCE 908 ---
Recovery of these whales has been unexpectedly slow, probably due to continued whaling by Soviet factory ships as late as 1972 and a subsistence harvest of these whales occurred in Tonga up to 1978.

--- SENTENCE 909 ---
In any event, whales in American Samoa remain few in number, and in some years hardly any are seen here.

--- SENTENCE 910 ---
Because humpbacks use our waters to give birth to their young, it is important to protect them when they are here.

--- SENTENCE 911 ---
Enjoy their presence, but don't pester them.

--- SENTENCE 912 ---
Avoid the temptation to boat right up to them or follow them at close quarters.

--- SENTENCE 913 ---
Boaters, divers and swimmers should stay at least 100 yards away, and watch from there.

--- SENTENCE 958 ---
Flying Foxes (fruit bats)**

Large flying foxes, also know as fruit bats, are one of the more unusual animals in American Samoa, especially for visitors from areas where bats are small and rarely seen.

--- SENTENCE 959 ---
Three species inhabit our islands – two large fruit bats (*Pteropus samoensis, P. tonganus*) and a small insecteating bat (*Emballonura semicaudata*).

--- SENTENCE 960 ---
These three are the only native mammals in the Samoan islands.

!

--- SENTENCE 961 ---
The two flying foxes are especially distinctive: they are renowned for being large (with a wing span up to 3 feet wide) and active both day and night.

--- SENTENCE 962 ---
*Pteropus samoensis* (*pe'a vao*) is commonly called the Samoan flying fox.

--- SENTENCE 963 ---
It is presently found only in the Samoan Archipelago and Fiji.

--- SENTENCE 964 ---
It once occurred in Tonga but is now extinct there.

--- SENTENCE 965 ---
The other flying fox, *Pteropus tonganus* (*pe'a fanua*), has several common names such as the Insular, White-naped, White-necked or Tongan fruit bat.

--- SENTENCE 966 ---
It has a wider distribution in the Pacific, ranging from islands off Papua New Guinea to the Cook Islands.

--- SENTENCE 967 ---
In American Samoa, flying foxes can be seen flying, soaring, feeding, or just hanging in trees.

--- SENTENCE 968 ---
Although individuals of the two species overlap in size (adults weigh 300-600 grams), there are ways to differentiate them from a distance.

--- SENTENCE 969 ---
When silhouetted against the sky, the *pe'a vao* has a more triangular shape, with wings that are slightly scalloped and relatively dark and opaque.

--- SENTENCE 970 ---
Their flight appears more relaxed, usually with slower wing beats and deeper wing strokes.

--- SENTENCE 971 ---
It is not unusual to observe them soaring in the air in the day, taking advantage of rising currents of warm air (thermals) to seemingly float up and about without flapping their wings.

--- SENTENCE 972 ---
In contrast, *pe'a fanua* has a more cross-like appearance: the neck and head appear more pronounced, the wings are narrower and more scalloped, and the hind legs stretch out like a tail.

--- SENTENCE 973 ---
In flight, *pe'a fanua* tend to have faster wing beats and shallower wing strokes.

--- SENTENCE 974 ---
They are less likely to soar in thermals and generally take a directional route to and from roosting sites at dawn or dusk.

!

--- SENTENCE 975 ---
Despite these differences, it takes keen eye to distinguish the two species from a distance.

--- SENTENCE 976 ---
Close up, the *pe'a vao* may sport a white to yellowish triangular patch that starts from the forehead and extends to the back of its head, or it may simply exhibit a generally grayish head with or without flecks of white hair

(much like a graying man).

--- SENTENCE 977 ---
Its neck and shoulder areas are a beautiful russet brown, while the rest of the body has a dusty black appearance.

--- SENTENCE 978 ---
The *pe'a fanua*, on the other hand, has a basically black head and body.

--- SENTENCE 979 ---
These black areas serve to set off a distinctive band of creamy yellow on the back of the neck and which extends slightly below its shoulders as if in a cape.

--- SENTENCE 980 ---
This explains why they are called whitenaped fruit bats although the color is not really white.

--- SENTENCE 981 ---
The two species have quite different social behaviors.

--- SENTENCE 982 ---
During the daytime, *pe'a fanua* form large roosting groups or colonies of hundreds to thousands of bats.

--- SENTENCE 983 ---
These colonies are generally organized according to their reproductive status and may be composed of bachelor males, clusters of females defended by an adult male (suggesting a harem mating system), or groups of females and their young.

--- SENTENCE 984 ---
In any case, individuals appear to be relatively "faithful" to their roosts, usually returning to their respective colonies following foraging flights.

--- SENTENCE 985 ---
But the *pe'a vao* does not do this.

--- SENTENCE 986 ---
Instead, these bats usually roost singly on branches, or as pairs of males and females (suggesting a monogamous mating system), or as a female with its young.

--- SENTENCE 987 ---
When roosting, *pe'a vao* males tend to hang from exposed

or dead branches of trees on ridge tops while females roost in more covered positions on forest slopes.

--- SENTENCE 988 ---
There is little information on how long flying foxes live, although some held in captivity have lived 20 years.

--- SENTENCE 989 ---
We assume that they live shorter lives in the wild, because natural catastrophes like tropical cyclones may periodically reduce their numbers, as occurred in the early 1990s.

--- SENTENCE 990 ---
With proper management and protection from hunting, the

!

--- SENTENCE 991 ---
[](_page_44_Picture_6.jpeg)

populations will generally bounce back but this requires a number of years, because females of both species probably have only one young per year and we do not know how many of their young survive to adulthood.

--- SENTENCE 992 ---
The care and energy that both bat species put into their young is remarkable.

--- SENTENCE 993 ---
Pregnancy lasts approximately 5 months in both species, and once the young are born, it takes at least another 3 months before they are weaned.

--- SENTENCE 994 ---
Even after they are capable of flight, the young continue to receive parental care, perhaps until they reach adult size or become reproductively active themselves.

--- SENTENCE 995 ---
We know this from observations of pairs of individuals seen to alight independently on the same tree and subsequently come together with one individual (presumably the juvenile) being wrapped up in the other's wings as they settle down to roost.

--- SENTENCE 996 ---
Sightings of pregnant females and individuals carrying young in flight indicate that *pe'a vao* give birth mostly between April and June.

--- SENTENCE 997 ---
*Pe'a fanua* births appear to occur yearround but are more common in January and June to August.

--- SENTENCE 998 ---
Although their name indicates that they are fruit-eaters, both species also eat nectar, pollen, leaves, and sap.

--- SENTENCE 999 ---
They tend to consume only the "juice" of fruits and leaves.

--- SENTENCE 1000 ---
To do this, a bat will carefully chew on food (usually eating around large seeds), press the pulp against the roof of its mouth with its tongue, squeeze and suck in the juice, then spit out most of the pulp in pellets called "ejecta."

--- SENTENCE 1001 ---
These ejecta are especially abundant under breadfruit trees (*ulu*) where the bats have been feeding overnight.

--- SENTENCE 1002 ---
Among the splatter of mushy bits of the fruit, you can find these pellets of drier material that sometimes show tooth and palatal (roof of the mouth) impressions, much like a dental cast produced at a dentist's clinic.

--- SENTENCE 1003 ---
Ejecta pellets help us to identify food items that bats eat even when we do not directly observe them feeding.

--- SENTENCE 1004 ---
In addition to direct observations and analysis of ejecta, their diets are also determined through

pollen present in facial hair and in feces.

--- SENTENCE 1005 ---
These data help scientists determine which plants are important because they are preferred, nutritious and/or available year-round.

--- SENTENCE 1006 ---
At least 42 plant species provide food for the *pe'a vao* and *pe'a fanua* in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1007 ---
These consist mostly of forest tree species, such as *aoa* (*Ficus prolixa* and *Ficus obliqua*), *asi* (*Syzigium inophylloides*), *a'amati'e* (*Elaeocarpus ulianus*), *ifi* (*Inocarpus fagifer*), *fetau* (*Callophyllum inophyllum*), *mamalava* (*Planchonella samoensis*), and *tava* (*Pometia pinnata*).

--- SENTENCE 1008 ---
The bats also eat fruits grown by man such as mango (*Mangifera indica*) and *ulu* (*Artocarpus altilis*), but damage to crops is limited if fruits are harvested before they ripen on the tree.

--- SENTENCE 1009 ---
Also, when you hear the bats squealing in banana patches at night, it's usually because they are fighting over the banana flowers, not the fruits.

--- SENTENCE 1010 ---
Despite the broad similarity in their diets, *pe'a fanua* exploit flower resources and feed on trees (especially domestic ones) closer to human habitations more often than do *pe'a vao*.

!

--- SENTENCE 1011 ---
The rewards of the interaction between flying foxes and plants are certainly not one-sided, because the plants and forest also benefit from being visited by bats.

--- SENTENCE 1012 ---
When the *pe'a fanua* visit patches of *vavae* flowers (*Ceiba pentandra*), for example, they help transfer pollen from one tree to another and that, in turn, enhances fruit formation and production.

--- SENTENCE 1013 ---
Flying foxes are also important for seed dispersal.

--- SENTENCE 1014 ---
When they consume fruits with small seeds (such as *aoa*), some seeds that get swallowed do not get digested but are carried and deposited away from the tree source.

--- SENTENCE 1015 ---
In American Samoa, flying foxes are the only

animals that can carry fruits with large seeds, such as *ifi*, over distances and uphill.

--- SENTENCE 1016 ---
Studies of bat movements on Tutuila reveal that bats may commute between feeding areas up to 40 km apart during a single night.

--- SENTENCE 1017 ---
Seeds that are transported can colonize naturally bare or cleared areas and aid in the revegetation of gaps in forests where trees may have fallen from disease, age, or bad weather.

--- SENTENCE 1018 ---
It may bother us that flying foxes eat some of the fruit that we grow for ourselves, but these bats are tireless workers that help maintain the health of our rainforest, and they are fun to watch.

--- SENTENCE 1019 ---
Ruth C. B. Utzurrum DMWR

--- SENTENCE 1020 ---
Banyan trees -- lifeline for Samoa's wildlife**

In the complex world of the Samoan rainforest, each of our native plants and wildlife contributes a unique and essential part.

--- SENTENCE 1021 ---
Just as the beauty of a hymn would be less without the harmonies of different voices, so the beauty of the Samoan forests would be less if any of our plants or animals were lost.

--- SENTENCE 1022 ---
And yet, as in a choir, there are a few natural "voices" that sing out above the rest.

--- SENTENCE 1023 ---
These are the plants and animals most important for the harmony of Samoa's natural world.

--- SENTENCE 1024 ---
Foremost among these are the *aoa*, or banyan trees.

--- SENTENCE 1025 ---
Banyans are fascinating plants.

--- SENTENCE 1026 ---
Now, a lot of people don't think of plants as "fascinating".

--- SENTENCE 1027 ---
After all, they just sit there, right?

--- SENTENCE 1028 ---
Although banyans give life to countless animals by feeding them with their fruit, all banyans start out as killers -- stranglers, to be exact.

--- SENTENCE 1029 ---
Another name for the banyan is "strangler fig".

--- SENTENCE 1030 ---
The *aoa* is a member of the fig family.

--- SENTENCE 1031 ---
They earn the other part of their name by their strange way of growing.

--- SENTENCE 1032 ---
Most plants, of course, sprout from seeds that have fallen to the ground, and grow up toward the sun.

--- SENTENCE 1033 ---
Banyans usually do the opposite: they sprout from seeds that have been left by birds high in a tree, and grow down toward the ground.

!

--- SENTENCE 1034 ---
The seedling first anchors itself by sending roots into the little pocket of decaying leaves or moss where it has sprouted, then it wraps more and more roots around the tree that supports it, called the "host" tree.

--- SENTENCE 1035 ---
Soon the banyan's roots are spreading down the trunk, and finally they reach all the way to the ground.

--- SENTENCE 1036 ---
By then the fate of the host tree is sealed.

--- SENTENCE 1037 ---
It is literally buried beneath the growing banyan -- strangled to death.

--- SENTENCE 1038 ---
Eventually the body of the host tree decays away, leaving no trace behind, except sometimes a long hollow extending up inside the middle of the full-grown banyan.

--- SENTENCE 1039 ---
Because banyans will start life on almost any type of host tree, they kill very few of any particular kind, and so are no threat to other plant species.

--- SENTENCE 1040 ---
Still, they're rather...fascinating, don't you think?

--- SENTENCE 1041 ---
In Samoa, we have two different kinds of native banyan trees, both called *aoa*.

--- SENTENCE 1042 ---
One kind (*Ficus prolixa*) becomes a huge tree, with an enormous spreading trunk that seems to be a twisted net of many smaller trunks.

--- SENTENCE 1043 ---
This kind of banyan has green fruit.

--- SENTENCE 1044 ---
The stronghold of this kind of banyan in American Samoa was the Tafuna Plain, where hundreds of the huge trees used to grow.

--- SENTENCE 1045 ---
Today, only a small fraction remain.

--- SENTENCE 1046 ---
Most have been cut down to make room for the uncontrolled development in this part of Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 1047 ---
Fortunately, a few magnificent trees still survive.

--- SENTENCE 1048 ---
The other kind of *aoa* (*Ficus obliqua*) is usually much smaller and does not have such a spreading form.

--- SENTENCE 1049 ---
Its trunk usually looks more "normal", though it may have a hollow inside.

--- SENTENCE 1050 ---
This type of banyan seems to favor cliffs and steep slopes, although it can also be found on flat land.

--- SENTENCE 1051 ---
It doesn't always start life as a strangler, but sometimes grows up by itself.

--- SENTENCE 1052 ---
The easiest way to recognize this kind of *aoa* is its fruit, which is orange or red when ripe.

--- SENTENCE 1053 ---
There is also an introduced kind of banyan tree in Samoa, the *pulu*.

--- SENTENCE 1054 ---
This tree, originally from Asia, is usually planted along the coast in villages; a large one grows next to the public market in Fagatogo.

--- SENTENCE 1055 ---
*Pulu* trees differ from *aoa* trees by their much larger leaves and larger, fuzzy red fruit.

--- SENTENCE 1056 ---
Although *pulu* are handsome trees that are useful for stabilizing our coasts, their fruits are not favored by wildlife, and so they do not play an important role in Samoa's natural symphony.

--- SENTENCE 1057 ---
In contrast, both kinds of *aoa* are very important for wildlife.

--- SENTENCE 1058 ---
In fact, a good case can be made that they are the most important tree species for the fruit-eating birds and bats of Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1059 ---
There are two reasons why they are so important.

--- SENTENCE 1060 ---
First, they produce enormous amounts of fruit.

--- SENTENCE 1061 ---
When one of the huge banyans has crop of fruit, its spreading crown has room for a whole army of hungry fruit-eaters.

--- SENTENCE 1062 ---
In a single tree, I have seen three *lupe* (Pacific Pigeons), eight *manutagi* (Purple-capped Fruit-Doves), four *manuma*

--- SENTENCE 1063 ---
(Many-colored Fruit-Doves), a dozen *fuia* (Samoan Starling), scattered *iao* (Wattled Honeyeaters) and *miti vao* (Polynesian Starlings) -- all at the same time.

--- SENTENCE 1064 ---
No other Samoan trees provide such a feast.

--- SENTENCE 1065 ---
The second reason why banyans are so important is that they fruit at all times of year.

--- SENTENCE 1066 ---
Most kinds of large forest trees have a definite fruiting season: for example, *asi* tends to fruit from April to July, but some banyans have ripe fruit every month of the year.

--- SENTENCE 1067 ---
Between 10-30% of banyan trees have ripe fruit in any given month.

--- SENTENCE 1068 ---
This means that a fruit-eater can always find banyan fruit, even if all other fruits were out of season.

--- SENTENCE 1069 ---
One Samoan bird has come to depend on the reliable banyan.

--- SENTENCE 1070 ---
This is the *manuma*, or Many-colored Fruit-Dove, one of the rarest birds on Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 1071 ---
In two years of observation, I have recorded *manuma* eating fruit on 99 occasions.

--- SENTENCE 1072 ---
In 98 of these 99 times, the manuma were eating banyan fruit (the one exception was berries of the *soga* bush).

--- SENTENCE 1073 ---
One probable reason why the *manuma* is so rare today is the loss of many banyan trees, particularly in the Tafuna Plain.

--- SENTENCE 1074 ---
If this bird is to survive here, it is essential that banyan trees be protected, and, if possible, increased in number.

!

--- SENTENCE 1075 ---
[](_page_47_Picture_3.jpeg)

In addition to their important role in feeding wildlife, *aoa* trees also provide homes for many animals.

--- SENTENCE 1076 ---
*Lulu* (Barn Owls) frequently sleep and nest in the hollow trunks.

--- SENTENCE 1077 ---
*Tava'e* (tropicbirds) find large nesting shelters, and *fuia* (Samoan Starlings) and *ti'otala* (White-collared Kingfisher) find protected nest holes.

--- SENTENCE 1078 ---
From its open, sheltered base to its spreading, fruit-laden crown, a banyan tree is a haven for wildlife.

--- SENTENCE 1079 ---
Sadly, banyans today are under threat in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1080 ---
Many were severely damaged by cyclones in 1990-91.

--- SENTENCE 1081 ---
Many others have been cut down, and more are threatened by continued development, particularly in the Tafuna Plain.

--- SENTENCE 1082 ---
Some of the most magnificent banyans that still stand have been make into gigantic trash barrels, their hollow trunks filled with mounds of used diapers and rusting cans.

--- SENTENCE 1083 ---
This garbage is sometimes burned, which can kill the banyan even if that is not intended.

--- SENTENCE 1084 ---
What can you do?

--- SENTENCE 1085 ---
Give banyans the respect and protection they deserve.

--- SENTENCE 1086 ---
If your family is lucky enough to have an *aoa* on your land, please take care of it.

--- SENTENCE 1087 ---
Don't cut it, burn it , or use it as a garbage dump.

--- SENTENCE 1088 ---
If you notice a young banyan getting started, help it along by clearing away vines.

--- SENTENCE 1089 ---
If you know someone who has a banyan on their land, talk to them about its importance for wildlife.

--- SENTENCE 1090 ---
The banyan is a living testimony to the bounty and riches of Samoan nature, and is a lifeline for our wildlife.

!

--- SENTENCE 1091 ---
Natural foods for wildlife**

An animal's place in its environment is determined, more than anything else, by what it eats.

--- SENTENCE 1092 ---
By studying what the birds and bats of American Samoa eat, and when those different foods are available, we are better able to develop plans to manage and conserve our wildlife and to help these animals in a crisis, such as after a tropical cyclone.

--- SENTENCE 1093 ---
Most animals can be grouped by what and how many kinds of foods they eat.

--- SENTENCE 1094 ---
Animals that eat many different things are called generalists, while those that eat only one or a few foods are called specialists.

--- SENTENCE 1095 ---
True specialization is often a two-way dependency: an animal depends on a plant for food, and the plant depends on that animal to help it disperse its seeds.

--- SENTENCE 1096 ---
On remote islands like American Samoa, there is often not that much of any one kind of food, and cyclones can cause serious shortages of the foods that are available, so it is usually not a good idea for either a plant or an animal to rely on only one other creature.

--- SENTENCE 1097 ---
We have few bird or bat species that are specialists in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1098 ---
The technical word for the generalist, "omnivore", literally means "eats everything".

--- SENTENCE 1099 ---
Humans probably have the broadest diet of any animal -- we happily eat meat, leaves, seeds, fruit, and fungus -- not to mention Bongos.

--- SENTENCE 1100 ---
Animals that are most likely to survive in new environments, like when they first arrived on Tutuila, are often omnivores.

--- SENTENCE 1101 ---
A good example is the rat (*isumu*), which can eat fruit, eggs, crabs, fungi and probably many other things.

--- SENTENCE 1102 ---
"Carnivores" are those species that eat almost exclusively other animals.

--- SENTENCE 1103 ---
We usually think of carnivores as fierce hunters, like wolves or lions, but actually any animal that eats other animals is a carnivore.

--- SENTENCE 1104 ---
The Barn Owl, or *lulu*, is the only Samoan animal that hunts other birds and mammals, but we have lots of other carnivores, including all our fish-eating birds and even our many animals that eat insects.

--- SENTENCE 1105 ---
"Herbivore" is a description of those animals that eat only plants.

--- SENTENCE 1106 ---
This is a very general term, so it is better to specify what part of a plant is eaten, whether leaves, fruits, or nectar.

--- SENTENCE 1107 ---
Each kind of animal usually is good at eating only one, or at most two, of these parts of a plant, because they are so different.

--- SENTENCE 1108 ---
For example, to rely on leaves, you need strong teeth to grind up the tough fibers, and a big stomach to process all that material.

--- SENTENCE 1109 ---
Cows and horses are well equipped for the job.

--- SENTENCE 1110 ---
At the other extreme, to rely

on nectar (the sweet liquid inside of flowers), you need to be able to zip between lots of flowers and reach inside to suck up the small amount of juice in each one.

--- SENTENCE 1111 ---
Nectar-feeders tend to be small and energetic, with long beaks or tongues to reach inside flowers.

--- SENTENCE 1112 ---
Finally, to eat fruit, you need to be able to travel long distances, since trees with fruit are often hard to find.

--- SENTENCE 1113 ---
There are no native Samoan animals that are specialized for eating leaves (except insects and snails).

--- SENTENCE 1114 ---
However, leaves are regular parts of the diet of fruit bats (*pe'a*) and Pacific Pigeon (*lupe*).

--- SENTENCE 1115 ---
Perhaps the leaves contain a nutrient that can't be found in fruit, or maybe they help to fill up a hungry animal when there is little other food available.

--- SENTENCE 1116 ---
Nectar is a very important food for many of our local animals.

--- SENTENCE 1117 ---
The beautiful red and black Cardinal Honeyeater (*segasegamau'u*) is our most specialized nectar-eater.

--- SENTENCE 1118 ---
Its long bill and tongue enable it to reach deep into flowers, and its small size allows it to perch on even the tiniest twigs to reach the blossoms.

--- SENTENCE 1119 ---
Other birds that eat lots of nectar are the Wattled Honeyeater

(*iao*), and the pretty little parrot (*segavao*) found in Manu'a.

--- SENTENCE 1120 ---
It may come as a surprise that fruit bats also eat a lot of nectar.

--- SENTENCE 1121 ---
Bats eat flowers from many different kinds of plants, from those with large flowers (such as *gatae* and *futu*), medium sized flowers (such as *fa'i*), to those with large numbers of smaller flowers (such as *asi*, *maota*, and *a'amati'e*).

--- SENTENCE 1122 ---
The bats lick flowers using their long tongues, or sometimes take a shortcut and eat the entire flower.

!

--- SENTENCE 1123 ---
[](_page_49_Picture_2.jpeg)

Fruit is the most common item in the diet of the Samoan Starling (*fuia*), Pacific Pigeon (*lupe*), Purplecapped Fruit Dove (*manutagi*), and fruit bats (*pe'a*).

--- SENTENCE 1124 ---
Though all these animals share many of the same foods, each has its favorites.

--- SENTENCE 1125 ---
Differences in what each species eats are partly caused by the different ways they feed and by their different sizes.

--- SENTENCE 1126 ---
Since *lupe* and *manutagi* swallow fruits whole, this limits the size of fruit they can eat.

--- SENTENCE 1127 ---
*Manutagi* seem to eat no fruits larger than *moso'oi*, but *lupe* are not as limited because they have really big mouths and can eat all but the largest fruits.

--- SENTENCE 1128 ---
Although *fuia* are smaller than *lupe*, they have stronger bills.

--- SENTENCE 1129 ---
This allows them to pry open tough husks, and to poke into very large fruits like *esi*.

--- SENTENCE 1130 ---
*Pe'a* can eat the largest and hardest fruits because of

their strong jaws and sharp teeth.

--- SENTENCE 1131 ---
They have a very unique way of feeding: they take a bite from a fruit, but don't really swallow it.

--- SENTENCE 1132 ---
Instead, each bite is squeezed in the mouth, the *pe'a* swallows the juice and pulp, then flicks its head and spits out a flattened mass of leftover fruit called an 'ejecta'.

--- SENTENCE 1133 ---
The white-naped fruit bats (*pe'a fanua)* seem to especially like fruits from plantations such as mangos, and those from coastal trees such as *fetau*, although maybe they really like flowers best.

--- SENTENCE 1134 ---
Samoan fruit bats (*pe'a vao)* generally prefer fruits found in the forest, both from non-native trees like *ifi* and native species such as *a'amati'e*, *asi* and *gasu*.

--- SENTENCE 1135 ---
Both bats like figs, especially the green-fruited kind of *aoa*, and perhaps most of all, *ulu* (breadfruit).

--- SENTENCE 1136 ---
But if you see bats in your *ulu*, you needn't worry that they are taking food from your mouth -- they like the overripe fruit that has been left on the tree so long that people wouldn't eat it anyway.

--- SENTENCE 1137 ---
Our most common Columbiformes (*lupe* and *manutagi*) all love the fruit of *moso'oi*.

--- SENTENCE 1138 ---
It's funny to watch a little *manutagi* struggling to choke down a big *moso'oi* fruit -- but they get it down every time.

--- SENTENCE 1139 ---
Another large fruit favored by *lupe* (and sometimes eaten by *manutagi*) is the nutmeg (*atone)*.

--- SENTENCE 1140 ---
This is

an interesting 'fruit', since what the birds eat is almost entirely indigestible seed -- the useful part is just a thin, brightly colored network (red or orange depending on the species of *atone*) that covers the seed.

--- SENTENCE 1141 ---
This colored part is very fatty and is a good food for birds even if there is not much on each fruit.

--- SENTENCE 1142 ---
Apparently this is a successful way to disperse seeds, since *atone* is the most common tree in the mature forests of Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 1143 ---
Fruits preferred by *manutagi* include *aoa*, *mati*, the white berries of *soga*, and the seeds inside *maota* and *mamala* fruits.

--- SENTENCE 1144 ---
Lupe* eat many of the same fruits, but are able to eat a greater variety because they are a big bird.

--- SENTENCE 1145 ---
They also eat the large fruits of *mamalava*, *mamalupe*, *a'amati'e* and *ala'a*.

--- SENTENCE 1146 ---
*Mamalupe* literally means 'pigeon's mouthful': one *lupe* once took a full 30 seconds to swallow a single *mamalupe* fruit!

!

--- SENTENCE 1147 ---
[](_page_49_Picture_9.jpeg)

Nectar feeders and fruit eaters don't just use their food plants -- they can help them as well by fertilizing their flowers.

--- SENTENCE 1148 ---
This happens when pollen from one flower sticks to a bird's feathers or the fur of a *pe'a*, and then rubs off when the animal visits another flower.

--- SENTENCE 1149 ---
Fruit eaters also help plants.

--- SENTENCE 1150 ---
Most of the time this happens when they eat the fruit but don't completely digest the seeds; when the animal defecates, the seeds may have been moved far from the parent tree where they have a better chance to grow.

--- SENTENCE 1151 ---
Many plants actually count on animals eating their fruits, and so they coat their seeds with a substance that makes them hard to digest.

--- SENTENCE 1152 ---
In this way, immobile trees accomplish their most difficult task -- dispersing (spreading) their seeds.

--- SENTENCE 1153 ---
When a tree species has no seed dispersers, its spread to new areas will be limited, it can become restricted to only a few places, and it is vulnerable to extinction from cyclones, diseases, or cutting by people.

--- SENTENCE 1154 ---
A good example of such a tree is *ifilele*, which tends to occur in only a few areas on the north side of Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 1155 ---
Probably the most important disperser of fruits in American Samoa is the *fuia*, since it eats a very wide variety of fruits.

--- SENTENCE 1156 ---
It is particularly important in regenerating (converting back to forest) areas that have been cleared for plantations or by cyclones.

--- SENTENCE 1157 ---
You can see this by noting how quickly a cleared area will become covered with *lau pata*, *maota*, and *masame*, or how *nonu* or *pualulu* begin sprouting in a clearing deep in the forest.

--- SENTENCE 1158 ---
Once an area has these initial forest tree species, the area becomes more suitable for tree species that prefer mature forests, like *mamalava* or *asi*.

!

--- SENTENCE 1159 ---
You can play an important role in helping the wildlife on our islands by helping our native trees.

--- SENTENCE 1160 ---
First, avoid cutting down or burning native trees, especially slower growing species such as *au'auli* or

important coastal species such as *fetau*.

--- SENTENCE 1161 ---
Other trees are so rare or important that they simply should never be cut, including *aoa*, *tava*, and *togo*.

--- SENTENCE 1162 ---
Some plantations illustrate a good compromise between the needs of people and the needs of the forest.

--- SENTENCE 1163 ---
As the plantation is cleared, smaller, fast growing trees like *lau pata* or *atone* are cut, but larger trees are avoided, or only their limbs are pruned (cut off).

--- SENTENCE 1164 ---
In this way crops like *talo* (taro) get the sunlight they need, but it does not kill large trees that are adapted to recovering from cyclone damage.

--- SENTENCE 1165 ---
Such trees may resprout leaves and branches, probably about the time a farmer is done harvesting the crops from the plantation.

--- SENTENCE 1166 ---
Second, help native trees by planting them.

--- SENTENCE 1167 ---
You can request seedlings of native species from the Land Grant at the Community College.

--- SENTENCE 1168 ---
Always plant native trees instead of exotic (foreign) trees unless needed for their fruit.

--- SENTENCE 1169 ---
For example, *aoa* should be planted instead of *pulu* trees.

--- SENTENCE 1170 ---
Flame trees, *vaepovi* and African tulip trees may look attractive to people, but these trees benefit fewer wildlife species than our own native trees.

--- SENTENCE 1171 ---
For areas such as lawns where large trees are not wanted, tree ferns (*olioli*) are an attractive native option, while the various kinds of *mati* are good choices for wildlife.

--- SENTENCE 1172 ---
In places where too much shade is undesirable, *filimoto* is a good choice.

!

--- SENTENCE 1173 ---
Island streams**

Tutuila Island has about 141 small streams that flow year-round along at least a portion of their main channel.

--- SENTENCE 1174 ---
These streams are steep, shallow and short (most are less than a mile long).

--- SENTENCE 1175 ---
Stream flows are generally low but they can flood quickly in response to heavy downpours.

--- SENTENCE 1176 ---
But even with the high rainfall in our mountains (200-300 inches per year), the water drains quickly to sea or percolates into the porous volcanic soil to recharge our groundwater supply of drinking water.

--- SENTENCE 1177 ---
Our streams support surprisingly few species -- there are only about 8-12 freshwater fish species, and not many more freshwater invertebrates.

--- SENTENCE 1178 ---
The principal species are freshwater eels (*tuna*), gobies (*apofu, mano'o*), mountain bass (*sesele, inato*), shrimp (*ulavai*) and snails (*sisivai*).

--- SENTENCE 1179 ---
Additional species may enter the lower ends of streams, but they are not restricted to a freshwater stream environment.

--- SENTENCE 1180 ---
Three non-native fish species were also introduced here, probably in the 1970s: mollies (*fo-vai; Poecilia mexicana*) and mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis*) to control mosquitoes, and tilapia (*Oreochromis mossambicus*) to grow in aquaculture.

--- SENTENCE 1181 ---
The impact of these alien species on the native populations is not known.

--- SENTENCE 1182 ---
Additionally, we can only wonder about the impact of the alien

!

--- SENTENCE 1183 ---
[](_page_51_Picture_4.jpeg)

marine toad (*lage*) that sometimes has thousands of its young tadpoles swimming in local creeks.

--- SENTENCE 1184 ---
The low number of species in our streams is in stark contrast to the many marine species living in our coastal marine waters (890 fish species and countless invertebrates).

--- SENTENCE 1185 ---
Part of the explanation for this difference is simply that our streams are small and offer limited habitat for stream-dwelling organisms.

--- SENTENCE 1186 ---
But another more interesting aspect of this low diversity is: how did any freshwater species get to American Samoa in the first place?

--- SENTENCE 1187 ---
We are a small island surrounded by hundreds of miles of deep ocean.

--- SENTENCE 1188 ---
Freshwater species generally cannot survive in saltwater, so how could these freshwater fish, shrimps and snails cross the ocean barrier to get here?

--- SENTENCE 1189 ---
The trick is that they all have a marine stage in their life cycle.

--- SENTENCE 1190 ---
After they spawn, their newly hatched larvae wash out of the stream into the ocean where they drift about as marine plankton for a few weeks or months.

--- SENTENCE 1191 ---
Some make their way back to a coastline where they seek a stream to live out the rest of their lives.

--- SENTENCE 1192 ---
It might be expected that the few freshwater species that got to our remote islands have evolved over thousands of years into unique (endemic) species found nowhere else in the world, but the opposite is generally true.

--- SENTENCE 1193 ---
The marine stage of these species allows a wide dispersal and continual genetic mixing of populations, so most of the species inhabiting our streams are widely distributed across the South Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 1194 ---
Because streams drain the valleys we live in, they serve as good indicators of how well we are taking care of the land.

--- SENTENCE 1195 ---
Sad to say the message is not good.

--- SENTENCE 1196 ---
Our streams once provided food and drinking water, but now they are treated as a place for people to throw rubbish and piggery wastes.

--- SENTENCE 1197 ---
There have even been deaths in American Samoa due to leptospirosis, a bacteria from piggeries and other animals that pollutes many streams.

--- SENTENCE 1198 ---
And, after a heavy rainfall, some streams turn chocolate brown with the dirt that erodes from the landscape.

--- SENTENCE 1199 ---
Much of this soil erosion is due to poor land-use practices such as the farmer's bare-earth clearings for plantations on steep mountain slopes and the run-off from inadequately designed construction sites.

--- SENTENCE 1200 ---
In the former case, the farmer not only loses the soil needed to grow his crops, but the eroded dirt fouls our streams and ends up in coastal waters where it harms our coral reefs.

--- SENTENCE 1201 ---
It does not have to be this way.

--- SENTENCE 1202 ---
There are better ways to dispose of rubbish and to prevent erosion that can make streams a healthier place for fish as well as for the children who play in the streams.

--- SENTENCE 1203 ---
Life in a Samoan stream: fish, snails & shrimp**

Streams are the plumbing of our islands.

--- SENTENCE 1204 ---
They rely solely on rain, which seeps from steep rainforestclad hills and trickles through the porous volcanic soil carrying with it the products of the land.

--- SENTENCE 1205 ---
Small streams join with others to create a mosaic of channels across the islands.

--- SENTENCE 1206 ---
Waterfalls, cascades, pools and riffles make each stream unique and influence the kinds of animals found there.

--- SENTENCE 1207 ---
Our freshwater stream animals play an important role in maintaining a healthy stream ecosystem.

--- SENTENCE 1208 ---
These species might be overlooked because they are shy and rather blandly colored, which might lead to the assumption that they are not there at all, but that of course is not true.

--- SENTENCE 1209 ---
The best way to observe them is to find a spot on a rock and quietly watch until they emerge from their hiding places.

--- SENTENCE 1210 ---
Keep in mind that from the animal's point of view there is a lot going on.

--- SENTENCE 1211 ---
Animals are constantly interacting with each other as well as with their environment.

--- SENTENCE 1212 ---
The presence of a predator, for example, will have a huge influence on the behavior of its prey.

--- SENTENCE 1213 ---
It is a constant game of cat and mouse, eat or be eaten.

--- SENTENCE 1214 ---
Habitat type, or where an animal likes to live, is also very important.

--- SENTENCE 1215 ---
Certain species prefer pools, others like shallow riffles, and for most species, waterfalls are barriers to their upstream movements although a few hardy species can climb up them.

--- SENTENCE 1216 ---
Predatory fishes, like the freshwater eel (*Angulia* spp.), mountain bass (*Kulia* spp.), and dusky sleeper (*Eleotris fusca*) tend to dominate the lower reaches of a stream and feed on smaller fish and shrimp.

--- SENTENCE 1217 ---
They are only average climbers and cannot make it up the steep slopes of a waterfall.

--- SENTENCE 1218 ---
Instead they rely mainly on their powerful swimming and gliding ability to move through small rapids and over large boulders and rocks.

--- SENTENCE 1219 ---
Look for eels in crevices and holes.

--- SENTENCE 1220 ---
Mountain bass are often seen in

!

--- SENTENCE 1221 ---
[](_page_52_Picture_5.jpeg)

pools not far from the ocean.

--- SENTENCE 1222 ---
Their silvery sides and flag-like tails make them easy to spot.

--- SENTENCE 1223 ---
The dusky sleeper uses stealth and surprise to catch its prey.

--- SENTENCE 1224 ---
It props itself on rocks using its fins and turns darker for camouflage when ambushing prey.

--- SENTENCE 1225 ---
For this reason this species is not an easy one to spot.

--- SENTENCE 1226 ---
Herbivorous fishes, like the small gobies (*Stiphodon* spp.

--- SENTENCE 1227 ---
and *Sicyopterus* spp.), are usually present in large numbers.

--- SENTENCE 1228 ---
They may be seen in quiet riffles and pools grazing on long green algal strands or the

slippery brown algae coating the rocks.

--- SENTENCE 1229 ---
An interesting feature of the goby is that its pelvic fins form a suction cup that enables the goby to cling to rocks and climb waterfalls.

--- SENTENCE 1230 ---
One goby (*Stiphodon hydoreibatus*) is endemic to Samoa and found nowhere else in the world.

!

--- SENTENCE 1231 ---
Stream snails, which all belong to the family Neritidae, are herbivores that graze on the algae growing on stream rocks.

--- SENTENCE 1232 ---
About a dozen species are known locally.

--- SENTENCE 1233 ---
Two distinct types of neritid snails are present.

--- SENTENCE 1234 ---
One is the rounded black/brown shell of the *Neritina* or *Clithon* species.

--- SENTENCE 1235 ---
The shell of these has little or no spire and an interesting device called an "operculum" that acts like a trap door.

--- SENTENCE 1236 ---
This allows the snail to retract into its shell and literally close the door -- a great mechanism to have when you do not want to be eaten.

!

--- SENTENCE 1237 ---
[](_page_53_Picture_3.jpeg)

Neritina variegata Clithon corona Septaria sanguisuga

--- SENTENCE 1238 ---
The other type of shell is the cap-like *Septaria* species.

--- SENTENCE 1239 ---
It has no visible operculum and therefore the snail cannot retract into its shell.

--- SENTENCE 1240 ---
Instead it clamps to rocks with its muscular foot, making it very hard to detach.

--- SENTENCE 1241 ---
This is useful when you live in turbulent rapids or on steep walls.

--- SENTENCE 1242 ---
Besides these two differences, you may also notice variations in shell color, pattern and texture.

--- SENTENCE 1243 ---
They range from light to very dark brown with zigzag, wavy, or straight lines, or none at all.

--- SENTENCE 1244 ---
They may be smooth and shiny or grooved and wrinkled.

--- SENTENCE 1245 ---
Some even have horns.

--- SENTENCE 1246 ---
A small turret shaped snail (*Melanoides* sp.) can also be found near the edge of streams in gravel and mud.

--- SENTENCE 1247 ---
This species is found throughout the Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 1248 ---
Probably the most versatile freshwater species are the shrimp.

--- SENTENCE 1249 ---
American Samoa is home to two families, Atyidae and Palaemonidae, each with 4-5 species.

--- SENTENCE 1250 ---
The palaemonid *Macrobrachium* shrimp are possibly the most well known and can be easily seen in the lower to middle reaches of our streams.

--- SENTENCE 1251 ---
They are easy to identify by their large pincers.

--- SENTENCE 1252 ---
When wading through pools and shallow runs, watch for these shrimp as they make a quick retreat at your approach.

--- SENTENCE 1253 ---
Although shy, they will reappear if you keep still.

--- SENTENCE 1254 ---
These scavengers feed on both plant and animal material.

--- SENTENCE 1255 ---
They may also dislodge *Neritina* and *Corona* snails from rocks, open them up and devour them.

!

--- SENTENCE 1256 ---
Atyidae shrimp can be found throughout many clean streams.

--- SENTENCE 1257 ---
Some are excellent climbers and congregate in the lee of rocks and boulders of fast flowing streams.

--- SENTENCE 1258 ---
Others seek a quiet place in the vegetation and roots of plants near a stream edge where flow is slow.

--- SENTENCE 1259 ---
These shrimp exhibit distinct feeding behaviors.

--- SENTENCE 1260 ---
In fast flowing water they orient themselves in the direction of the current and spread the fine brushes on their front legs to form upturned umbrellas.

--- SENTENCE 1261 ---
They use these to filter food particles carried in the current and then push them into their mouth.

--- SENTENCE 1262 ---
In slower moving water they use their brushes to pluck food items from plants and gravel.

!

--- SENTENCE 1263 ---
Although some insects are common inhabitants in streams around the world, they are not well-represented on our small oceanic islands.

--- SENTENCE 1264 ---
Few of the terrestrial insects we see in American Samoa have an aquatic stage in their life cycle (some exceptions being a few dragonflies, damselflies, midges, mayflies, and beetles).

--- SENTENCE 1266 ---
Snakes In Samoa !

--- SENTENCE 1267 ---
**

Snakes in Samoa?

--- SENTENCE 1268 ---
Two kinds, one right here on Tutuila Island and the other on Ta'u Island.

--- SENTENCE 1269 ---
Fortunately, neither is the dreaded brown tree snake (more about that below).

--- SENTENCE 1270 ---
Also fortunately, neither is poisonous, both mind their own business, and they are no threat to anyone.

--- SENTENCE 1271 ---
On Tutuila, we have an unusually small black snake that looks like an odd earthworm about 6 inches long.

--- SENTENCE 1272 ---
A closer inspection reveals that it has tiny scales.

--- SENTENCE 1273 ---
It's called the potted soil snake or blind burrowing snake (*Ramphotyphlops braminus*), because it has almost no eyes and it burrows through the soil.

--- SENTENCE 1274 ---
This secretive nocturnal snake is occasionally found by someone digging in their garden.

--- SENTENCE 1275 ---
It eats small soil creatures like termites and insect larvae.

!

--- SENTENCE 1276 ---
This harmless snake is widely distributed around the world, but it is not native to our islands.

--- SENTENCE 1277 ---
It was probably introduced here when its eggs were carried in the soil attached to some imported plants or machinery.

--- SENTENCE 1278 ---
In 1993, it was found in the Tafuna area.

--- SENTENCE 1279 ---
Others were found in the Pago Pago area in 2001.

--- SENTENCE 1280 ---
The other snake found infrequently on Ta'u Island is the *gata* or Pacific boa (*Candoia bibroni*).

--- SENTENCE 1281 ---
It also occurs in western Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1282 ---
At one time it inhabited Tutuila Island (its bones were found there) but it went extinct for unknown reasons.

--- SENTENCE 1283 ---
On Ofu, an older resident remembers seeing one there when he was a child.

--- SENTENCE 1284 ---
The Pacific boa is more commonly found on islands closer to Indonesia; American Samoa appears to be the eastward limit of its distribution.

--- SENTENCE 1285 ---
It can grow to a respectable length of 3 to 6 feet and is tan or darkly colored, but its coloration can be variable.

--- SENTENCE 1286 ---
This species is usually found in forests, it is active mainly at night, and it probably eats lizards, rats, and small birds and bird eggs.

--- SENTENCE 1287 ---
The Pacific boa looks a lot like another undesirable snake species, the brown tree snake (*Boiga irregularis*), which we don't have in Samoa and hopefully never will.

--- SENTENCE 1288 ---
You may have heard that the

brown tree snake invaded Guam and caused havoc there.

--- SENTENCE 1289 ---
It wiped out Guam's native bird species and helped decimate their fruit bat populations by eating the young bats left hanging in the trees when the adults flew off to find food.

--- SENTENCE 1290 ---
Parents in Guam were even advised to keep their infants and small children away from this snake because it is somewhat poisonous and occasionally has been caught lunching on a baby's arm.

--- SENTENCE 1291 ---
How's that for a reptilian nightmare?

!

--- SENTENCE 1292 ---
Well, not to fear.

--- SENTENCE 1293 ---
We do not have the brown tree snake in Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1294 ---
So, if you see a large snake on Ta'u island, don't kill it.

## **29.

--- SENTENCE 1328 ---
Disappearing land snails**

Snails belong to the second largest group of animals on earth, the mollusks.

--- SENTENCE 1329 ---
Only the arthropods (insects, crustaceans and their relatives) have more known species.

--- SENTENCE 1330 ---
Snails are found just about everywhere – the ocean, streams, lakes, and on land.

--- SENTENCE 1331 ---
Over 90 native species of land snails occur in the Samoan Archipelago.

--- SENTENCE 1332 ---
Of these, 64 occur in western Samoa and 47 in American Samoa (many are found in both places).

--- SENTENCE 1333 ---
Slugs, which are snail-like mollusks that have no shell, also occur locally, but none is a native species -- all are recent introductions to our islands.

!

--- SENTENCE 1334 ---
Many of our native land snails are found only on our islands – that is, they are endemic to the Samoan Archipelago.

--- SENTENCE 1335 ---
Some of them even occur only on a single island, so they are endemic to that island.

--- SENTENCE 1336 ---
Their shells come in all shapes and sizes – flat, tall, rounded or spiraled.

--- SENTENCE 1337 ---
Some live in trees, where they may eat dying leaves.

--- SENTENCE 1338 ---
Others live on the ground and probably feed on dead leaves.

--- SENTENCE 1339 ---
Together with fungi and other microorganisms that help to decompose the leaf debris, snails contribute to the cycling of nutrients through the ecosystem.

!

--- SENTENCE 1340 ---
We do not know much about the basic biology of these land snails.

--- SENTENCE 1341 ---
Some species have separate males and females, but others are hermaphrodites, where each snail is both male and female.

--- SENTENCE 1342 ---
However, most hermaphroditic snails still reproduce by mating with another individual – each snail can act simultaneously as a male and as a female, or in some species the snails take turns being males and females.

--- SENTENCE 1343 ---
Most snails lay eggs, but some give birth to live young – miniature snails that simply crawl away.

--- SENTENCE 1344 ---
The snails that produce live young tend to grow and reproduce very slowly – some of the tree snails may take over a year to reach full size and may live as long as 5-10 years, producing only 10-20 young per year.

--- SENTENCE 1345 ---
This contrasts with egg-laying species that probably grow much quicker, produce many eggs, but do not live as long.

--- SENTENCE 1346 ---
There are three possible ways that land snails could have crossed the ocean to get to our remote islands.

--- SENTENCE 1347 ---
First, they might have been carried over the ocean from a distant continent – or from another island – on rafts of driftwood or fallen logs.

--- SENTENCE 1348 ---
But salt kills land snails, so this seems unlikely.

--- SENTENCE 1349 ---
Perhaps they were carried here by birds.

--- SENTENCE 1350 ---
Sometimes snails get caught up in the feathers of birds, especially if they are really small snails.

--- SENTENCE 1351 ---
And third, they might have been blown by the wind.

--- SENTENCE 1352 ---
Scientists have found that very small

!

--- SENTENCE 1353 ---
[](_page_57_Picture_8.jpeg)

snails can indeed be blown long distances by strong winds.

--- SENTENCE 1354 ---
A small snail attached to a leaf, caught up in a cyclone, could be blown hundreds, perhaps thousands of miles across the ocean.

--- SENTENCE 1355 ---
Through evolutionary time (millions of years), these seemingly unlikely events had only to happen very occasionally in order for a few land snails to eventually colonize our islands.

--- SENTENCE 1356 ---
Once the land snails arrived and managed to survive, they began to evolve to local conditions.

--- SENTENCE 1357 ---
Some species changed and became so different from their ancestors that scientists now identify them as different species.

--- SENTENCE 1358 ---
Others evolved into more than one species.

--- SENTENCE 1359 ---
This is how the Samoan islands came to have many land snail species found nowhere else on earth – they evolved after they arrived here.

--- SENTENCE 1360 ---
Many of the local snail species have attractively colored shells and have often been used in the making of *ula* or *leis* and for other ornamental purposes.

--- SENTENCE 1361 ---
For instance, the hanging light fixtures in the old lobby of the Rainmaker Hotel in Pago Pago contained 10,000 or more shells of tree snails that used to be abundant in the forests of Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 1362 ---
But now many of our local snail species are disappearing.

--- SENTENCE 1363 ---
At least seven species have become extremely rare and one of them, *Diastole matafaoi* from Tutuila, is now extinct.

--- SENTENCE 1364 ---
Others are undoubtedly in trouble but have simply not been fully evaluated yet.

--- SENTENCE 1365 ---
The two main reasons for their decline are the same reasons that biodiversity is vanishing all over the world.

--- SENTENCE 1366 ---
First is habitat destruction or modification.

--- SENTENCE 1367 ---
As native forest is cut down for timber or cleared for agriculture or urban development, the habitat of those snail species that depend on the forest disappears and so the snails disappear too.

--- SENTENCE 1368 ---
Second is the introduction to the islands of alien species – species that have been brought to the islands by humans, either on purpose or accidentally.

--- SENTENCE 1369 ---
These aliens include pigs that destroy the forest by rooting for food and creating wallows, plants that grow and reproduce more strongly than the native species and replace them in the forest, birds that spread alien plants by carrying their seeds into native forest, rats and ants that prey on snails, and many other species that people have introduced to the islands.

!

--- SENTENCE 1370 ---
These alien species also include snails and slugs that come from elsewhere in the world.

--- SENTENCE 1371 ---
Most people are likely to see only these aliens unless they hike into the most pristine native forest.

--- SENTENCE 1372 ---
Perhaps the most commonly seen are the giant African snail (*sisi aferika*, *Achatina fulica*) and the large black or brown slugs.

--- SENTENCE 1373 ---
But there are many other species that are not so obvious but that can be found easily just by turning over some leaves on the ground almost anywhere in the islands.

--- SENTENCE 1374 ---
The giant African snail is a pest of agriculture and in

gardens but some of these other less well-known species may also be causing problems for the native snail species.

--- SENTENCE 1375 ---
Two of them (*Subulina octona* and *Paropeas achatinaceum*) are now the most abundant snails in the leaf debris of native forest throughout the islands.

--- SENTENCE 1376 ---
It is quite possible that they are impacting native snails by out-competing them for the resources they depend upon.

!

--- SENTENCE 1377 ---
Another alien snail, the predatory snail known as the 'cannibal snail' or 'rosy wolf snail' (*Euglandina rosea*) was introduced in an ill-conceived attempt to control the giant African snail by eating it.

--- SENTENCE 1378 ---
It was introduced even though there is no scientific evidence that it would reduce populations of the African snail.

--- SENTENCE 1379 ---
The problem is that it attacks native snail species that had evolved in the absence of such aggressive predators.

--- SENTENCE 1380 ---
Some of our local species reproduce at a very slow rate and this means that their

populations are highly vulnerable to sustained predation.

--- SENTENCE 1381 ---
*Euglandina rosea* is thought to have been introduced only to Tutuila and Ta'u.

--- SENTENCE 1382 ---
In western Samoa it was introduced to Upolu in the 1990s.

--- SENTENCE 1383 ---
An even more voracious predator of snails, the flatworm *Platydemus manokwari*, was unfortunately introduced to Upolu in 2003 to control the African snail.

--- SENTENCE 1384 ---
This freeliving flatworm appears to be common on Tutuila Island as well.

--- SENTENCE 1385 ---
It is black or dark brown, with a single line down its back, and it can grow to almost 3 inches long and 1/4 inch wide.

--- SENTENCE 1386 ---

--- SENTENCE 1480 ---
Mosquitoes, Filariasis & Dengue Fever**

Mosquitoes may play a role in the grand scheme of things, but I'm not really keen on the idea that they want to suck my blood.

--- SENTENCE 1481 ---
Worse still, they can infect us with two diseases in American Samoa -- more about that shortly.

--- SENTENCE 1482 ---
At least 12 species of mosquitoes (*namu*) occur in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1483 ---
Most are common across the western South Pacific region, but some are endemic to the Samoan islands (*Aedes upolensis, Coquillettidia samoaensis, Ochlerotatus samoanus, Oc. tutuilae*) and a few are newly introduced.

--- SENTENCE 1484 ---
Their life cycle involves several stages, one is the irritating adult mosquito, but their juveniles live in water.

--- SENTENCE 1485 ---
To reproduce, the adult female needs to obtain a blood meal (from you) to develop her eggs.

--- SENTENCE 1486 ---
She then lays her eggs in quiet puddles of water where they hatch and grow.

--- SENTENCE 1487 ---
The larvae breath air, so they generally hang around at the water surface but will wiggle quite actively when disturbed.

--- SENTENCE 1488 ---
After a week or two, the larvae pupate (a resting stage) and then emerge as flying adults.

--- SENTENCE 1489 ---
Females may live for 2-3 weeks and lay several clutches of eggs (each requires a blood meal).

--- SENTENCE 1490 ---
Only the female mosquito searches for a blood meal; the males (the ones with fuzzy antennae) feed on plant nectar.

--- SENTENCE 1491 ---
Also, not all the mosquitoes are after you personally -- some species seek out birds as their preferred blood meal.

!

--- SENTENCE 1492 ---
The female mosquito finds you by following signals given off by your body such as body heat, odors and carbon dioxide.

--- SENTENCE 1493 ---
Light and movement also help her zero-in on you.

--- SENTENCE 1494 ---
She then sinks her long and thin mouth parts into your skin and sucks out a drop of blood, engorging so much that she can barely fly away.

--- SENTENCE 1495 ---
During the biting process, she secretes saliva into the cut to prevent the blood from coagulating.

--- SENTENCE 1496 ---
Your body's reaction to this saliva is what causes the bite to itch and swell.

--- SENTENCE 1497 ---
Mosquitoes can carry two diseases in American Samoa: filariasis and dengue.

--- SENTENCE 1498 ---
Both can cause serious problems.

--- SENTENCE 1499 ---
These diseases are spread by specific mosquito species: dengue (principally *Aedes polynesiensis* and *Ae. aegypti)* and filariasis (mainly *Aedes polynesiensis,* and *Oc. samoanus*).

--- SENTENCE 1500 ---
Filariasis, also known as elephantiasis (*tutupa, mumu*), is caused by parasitic nematode worms called filariae.

--- SENTENCE 1501 ---
When an infected mosquito bites you, these minute worms crawl from the mosquito's mouthparts onto your body and into the cut.

--- SENTENCE 1502 ---
This is not a particularly efficient way to enter your body, so the number of them transmitted from a single mosquito bite is probably limited.

--- SENTENCE 1503 ---
Multiple mosquito bites by infected *A. polynesiensis* may be needed before a person develops the full-blown disease.

--- SENTENCE 1504 ---
The worms lodge in a person's lymphatic system where they can live for 4-6 years and produce millions of minute larvae (microfilariae).

--- SENTENCE 1505 ---
These larvae circulate in your blood and are sucked up by other mosquitoes that bite you and thus can be spread to other people.

--- SENTENCE 1506 ---
In severe cases, filariasis can cause a very large swelling of arms, legs or genitals.

--- SENTENCE 1507 ---
The number of people infected in American Samoa is considerable.

--- SENTENCE 1508 ---
In two surveys, people in several villages here were examined for filariasis and 17% (in 1999) and 13% (in 2002) were found to be currently or recently infected.

--- SENTENCE 1509 ---
But not all these people have swollen limbs.

--- SENTENCE 1510 ---
In general, about half of the people who are infected show no symptoms of the disease, but they may develop them as they get older.

--- SENTENCE 1511 ---
Our local Public Health Department together with the Center for Disease Control has joined a worldwide program to reduce filariasis by giving every person on island a medication that kills the

microfilariae circulating in your blood, thus preventing their further spread by mosquitoes.

--- SENTENCE 1512 ---
The cycle of infection can be broken if the number of people carrying this disease is significantly reduced.

--- SENTENCE 1513 ---
Dengue fever is also transmitted by mosquitoes.

--- SENTENCE 1514 ---
Dengue is a viral disease that is rapidly expanding in tropical and subtropical areas of the world.

--- SENTENCE 1515 ---
It's not native to American Samoa but is periodically brought here by travelers from other infected areas.

--- SENTENCE 1516 ---
It then gets spread around primarily by the daytimebiting *Aedes polynesiensis* and *Ae.

--- SENTENCE 1517 ---
This virus is present in the mosquito's saliva, so it is injected directly into you when an infected mosquito bites you.

--- SENTENCE 1518 ---
Symptoms range from mild fevers to severe and potentially life-threatening illness.

--- SENTENCE 1519 ---
No vaccine is available yet.

!

--- SENTENCE 1520 ---
[](_page_63_Picture_3.jpeg)

A key point to emphasize here is that filariasis and dengue cannot be spread directly from person to person.

--- SENTENCE 1521 ---
An intermediate host, the mosquito, is required to transmit the disease.

--- SENTENCE 1522 ---
Three other mosquito-borne diseases are worth mentioning.

--- SENTENCE 1523 ---
The most serious one, malaria, does not occur in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1524 ---
It is found in the western region of the South Pacific, but the particular species of mosquito that transmits malaria (the *Anopheles* mosquito) does not occur this far east.

--- SENTENCE 1525 ---
A

different type of malaria does occur here, but it infects only birds (avian malaria).

--- SENTENCE 1526 ---
The birds don't seem to be greatly affected by it however, perhaps because they've adapted to it over a long period of time.

--- SENTENCE 1527 ---
Similarly, there is a mosquito-borne parasitic nematode on island that affects dogs (heartworm).

--- SENTENCE 1528 ---
Will tourists to American Samoa pick up these diseases?

--- SENTENCE 1529 ---
First, dengue is usually not present here, only when an infected traveler brings the disease here.

--- SENTENCE 1530 ---
Second, filariasis probably requires multiple bites by specific mosquito species, and probably only a small percent of this species actually carries the disease at any given time.

--- SENTENCE 1531 ---
Scientists predict that we may see an increase in such diseases due to global warming and climate change.

--- SENTENCE 1532 ---
Although exact mechanisms are not known, many pathogens are sensitive to temperature; for example, growth, reproduction, and biting rates of insects all increase with higher temperatures.

--- SENTENCE 1533 ---
Global warming appears to be triggering a number of disease epidemics worldwide, involving a diversity of pathogens (viruses, bacteria, fungi, parasites) and a wide range of hosts (humans, corals, oysters, terrestrial plants, birds).

--- SENTENCE 1534 ---

- **1.

--- SENTENCE 1535 ---
Find their breeding sites**.

--- SENTENCE 1536 ---

--- SENTENCE 1537 ---
Survey your yard and eliminate areas where water can collect, such as discarded tires, buckets, coconut shells, saimin bowls, aluminum cans, cemetery urns, clogged roof gutters, etc.

--- SENTENCE 1538 ---

- **2.

--- SENTENCE 1539 ---
When the mosquitoes are out, wear long-sleeved shirts and long-pants.

--- SENTENCE 1540 ---
Use repellants and insecticides safely.

--- SENTENCE 1541 ---
Repair window screens on your house, and if needed, sleep under mosquito net tents while resting during the day and night.

--- SENTENCE 1542 ---

--- SENTENCE 1543 ---
Remember, you can be infected with the filariasis worm and not have symptoms.

--- SENTENCE 1544 ---

Fungi: the vital decomposers**

What do leaf spots, beer, itchy feet, rotting trees, fresh bread, mold, and pizza have in common?

--- SENTENCE 1549 ---
Different kinds of fungi (the pleural of fungus is fungi) can cause diseases in plants and humans, make alcohol and raise bread.

--- SENTENCE 1550 ---
Certain fungi (mushrooms) are cooked and eaten.

--- SENTENCE 1551 ---
Fungi are sometimes used by Samoans to make fermented drinks, such as *pulouaitu* (ghost hat).

--- SENTENCE 1552 ---
This drink is made from mushrooms growing out of cattle dung that are boiled, strained, and drunk to cause an alcohol-like feeling.

--- SENTENCE 1553 ---
*Alafa* is an interesting forest mushroom (*Mycena* sp.?)

--- SENTENCE 1554 ---
that glows in the dark and has actually been used to illuminate or mark trails at night.

--- SENTENCE 1555 ---
Children also create a strange effect by sticking *alafa* on their faces.

--- SENTENCE 1556 ---
Several other unrelated organisms also produce this

!

--- SENTENCE 1557 ---
[](_page_64_Picture_4.jpeg)

bioluminescence, or biological light, by metabolizing a chemical in their bodies called luciferin.

--- SENTENCE 1558 ---
Different organisms produce different colors of light: green for jellyfish, greenish-yellow for fireflies, red for railroad worms, and greenish-blue for glow worms and mushrooms.

--- SENTENCE 1559 ---
Scientists continue to study this light-producing process, which is extremely efficient (almost 90%).

--- SENTENCE 1560 ---
People used to think fungi were a kind of plant and placed them in the plant kingdom.

--- SENTENCE 1561 ---
But fungi are so different from any other organism, they were given their own kingdom.

--- SENTENCE 1562 ---
Most fungi (except yeasts and a few others) are made up of many cells arranged in long strands called hyphae.

--- SENTENCE 1563 ---
All the hyphae together make up the mycelium, or body of the fungus.

--- SENTENCE 1564 ---
As the hyphae grow through their food, a plant for example, they release enzymes.

--- SENTENCE 1565 ---
These enzymes dissolve the plant cell walls and the fungus mycelium absorbs the nutrients from the plant.

--- SENTENCE 1566 ---
We are usually aware of fungi only when they damage something.

--- SENTENCE 1567 ---
That's because they live inside the things they are eating.

--- SENTENCE 1568 ---
Sometimes we see their white mycelium in rotted wood, but most often we don't see them until they form "fruiting bodies".

--- SENTENCE 1569 ---
These fruiting bodies make thousands and thousands of spores that grow into new fungi wherever they land.

--- SENTENCE 1570 ---
Sometimes these spores are brightly colored, like the blue, green, or white mold on old food, or the black mold in the bathroom shower.

--- SENTENCE 1571 ---
Other times we find mushrooms, another kind of fruiting body, growing on the ground or from trees.

--- SENTENCE 1572 ---
Fruiting bodies are an indication that the insides of the trees are being eaten.

--- SENTENCE 1573 ---
These fungi may be thin and delicate, tough and rubbery, or hard as wood.

--- SENTENCE 1574 ---
The "ear fungus" (*Auricularia*) is brownish-purple, and thin like an ear, while the "tooth fungus" (*Flavodon*) is bright chartreuse with short tooth-like pores on which the spores are formed.

--- SENTENCE 1575 ---
Most wood decay fungi are called conks, or shelf fungi.

--- SENTENCE 1576 ---
One of the most common "wood rotters" in American Samoa is the artist's conk (*Ganoderma australe*).

--- SENTENCE 1577 ---
This fungus grows out from the tree in the shaped of a fan.

--- SENTENCE 1578 ---
It has a light brown top that is sometimes dusty with spores from the conks above

!

--- SENTENCE 1579 ---
[](_page_64_Picture_10.jpeg)

it, and a whitish-gray lower surface.

--- SENTENCE 1580 ---
The gray surface looks smooth but is actually made up of thousands of very small pores, inside of which the spores are formed.

--- SENTENCE 1581 ---
If this pore surface is lightly scratched, a brown line remains, and for centuries people have left messages or drawn pictures on them, hence their name, "artist's conk".

--- SENTENCE 1582 ---
Some fungi threaten our food supply.

--- SENTENCE 1583 ---
The black leaf streak disease of bananas (*lausu lui*, or black Sigatoka) is caused by a fungus (*Mycosphaerella fijiensis*) that you can only see through a microscope.

--- SENTENCE 1584 ---
It damages the banana leaves, making the fruit small and of poor quality.

--- SENTENCE 1585 ---
Farmers have to use special chemicals to control this disease, which cost them time and money.

--- SENTENCE 1586 ---
The taro leaf blight disease (*lega*) that killed most of the Samoan taro in 1993-1994 was caused by a water mold (*Phytophthora colocasiae*).

--- SENTENCE 1587 ---
We used to call water molds 'fungi', but scientific tests show they are more closely related to certain red algae.

--- SENTENCE 1588 ---
They have spores that swim and their mycelium is made of very different ingredients.

--- SENTENCE 1589 ---
Water molds are no longer in the kingdom Fungi, but have been placed in one of the new kingdoms, either Protoctista or Chromista.

--- SENTENCE 1592 ---
The colorful lichens that grow on trees and rocks are actually fungi and algae living together.

--- SENTENCE 1593 ---
This type of relationship is called mutualism, where both organisms benefit from the relationship.

--- SENTENCE 1594 ---
In severe climates, the fungus protects the alga from damaging ultraviolet light and supplies water and dissolved

minerals.

--- SENTENCE 1595 ---
The alga makes food for both organisms through photosynthesis.

--- SENTENCE 1596 ---
In other instances, this symbiosis could be considered parasitism.

--- SENTENCE 1597 ---
In American Samoa, many algae living on leaves or tree bark receive enough moisture and shade to exist without the help of a fungus.

--- SENTENCE 1598 ---
In fact, the fungus may actually damage or kill the algae.

--- SENTENCE 1599 ---
Fungi fill a very important role in nature by breaking down dead organisms into their basic elements so those elements can be used again to build new organisms.

--- SENTENCE 1600 ---
This nutrient recycling is especially important in the tropics where organisms are born and die at a very fast rate.

--- SENTENCE 1601 ---
If it weren't for fungi, bacteria, and other small organisms that eat dead plants, dead trees and bushes would soon bury us.

--- SENTENCE 1603 ---

#### **34.

--- SENTENCE 1606 ---
Pest invaders are here**

The history of life on islands is a story of invasions.

--- SENTENCE 1607 ---
Ever since the high islands of American Samoa rose out of the sea as barren piles of volcanic rock, living things have been making the long and dangerous journey across the Pacific to reach this new land.

--- SENTENCE 1608 ---
Until a few thousand years ago, every plant, insect, and

!

--- SENTENCE 1609 ---
[](_page_66_Picture_3.jpeg)

bird that lived on our islands was the descendant of a lucky adventurer that had crossed hundreds or thousands of miles of open ocean to establish a new colony here.

--- SENTENCE 1610 ---
The first Samoans were also such lucky adventurers, making the perilous voyage here in their journeying canoes.

--- SENTENCE 1611 ---
But their arrival marked the beginning of a new way for plants and animals to reach our islands -- being carried here, either by accident or on purpose, by people.

--- SENTENCE 1612 ---
Ever since then, the environment of American Samoa has changed tremendously.

--- SENTENCE 1613 ---
Some of these changes may have benefited some wildlife species.

--- SENTENCE 1614 ---
For example, the introduction several important food plants such as breadfruit and bananas provide a year-round source of food for some birds and fruit bats, rather than seasonally like some native plants that are eaten by wildlife.

--- SENTENCE 1615 ---
Other changes brought by the Polynesians were harmful to the environment -- for example, the introduction of rats (*isumu*).

--- SENTENCE 1616 ---
And since the arrival of Europeans and Americans, the rate of introduction of new plants and animals to American Samoa has increased tremendously.

--- SENTENCE 1617 ---
The results have often been disastrous.

--- SENTENCE 1618 ---
Some plants brought by the Polynesians "escaped" to spread widely through the natural forest such as *ifi* and *nonu* trees.

--- SENTENCE 1619 ---
More recently, about 250 alien plant species (many of them weeds) have also become established in American Samoa and some of these threaten our native forest.

--- SENTENCE 1620 ---
One that is familiar to everyone is the "mile-a-minute" vine, or *fuesaina* (*Mikania micrantha*).

--- SENTENCE 1621 ---
This was introduced by accident, sometime before 1924.

--- SENTENCE 1622 ---
It is now a major pest in plantations and forests, and has spread tremendously following tropical cyclones in the early 1990s.

--- SENTENCE 1623 ---
The vine needs sunlight for its very fast growth, and so doesn't do well inside the shade of the mature forest.

--- SENTENCE 1624 ---
The cyclones, however, opened up the forests to sunlight by knocking down many trees and breaking off the tops of thousands more.

--- SENTENCE 1625 ---
Vines like these can form layers several feet thick that can choke the seedlings of native trees and slow the recovery of our forests from the damage of the cyclones.

--- SENTENCE 1626 ---
Another well-known pest to agriculture is the giant African snail (*sisi aferika, Achatina fulica*).

--- SENTENCE 1627 ---
This was reportedly introduced to the Pacific when the governor of Tahiti imported them to satisfy the hunger of his mistress for escargot, or edible snails.

--- SENTENCE 1628 ---
Bad, bad idea -- not only were the snails not edible, but they quickly spread throughout Polynesia, and became a major pest of taro and other crops.

--- SENTENCE 1629 ---
Unfortunately, the story does not end there.

--- SENTENCE 1630 ---
In the hopes to control the giant snails, a predatory snail (*Euglandina rosea*) native to Florida was deliberately introduced here in 1980.

--- SENTENCE 1631 ---
The idea was that this new snail -- the one with the long pinkish shell -- would kill off all the giant snails.

--- SENTENCE 1632 ---
Instead, it has driven the native land snails of the Pacific islands to the edge of extinction.

--- SENTENCE 1633 ---
Most of the native snails of Tahiti are now extinct, and the Samoan snails, which used to

be collected by the thousands to make *ula*, are almost gone.

--- SENTENCE 1634 ---
Meanwhile, the giant snail continues to thrive despite the new predator.

--- SENTENCE 1635 ---
Another example is of an invasion that is still under way -- by introduced birds.

--- SENTENCE 1636 ---
The first bird introduced to American Samoa was, of course, the chicken (*moa*).

--- SENTENCE 1637 ---
Although these occasionally nest in the forests, they are not truly established as wild birds here, and are no threat to our native birdlife.

--- SENTENCE 1638 ---
However, we do have three introduced bird species on Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 1639 ---
One of these is the *manu palagi*, or Red-vented Bulbul, the black bird with the crest that is common in nearly every village.

--- SENTENCE 1640 ---
This species became established here in the 1950's.

--- SENTENCE 1641 ---
The other two species both arrived in the 1980's and there seems to be no name for them in Samoan.

--- SENTENCE 1642 ---
These are the Common Myna and the Jungle Myna.

--- SENTENCE 1643 ---
They look almost the same -- both are blackish brown, with big white patches in the wings and tail when they fly.

--- SENTENCE 1644 ---
They are now abundant from Pago Pago to Leone and are spreading to the eastern and western ends of the island.

--- SENTENCE 1645 ---
None of these three introduced birds has made it to Manu'a, which is still home to only our native Samoan birds.

!

--- SENTENCE 1646 ---
What's wrong with having new kinds of birds to live in our villages and gardens?

--- SENTENCE 1647 ---
In some parts of the world, including Hawaii, both bulbuls and mynas are serious pests on fruit crops such as guava.

--- SENTENCE 1648 ---
Second, they may spread the seeds of pest plants, like mile-a-minute vine and "Koster's curse" weed (*Clidemia hirta*), that native birds do not eat as readily.

--- SENTENCE 1649 ---
Third, they may drive out native birds, like the *iao* or Wattled Honeyeater.

--- SENTENCE 1650 ---
Although we don't know for sure that this is happening, there seem to be more *iao* around villages in Manu'a than on Tutuila where the introduced birds are common.

--- SENTENCE 1651 ---
Finally, the introduced birds may spread diseases that will attack our native birds.

--- SENTENCE 1652 ---
This has happened in Hawaii.

--- SENTENCE 1653 ---
In fact, on the main islands of Hawaii, the native birds have been almost completely exterminated.

--- SENTENCE 1654 ---
Every bird you seen in the lowlands of Hawaii is an introduced species; the native birds hang on only in isolated mountain forests.

--- SENTENCE 1655 ---
We don't want our Samoan

The lesson to learn from these examples is that we must be very, very careful when thinking about introducing a new plant or animal to Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1656 ---
Even species that seem beneficial, like the predatory snail, can have bad

--- SENTENCE 1657 ---
And once a new species is established, it is almost impossible to get rid of.

--- SENTENCE 1658 ---
Wisely, the Territory has established strict laws against bringing in exotic animals and any plant that may become a noxious weed.

--- SENTENCE 1659 ---
Flowering plants, like Honolulu rose (*Clerodendrum chinese*), may look pretty in a garden but turn into major pests when they run wild.

--- SENTENCE 1660 ---
American Samoa's forests and wildlife are unique to all the world, having developed here over hundreds of thousands of years in isolation.

--- SENTENCE 1661 ---
We must take care to preserve and protect this special heritage by staying alert to keep unwelcome invaders from our shores.

--- SENTENCE 1663 ---
Common pests and diseases of local agricultural plants**

Gazing up at the beautiful mountains on our islands, we see hillsides of lush green tropical foliage -- a picture of botanical health.

--- SENTENCE 1664 ---
That's true for the most part, but if we look closer, we notice that some plants can get sick and die, or they can be injured when insects, animals, strong winds or landslides damage their leaves, stems or roots.

--- SENTENCE 1665 ---
Sometimes insect pests or diseases spread fast, injuring or killing many plants over a large area.

--- SENTENCE 1666 ---
Then we take notice, particularly if we were growing those plants to eat.

--- SENTENCE 1667 ---
Here are five common plant pests and diseases that can cause serious damage to local agricultural plants.

--- SENTENCE 1668 ---
Taro leaf blight disease (*lega, laumu*).

--- SENTENCE 1669 ---
Many people became acutely aware of this plant disease in 1993-94 when it destroyed most of the Samoan taro crop.

--- SENTENCE 1670 ---
The disease is caused by a fungus (*Phythophthora colocasiae*) that has been in Fiji for many years, but we don't know how it came to American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1671 ---
Perhaps it arrived here on taro tops (*tiapula*) from another island, or its fungal spores blew in on the winds of Cyclone Val in 1991.

--- SENTENCE 1672 ---
Taro leaf blight is most severe when the weather is cool and wet.

--- SENTENCE 1673 ---
Rain splashes spores of the fungus into the air, which are then blown by wind from plant to plant.

--- SENTENCE 1674 ---
The expanding brown spots made by this fungus cause leaves to die faster than normal, so the plant must use its energy to make more leaves instead of storing the energy in the taro corm (*i'o ole talo*) that we want to eat.

--- SENTENCE 1675 ---
Farmers are now growing taro varieties from Micronesia that are more resistant to the fungus.

--- SENTENCE 1676 ---
The Palau taro, for example, still gets the disease, but less severely, so the leaves live longer and corms are of normal size.

--- SENTENCE 1832 ---
The Pacific Pigeon (***lupe***), Samoa's royal bird**

The *lupe*, or Pacific Pigeon, is the king of Samoa's birds and in many ways it is the most culturally important bird in our islands.

--- SENTENCE 1833 ---
It is our largest forest bird and is the only one able to feed on, and spread, the large seeds of some of our most important rainforest trees.

--- SENTENCE 1834 ---
Its cultural significance is revealed by the many Samoan proverbs that relate to the *lupe* and the art of *lupe*-hunting.

--- SENTENCE 1835 ---
The importance of *lupe* is also shown by the amazing *tia seulupe* (star-mounds) which are massive stone platforms built by the ancient Samoans, which served at least partly as pigeon-trapping sites.

--- SENTENCE 1836 ---
The *lupe* is a member of a group of birds called the imperial pigeons because of their large size and dignified appearance.

--- SENTENCE 1837 ---
Most imperial pigeons live in the islands of the Pacific, although a few reach the mainland of Asia and one occurs in Australia.

--- SENTENCE 1838 ---
These birds are closely related to the much smaller fruit-doves, like the *manutagi* (Purple-capped Fruit Dove) but are only distantly related to the common pigeon that waddles around the cities of the world.

--- SENTENCE 1839 ---
The *lupe* is found from the islands north of New Guinea east through Fiji, Tonga, Samoa, Niue, and the Cook Islands.

--- SENTENCE 1840 ---
Interestingly, throughout most of this wide range, it is usually found on small islands

!

--- SENTENCE 1841 ---
[](_page_72_Picture_4.jpeg)

and atolls, not on large 'high islands'.

--- SENTENCE 1842 ---
This is most striking in Fiji, where *lupe* are found on the small islands of the Lau group, but not on the large islands of Vanua Levu, Viti Levu or Taveuni.

--- SENTENCE 1843 ---
A closely related imperial pigeon replaces the *lupe* on those high islands.

--- SENTENCE 1844 ---
*Lupe* are a common sight in forested areas of American Samoa and can be seen flying high over villages or bays.

--- SENTENCE 1845 ---
They even visit *moso'oi* and *poumuli* trees near houses, if those houses are not too far from forests.

--- SENTENCE 1846 ---
Even when not seen, they announce their presence with easy to hear calls: a low, rising and falling moo like that of a cow, or a loud, rolling prrrrrhhh.

--- SENTENCE 1847 ---
One of the benefits of American Samoa's ban on hunting *lupe* (and *pe'a*) after the cyclones in the early 1990s is that they become less scared of people, giving us better chances to see and appreciate these spectacular birds.

--- SENTENCE 1848 ---
Their nests are hard to find.

--- SENTENCE 1849 ---
During all our time in the forests, we have seen only a few.

--- SENTENCE 1850 ---
We have also seen evidence of nest-building (for example, *lupe* flying with sticks in their bills) on several other occasions.

--- SENTENCE 1851 ---
All these observations were during the months of January through September.

--- SENTENCE 1852 ---
*Lupe* nests are open platforms of twigs, with no lining.

--- SENTENCE 1853 ---
They are usually placed in dense clumps of leaves high up in trees, making them very hard to see.

--- SENTENCE 1854 ---
These birds lay only a single white egg.

--- SENTENCE 1855 ---
Both parents help incubate the egg and feed the chick once it hatches.

--- SENTENCE 1856 ---
All members of the pigeon family have an amazing ability: they feed their young a sort of "milk".

--- SENTENCE 1857 ---
This is a nutritious liquid that is a combination of digested food and other substances secreted by special glands in the bird's digestive system.

--- SENTENCE 1858 ---
Both male and female pigeons make this milk, so both parents take an equal part in feeding the young.

--- SENTENCE 1859 ---
Information from related species of imperial pigeons suggests that *lupe* eggs take about 27 days to hatch, and that the young spend about 28 days in the nest after hatching.

--- SENTENCE 1860 ---
It seems like *lupe* could nest more than once a year, but most young birds are seen from July to August, so most nesting probably occurs at just one time each year.

--- SENTENCE 1861 ---
*Lupe* eat many different rainforest foods, and not just fruits from favored trees like *atone, ma'ali, malili, mamala, mamalava, moso'oi* or *aoa*.

--- SENTENCE 1862 ---
They also eat fruits from vines such as *mamalupe* (pigeon's

mouthful) and low shrubs like *toitoi*.

--- SENTENCE 1863 ---
*Lupe* eat leaves from many different trees, including *au'auli, a'amati'e* and *ifi*, although in almost all cases they like the young leaves best.

--- SENTENCE 1864 ---
This makes sense, since young leaves are probably the most tender and easy to digest, and may have fewer poisonous chemicals than old leaves.

--- SENTENCE 1865 ---
*Lupe* also like flowers on occasion, both from trees like *ala'a* or *ma'ali* and from shrubs such as *ti*.

--- SENTENCE 1866 ---
Obviously, the *lupe* is not a picky eater.

--- SENTENCE 1867 ---
This may explain why it is such a widespread and successful bird, since it will eat food from both coastal and mountain areas.

--- SENTENCE 1868 ---
In fact, after Cyclone Val in 1991, many *lupe* on the coast near Vaitogi survived by eating the fruits of *toitoi*.

--- SENTENCE 1869 ---
Thus, in times of limited food, it can find food where other birds might not.

--- SENTENCE 1870 ---
At other times, *lupe* may eat unusual foods (like leaves and flowers) to get certain nutrients, just the way people sometimes take vitamin pills to supplement their regular diets.

--- SENTENCE 1871 ---
*Lupe* are also able to eat some fruits that smaller birds cannot, and so may be important for spreading these trees to new areas or even among different islands.

--- SENTENCE 1872 ---
Since the *lupe* is such an adaptable bird, and has been able to recover both from human activities like hunting and from natural disturbances like the tropical cyclones in the early 1990s, it may seem surprising that there are many fewer now than in earlier times.

--- SENTENCE 1873 ---
The most dramatic evidence for this are the many *tia seulupe*, or star mounds, that were built by ancient Samoans.

--- SENTENCE 1874 ---
A survey discovered the remains of over 60 *tia* in the eastern part of Tutuila alone.

--- SENTENCE 1875 ---
Each *tia* is a massive stone platform with one or more arms extending from it.

--- SENTENCE 1876 ---
Pigeon-catching huts were built on top of

!

--- SENTENCE 1877 ---
[](_page_73_Picture_4.jpeg)

the platform, and village chiefs competed to catch the most birds, using a tame *lupe* as a decoy, and a long-handled net to sweep up the flocks of pigeons that were attracted.

--- SENTENCE 1878 ---
Early missionary accounts tell how whole villages would spend weeks camping out in the forest around the *tia*, and that pigeoncatching season was a time of feasting and partying.

--- SENTENCE 1879 ---
*Lupe* must have been very abundant to support this elaborate cultural activity.

--- SENTENCE 1880 ---
While the *tia* certainly had ritual and religious importance in addition to their use as pigeon-catching sites, the latest studies suggest that pigeon-catching was central to their role in ancient Samoan society.

--- SENTENCE 1881 ---
A well-preserved star mound, with explanatory signs, can be visited in Ottoville, next to the Fatuoaiga Catholic Church compound.

--- SENTENCE 1882 ---
Now, though, the rainforest that once covered the Tafuna Plain have been cut down.

--- SENTENCE 1883 ---
This type of lowland forest, with its abundant *mamalava, tava, maota, mamala*, and *aoa* trees, was almost certainly the best habitat for *lupe* in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1884 ---
Today, only scattered *aoa* remain, and *lupe* are seldom seen in Tafuna.

--- SENTENCE 1885 ---
Even though *aoa* is a valuable food for *lupe*, we can see that it was the whole forest, and not just one kind of tree, that made the Tafuna rainforest so valuable to *lupe*.

--- SENTENCE 1886 ---
However, this large area of habitat is gone, and it seems we will never see as many *lupe* as did the first Samoans.

--- SENTENCE 1887 ---
Perhaps more than any other animal, the *lupe* is of irreplaceable importance to Samoa, both because of its natural role in spreading rainforest seeds, and its cultural role in the stories, proverbs, and heritage of the Samoan people.

--- SENTENCE 1888 ---

--- SENTENCE 1889 ---
The rare Many-colored Fruit Dove (***manuma***)**

One of the rarest birds that nest on Tutuila is also one of the most beautiful: the *manuma*, or Manycolored Fruit-Dove.

--- SENTENCE 1890 ---
The *manuma* here is considered a different subspecies than those from Fiji and Tonga because they have a slightly different color pattern.

--- SENTENCE 1891 ---
The male *manuma* is creamy white below and pale yellow above, with a dark crimson band across the back, a crimson patch on the forehead, and a purplish-red blotch on the breast.

--- SENTENCE 1892 ---
The female is very different and looks like the much more common *manutagi*, or Purple-capped Fruit-Dove.

--- SENTENCE 1893 ---
She is mostly green above and gray below, with a crimson forehead patch.

--- SENTENCE 1894 ---
Unlike the *manutagi*, the female *manuma* doesn't have a yellow band on its tail.

--- SENTENCE 1895 ---
The *manuma* was not rare on Tutuila in the 19th and early 20th centuries, according to the journals of the early scientific expeditions.

--- SENTENCE 1896 ---
It was usually found in flocks; in 1923 a collector killed 10 birds with a single shot into a feeding tree on Ta'u.

--- SENTENCE 1897 ---
However, when the first modern studies of American Samoa's birds were done in mid-1970, only a small number of *manuma* could be found.

--- SENTENCE 1898 ---
Follow-up in the mid-1980's confirmed the *manuma*'s rarity.

--- SENTENCE 1899 ---
They estimated that the population size on Tutuila was only about 80 birds.

!

--- SENTENCE 1900 ---
[](_page_74_Picture_5.jpeg)

After the cyclones in the early 1990s, even fewer *manuma* were seen, and perhaps less than 50 remained on all of Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 1901 ---
Today, however, *manuma* are regularly seen at some locations on Tutuila, although not in very large numbers.

--- SENTENCE 1902 ---
Biologists would call their current distribution rare and local, but they are occasionally sighted at many places around American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1903 ---
Interestingly, *manuma* are much more common in Fiji, Samoa and Tonga, sometimes being found well away from mature forests.

--- SENTENCE 1904 ---
We know very little about the biology of *manuma* in American Samoa, or why it is less common here than elsewhere.

--- SENTENCE 1905 ---
These are social birds, often being seen in small flocks,

but they are also very protective of feeding areas, driving other *manuma* away from their spot in the canopy of a fruiting tree.

--- SENTENCE 1906 ---
One odd thing is that you rarely see the same numbers of each sex -- almost always there are several more males than females in any flocks.

--- SENTENCE 1907 ---
We do know that they seem to have a very strong preference for the fruits of banyan (*aoa*) trees, and in American Samoa it is almost always seen near or in these trees.

--- SENTENCE 1908 ---
They are also known (from Fiji and Tonga) to eat *moso'oi*, *o'a* and *magele* fruits, all of which occur in America Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1909 ---
Perhaps there are still so few *manuma* here that they are seldom forced to eat anything but their favorite food, but would eat other fruits if necessary.

--- SENTENCE 1910 ---
It is dangerous for animals to specialize too much on any one food, especially on remote islands like American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1911 ---
This can be shown with an example from business.

--- SENTENCE 1912 ---
While a store that sells only antique cuckoo-clocks might do fine in New York, Los Angeles, or another big market, it would certainly fail very quickly in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1913 ---
There isn't enough business here for such a specialized service.

--- SENTENCE 1914 ---
Similarly, an island animal that concentrates on only one kind of food may find itself in trouble when that food supply fails.

--- SENTENCE 1915 ---
An animal that lives on a continent can move in search of the food it needs, but island animals have nowhere else to go.

--- SENTENCE 1916 ---
On larger islands like Upolu, Savai'i or Viti Levu, *manuma* may be able to travel long distances to get the food they like the best.

--- SENTENCE 1917 ---
This may be difficult on small islands like those of American Samoa, and means that animals can only specialize on very reliable food trees such as banyan trees (*aoa)*.

--- SENTENCE 1918 ---
These giant banyan trees, which are so important to *manuma*, face many problems of their own.

--- SENTENCE 1919 ---
The Tafuna Plain used to have many huge banyans, but most of these have been cut down to make way for the exploding human populations in that area.

--- SENTENCE 1920 ---
Moreover, banyans, with their large size and spreading crowns, are very vulnerable to storm damage.

--- SENTENCE 1921 ---
Many were killed or severely damaged by Cyclones Ofa and Val in the early 1990s.

--- SENTENCE 1922 ---
Even those that survived the cyclones had their leaves and fruit stripped off, leading to a prolonged period of famine for *manuma*.

--- SENTENCE 1923 ---
There are two things that must happen if the *manuma* is to survive and remain a special part of Samoa's wildlife.

--- SENTENCE 1924 ---
First, the birds must be protected from hunting.

--- SENTENCE 1925 ---
Although the *manuma* is too rare to be sought by hunters, a few may have been killed every year by hunters out for *lupe* and *manutagi*.

--- SENTENCE 1926 ---
When a population is as small as the *manuma's*, even a few preventable deaths per year are a significant problem.

!

--- SENTENCE 1927 ---
The second thing that must be done if the *manuma* is to survive is to protect banyan trees on which they depend.

--- SENTENCE 1928 ---
Without enough banyans to assure a year-round supply of its favorite food, the *manuma* may well become extinct on Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 1929 ---
People need to protect these magnificent trees, which are important food sources for many other Samoan wildlife species, including the fruit bats (flying foxes).

--- SENTENCE 1930 ---
Pepper Trail, Joshua Seamon DMWR

--- SENTENCE 1931 ---
The honey-birds**

The most familiar birds of Tutuila's villages and gardens are the *iao* (Wattled Honeyeater) and the segasegamau'u (Cardinal Honeyeater).

--- SENTENCE 1932 ---
Although they look different, these birds are members of the same *aiga*, the family of birds called the honeyeaters.

--- SENTENCE 1933 ---
Honeyeaters are found throughout the Pacific islands, New Guinea, and Australia.

--- SENTENCE 1934 ---
As their name implies, most of them include the sweet nectar of flowers in their diet (though none of them eat real honey).

--- SENTENCE 1935 ---
The *iao* or Wattled Honeyeater (*Foulehaio carunculata*) is the commonest forest bird in Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1936 ---
It is also found in Tonga and Fiji.

--- SENTENCE 1937 ---
Although not a particularly beautiful bird, with its greenishbrown feathers and yellow flaps of bare skin on the face, its boundless energy and continuous song enliven our islands.

--- SENTENCE 1938 ---
Its bold and fearless nature keeps it busy chasing other birds, and its loud alarm calls warn other animals of the *lulu* (Barn Owl) or other danger.

--- SENTENCE 1939 ---
Samoan legend tells that when the *iao* calls at night, it means that ghosts (*aitu*) are near.

--- SENTENCE 1940 ---
The *iao* feeds at almost all the flowers of the forest, from small *atone* (nutmeg) flowers to the large crimson blooms of *gatae* (coral tree).

--- SENTENCE 1941 ---
Christmastime is the best time of year for *iao*,

because the *asi* trees are in full bloom.

--- SENTENCE 1942 ---
These white brushy flowers cover the tree crowns and are rich with nectar.

--- SENTENCE 1943 ---
At that time, the *iao* get so full of this nectar that they're like kids full of candy and cake at a party -- they rush every which way through the forest, chasing each other and yelling out songs at the top of their lungs.

--- SENTENCE 1944 ---
At other times when flowers aren't quite so abundant, they eat a lot of insects as well, and also include some soft fruits and berries in their diet.

--- SENTENCE 1945 ---
Even though the *iao* is our commonest bird, we really don't know very much about its habits.

--- SENTENCE 1946 ---
Their nests are beautifully woven cups of grass, typically well-hidden in the dense foliage of a tree.

--- SENTENCE 1947 ---
Usually only one or two eggs are laid.

--- SENTENCE 1948 ---
Most nesting appears to take place between September and December.

--- SENTENCE 1949 ---
The *segasegamau'u* or Cardinal Honeyeater (*Myzomela cardinalis*) is the prettiest bird of Samoa's gardens.

--- SENTENCE 1950 ---
Unlike most Samoan birds, the male and female Cardinal Honeyeater look very different: the male is bright red, with black wings and tail, while the female is a dull gray, with a little bit of red on the rump.

--- SENTENCE 1951 ---
These tiny birds (the smallest in American Samoa) always seem to be active, flitting among the *aute* (ornamental hibiscus), *teuila* (ginger), and *nonu* (Indian mulberry) in our gardens, or sampling nectar at flowers high in the forest treetops.

--- SENTENCE 1952 ---
Like the *iao*, the *segasegamau'u* is very vocal, though not as loud as its larger cousin.

--- SENTENCE 1953 ---
Its sweet warbling songs are familiar sounds in our villages and plantations.

!

--- SENTENCE 1954 ---
The *segasegamau'u* is even more of a honey-bird than the *iao*.

--- SENTENCE 1955 ---
Though it will occasionally eat small insects, it seems very dependent on flower nectar at all times of year.

--- SENTENCE 1956 ---
You can see the difference in the beaks of the two birds: the Cardinal Honeyeater has a very delicate, sharp beak that is perfect for slipping into flowers but not so good for grabbing big bugs.

--- SENTENCE 1957 ---
The beak of the Wattled Honeyeater is sturdier, more all-purpose: good both for flowers and insects.

--- SENTENCE 1958 ---
Perhaps because of its love of flowers, the *segasegamau'u* seems happy to live close to people, in gardens and plantations.

--- SENTENCE 1959 ---
Although much less common than the *iao* in the forest, it is the honeyeater that you're likely to see around villages.

--- SENTENCE 1960 ---
Around villages on Tutuila, that is; surprisingly, the *segasegamau'u* doesn't occur on any of the islands of Manu'a.

--- SENTENCE 1961 ---
Outside of American Samoa, the species is found in western Samoa, Vanuatu, and the Solomon Islands, and very close relatives occur in Micronesia and Fiji.

--- SENTENCE 1962 ---
The nest of the Cardinal Honeyeater is a beautiful, delicate cup of fine grass fibers, often decorated with moss.

--- SENTENCE 1963 ---
It may be placed high in a tree or almost on the ground in thick foliage.

--- SENTENCE 1964 ---
Four to five tiny eggs are laid.

--- SENTENCE 1965 ---
The *segasegmau'u* seems to nest in all months of the year.

--- SENTENCE 1966 ---
There used to be a third kind of honeyeater on Tutuila, the very large, blackish *ma'oma'o* or *mao*

--- SENTENCE 1967 ---
This is a real mystery bird.

--- SENTENCE 1968 ---
Larger than a *fuia* (Samoan Starling), with loud

> wailing calls, the *ma'oma'o* is remembered by some of the elders of Tutuila, and was collected by scientists here in the 1920s.

--- SENTENCE 1969 ---
However, except for a couple of possible sightings in the 1960s and 1970s, it has never been seen since.

--- SENTENCE 1970 ---
The *ma'oma'o* is now found only in the remote mountains of 'Upolu and Savai'i, where it is rare and little known.

--- SENTENCE 1971 ---
We will probably never know if this bird was once an important part of our forests, or if it occurred here only as a rare visitor from western Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1972 ---
According to legends, hearing the wails and screams of the *ma'oma'o* around a village meant that misfortune or a death was about to happen.

--- SENTENCE 1973 ---
Sadly, this prophecy seems to have come true for the *ma'oma'o* itself, which is now in danger of the greatest misfortune -- extinction.

!

--- SENTENCE 1974 ---
Samoa's starlings**

Starlings are a group of birds with a real image problem.

--- SENTENCE 1975 ---
Over much of the world, "starling" means just one thing -- the Common, or European Starling.

--- SENTENCE 1976 ---
This plump, short-tailed, oily black bird was originally found in Europe, but it has spread across the cool, temperate countries of the world, from the US to China, and from Australia to Argentina.

--- SENTENCE 1977 ---
Everywhere it goes, this bird becomes a pest in both cities and the countryside, often driving out native birds and destroying fruit crops.

--- SENTENCE 1978 ---
However, starlings are much more than this one obnoxious species.

--- SENTENCE 1979 ---
Most starlings are found in the tropics, and they are a varied, interesting and often beautiful family of birds.

--- SENTENCE 1980 ---
In the Pacific, starlings are a characteristic and important part of our native birdlife.

--- SENTENCE 1981 ---
In fact, the most unique of all our birds in American Samoa is the *fuia* or Samoan Starling.

--- SENTENCE 1982 ---
Why is the *fuia* so special?

--- SENTENCE 1983 ---
Well, not because of its appearance.

--- SENTENCE 1984 ---
With its dull brownish-black color, the *fuia* could hardly be called beautiful.

--- SENTENCE 1985 ---
No, the *fuia* is special because it is the only American Samoan bird that is "endemic" to Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1986 ---
This is a word used by scientists to describe something found in only one place.

--- SENTENCE 1987 ---
The *fuia* is found only in the islands of American and western Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1988 ---
All our other birds are found in at least one other group of islands.

--- SENTENCE 1989 ---
Therefore, if the *fuia* became extinct in the Samoas, there wouldn't be a single one left in the world.

--- SENTENCE 1990 ---
Fortunately, there is little danger that the *fuia* will become extinct.

--- SENTENCE 1991 ---
It is a survivor, a real Samoan success story.

--- SENTENCE 1992 ---
It is the most adaptable bird we have, equally at home in Pago Pago, in small

!

--- SENTENCE 1993 ---
As in most Samoan birds, male and female *fuia* look almost the same.

--- SENTENCE 1994 ---
The species seems to nest in all months of the year.

--- SENTENCE 1995 ---
*Fuia* nests are usually placed in hollows in trees: snapped-off coconut trunks are favorite nest sites.

--- SENTENCE 1996 ---
They will also nest among the dense fronds in the top of a coconut, and even use man-made nest sites, like cracked telephone poles.

--- SENTENCE 1997 ---
*Fuia* eggs are pale blue.

--- SENTENCE 1998 ---
We don't really know very much about the social life of the *fuia*: for example, do they mate for life?

--- SENTENCE 1999 ---
Do they defend territories from other *fuia*?

--- SENTENCE 2000 ---
Do they stay in one small area, or do they move all around the island?

--- SENTENCE 2001 ---
It would be interesting to know more about this most Samoan of our native birds.

--- SENTENCE 2002 ---
The *fuia* is not our only native starling; it has a small and shy cousin, the *miti vao*, or Polynesian Starling.

--- SENTENCE 2003 ---
Although much less common than the *fuia* in Samoa,

--- SENTENCE 2004 ---
the *miti vao* has a wider range, being found in Fiji, Tonga, and Niue as well as American and western Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 2005 ---
Unlike the *fuia*, the *miti vao* is almost entirely a bird of the forest, rarely seen in plantations, much less villages.

--- SENTENCE 2006 ---
The reasons for this aren't clear.

--- SENTENCE 2007 ---
It seems to have a broad diet, though not quite as accepting as the *fuia*.

--- SENTENCE 2008 ---
It eats both insects and fruit, and is often seen feeding on hard-seeded fruit like *mamala* and *taputoi*, though we don't know if this is because the *miti vao* prefers such food or because the more aggressive *fuia* drives it away from softer, more nutritious fruit.

--- SENTENCE 2009 ---
Across most of Tutuila you may have some difficulty finding the *miti vao*.

--- SENTENCE 2010 ---
There are a few places where they seem to be more common: Maloata on the west end of the island, between Afono and Vatia on the north side, and along the Mt. Alava Road.

--- SENTENCE 2011 ---
Look for a small, short-tailed bird with a grayish back, a pale breast with darker streaking, and white eyes.

--- SENTENCE 2012 ---
Its quiet but musical whistles and trills are very different from the harsh screeches and piercing whistles of the *fuia*.

--- SENTENCE 2013 ---
*Miti vao* nests are placed in hollows and holes, as are *fuia* nests.

--- SENTENCE 2014 ---
They usually lay two pale blue eggs with brown specklings.

--- SENTENCE 2015 ---
So few nests have been found here that we really don't know what time of the year this species prefers to breed, or if they may nest at any time.

--- SENTENCE 2016 ---
The *miti vao* is a species that apparently declined after tropical cyclones in 1990-91.

--- SENTENCE 2017 ---
We can only hope that populations will increase over time, provided that enough good forest remains to give this species the wild habitat it needs.

!

--- SENTENCE 2018 ---
One interesting thing about the *miti vao* is that there is a very different form of this bird in Manu'a.

--- SENTENCE 2019 ---
There, the *miti vao* are very dark on both upperparts and underparts, with heavy dark streaking on the breast.

--- SENTENCE 2020 ---
To become so different, the Manu'a birds must have been separated from those on Tutuila for a long time, probably thousands of years.

--- SENTENCE 2021 ---
Unfortunately, the native *fuia* and *miti vao* aren't Samoa's only starlings.

--- SENTENCE 2022 ---
Since the 1980's, two other members of the starling family have invaded Tutuila, and are now among our commonest birds.

--- SENTENCE 2023 ---
These are the black and white mynas that are common from Pago Pago Harbor to Leone.

--- SENTENCE 2024 ---
Two species of mynas are established here, both of which were originally native to India.

--- SENTENCE 2025 ---
The Common Myna is brownish black, with a yellow bill and a yellow patch of bare skin around the eye.

--- SENTENCE 2026 ---
The Jungle Myna is similar, but is darker and slimmer, with an orange beak and no yellow skin around the eye.

--- SENTENCE 2027 ---
Both species have large white patches in the wings and tail.

--- SENTENCE 2028 ---
The Common Myna is a major pest in many parts of the Pacific, including New Zealand, Hawaii, Fiji, Cook Islands, and French Polynesia.

--- SENTENCE 2029 ---
The Jungle Myna has become established only in Fiji and in the Samoan islands.

--- SENTENCE 2030 ---
Both species eat almost anything and are very happy in cities and villages, where they eat garbage and nest under roofs even in occupied buildings.

--- SENTENCE 2031 ---
These unwelcome invaders can damage guavas and other fruit crops, can spread disease, and may compete with our native birds in villages.

--- SENTENCE 2032 ---
So far, the mynas have not yet spread to the Manu'a Islands.

--- SENTENCE 2033 ---
It is important that we prevent their spread, and reserve American Samoa, as much as possible, for our native birds, including our native starlings, the *fuia* and the *miti vao*.

--- SENTENCE 2035 ---
Swiftlets & sheath-tailed bats (***o le pe'ape'a***): a mosquito's nightmare**

High above the villages, valleys, and mountains of Samoa flies a deadly predator, as terrifying as a tiger shark -- if you're a mosquito (*namu*), that is.

--- SENTENCE 2036 ---
This is the *pe'ape'a*, the only bird in Samoa that lives entirely on a diet of insects.

--- SENTENCE 2037 ---
But wait a minute -- *pe'a* means bat: isn't the *pe'ape'a* a kind of small bat?

--- SENTENCE 2038 ---
Well, yes and no.

--- SENTENCE 2039 ---
Actually two completely different creatures share the name *pe'ape'a* in Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 2040 ---
One is a bird, the common White-rumped Swiftlet that is seen flying by day all over our islands.

--- SENTENCE 2041 ---
Like all birds, this *pe'ape'a* has feathers and lays eggs.

--- SENTENCE 2042 ---
The other *pe'ape'a*, more properly

!

--- SENTENCE 2043 ---
[](_page_80_Picture_4.jpeg)

called *pe'ape'avai*, is a tiny bat, the Sheath-tailed Bat that is active only at night.

--- SENTENCE 2044 ---
This animal is now almost extinct in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 2045 ---
Like all bats, this *pe'ape'a* is a mammal and has fur and gives birth to live young that it feeds milk.

--- SENTENCE 2046 ---
The confusion arises because both creatures are tiny, active insect-eaters that are almost always in flight, and look similar as they dart and swoop after their prey.

!

--- SENTENCE 2047 ---
First let's talk about the common *pe'ape'a*: the bird.

--- SENTENCE 2048 ---
It belongs to a family of birds that are truly creatures of the air, the swifts.

--- SENTENCE 2049 ---
They have tiny legs and feet, and never land except at nests or in their roosting caves.

--- SENTENCE 2050 ---
Capturing and eating food, drinking, gathering nesting material, and yes, even mating, are all done in flight.

--- SENTENCE 2051 ---
In fact, it is likely that the *pe'ape'a* is like many other kinds of swifts and actually sleeps while flying.

--- SENTENCE 2052 ---
With their long, powerful wings and perfectly streamlined bodies, *pe'ape'a* are beautifully adapted for a life of flight.

--- SENTENCE 2053 ---
As they fly, *pe'ape'a* are continually hunting for small insects, especially mosquitoes, flies, and flying ants and termites.

--- SENTENCE 2054 ---
These are scooped up in the swift's huge mouth, which is made into an even larger trap by long bristle-like feathers around the mouth.

--- SENTENCE 2055 ---
The swiftlet is a very useful bird because of all the insects it eats.

--- SENTENCE 2056 ---
When the *pe'ape'a* finally does decide that it's time to land, it heads for a cave or a protected overhang on a cliff.

--- SENTENCE 2057 ---
There it flutters in to grasp the rock, usually hanging vertically.

--- SENTENCE 2058 ---
These caves and sheltered overhangs are also the nest site for the swifts.

--- SENTENCE 2059 ---
The nest is a small platform made of moss and fine twigs cemented together with the bird's saliva, attached to the rock.

--- SENTENCE 2060 ---
Some close relatives of the *pe'ape'a* make their nests entirely from dried saliva, which (believe it or not) are collected and cooked up to make that famous delicacy, bird's nest soup.

--- SENTENCE 2061 ---
The nests of our *pe'ape'a* are not suitable for this, so anyone with a taste for bird saliva will have to look elsewhere.

--- SENTENCE 2062 ---
Swiftlets lay one or two white eggs, and appear to nest at any time of year in Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 2063 ---
Although most nests are placed where at least a little light penetrates the cave, some are far back, where it is completely dark.

--- SENTENCE 2064 ---
How do the swifts find their way in and out?

--- SENTENCE 2065 ---
The answer is that these birds, like many small bats, have the amazing ability to echo-locate.

--- SENTENCE 2066 ---
They give loud clicking calls, and then listen to the echoes to orient themselves and avoid the walls of the cave.

--- SENTENCE 2067 ---
This ability is fairly undeveloped in birds, and the swiftlets don't use it to locate their insect prey, which is why they hunt during the daytime.

--- SENTENCE 2068 ---
In many small bats, however, echo-location is incredibly advanced, and is used to hunt tiny insects in complete darkness.

--- SENTENCE 2069 ---
One bat with this ability is the Sheath-tailed Bat, which brings us to our second *pe'ape'a*.

--- SENTENCE 2070 ---
The Sheath-tailed Bat (*Emballonura semicaudata*) also feeds on mosquitoes and other flying insects.

--- SENTENCE 2071 ---
But unlike the swift, this small bat does not seem to accept sheltered cliffs as roosting or breeding areas, inhabiting only deep and protected caves.

--- SENTENCE 2072 ---
There are few such caves in American Samoa, and therefore few good homes for the bat.

--- SENTENCE 2073 ---
Ever since Cyclone Ofa in 1990, the known bat caves on Tutuila have been almost deserted.

--- SENTENCE 2074 ---
Cyclones Ofa and Val swept water and debris into several caves, and the days of strong cyclone winds may have made it impossible for the bats to find food.

--- SENTENCE 2075 ---
Unless more bats survive in caves that we don't know about, the long-term survival of this useful and fascinating animal in American Samoa is doubtful.

--- SENTENCE 2076 ---
There is little we can do to help the bat except to stay away from their caves to avoid disturbing the few surviving animals.

--- SENTENCE 2077 ---
Sadly, the Sheath-tailed Bat seems to be endangered throughout most of its range, including in western Samoa and the Marianas, as well as here.

--- SENTENCE 2078 ---
And what about the swift?

--- SENTENCE 2079 ---
Although the population of these birds was reduced by cyclones in 1990-91, the species seems to be in no danger of extinction.

--- SENTENCE 2080 ---
It is still possible to see flocks of hundreds of swifts swirling together in areas where winds collect large numbers of insects, for example in Malaeimi Valley and in such highland areas as Aoloau and Afono Pass.

--- SENTENCE 2081 ---
There is every reason to believe that these birds will always enliven the Samoan sky -- and strike terror into the hearts of mosquitoes everywhere.

--- SENTENCE 2083 ---
Familiar and mysterious birds -- the rails**

The *ve'a*, or "roadrunner", is one of Samoa's most recognizable birds.

--- SENTENCE 2084 ---
It is a familiar sight tiptoeing out of the grass or running crazily across the road, its neck stretched out and its big feet trying to keep up.

--- SENTENCE 2085 ---
And its loud, screeching voice is a familiar sound, seeming to complain at the disturbance as we walk to the taro patch.

--- SENTENCE 2086 ---
However, for all its abundance, the *ve'a* is still a little-known bird, quickly disappearing from view when disturbed and impossible to follow in the thick grass it favors.

--- SENTENCE 2087 ---
What's more, the *ve'a* has two even more mysterious cousins in American Samoa, and another in western Samoa that, as we will see, is the most mysterious Samoan bird of all.

!

--- SENTENCE 2088 ---
[](_page_82_Picture_3.jpeg)

The *ve'a* and its cousins belong to the family of birds known as the rails (named from a old English word meaning to screech -- many of these birds have loud, harsh voices).

--- SENTENCE 2089 ---
The *ve'a* is called the Banded Rail, because of the black and white bands on its sides and underparts.

--- SENTENCE 2090 ---
It is found all across the Pacific from the Philippines and Indonesia to Samoa, Tonga, Fiji, and on to Australia and New Zealand.

--- SENTENCE 2091 ---
It is usually common wherever it occurs, with the exception of Fiji.

--- SENTENCE 2092 ---
On Fiji this formerly abundant bird has been exterminated on

the large islands of Vanua Levu and Viti Levu by an introduced predator, the mongoose.

--- SENTENCE 2093 ---
This shows how important it is to keep Samoa free from such destructive introduced animals.

--- SENTENCE 2094 ---
The *ve'a* is a very useful bird, eating many insects that can harm crops.

--- SENTENCE 2095 ---
In fact, it will eat almost anything, including fruit, worms, snails, mice, and even toads squashed flat on the roads.

--- SENTENCE 2096 ---
It sometimes can be seen deep in the forest, but prefers open areas with dense grass.

--- SENTENCE 2097 ---
Therefore, taro and banana plantations are some of its favorite places.

--- SENTENCE 2098 ---
The nest of the *ve'a* is very hard to find, being built on the ground well-hidden in thick grass.

--- SENTENCE 2099 ---
The *ve'a* lays 4 to 6 eggs.

--- SENTENCE 2100 ---
Like young chickens, young *ve'a* can run around almost as soon as they hatch, and they leave the nest immediately.

--- SENTENCE 2101 ---
It is common to see one or two fuzzy, dark gray chicks being led into the grass by their watchful parents.

--- SENTENCE 2102 ---
The other Samoan rail that most people know is the *manu ali'i*, known as the Purple Swamphen.

--- SENTENCE 2103 ---
This large and beautiful bird is a dark purple, with a bright red beak that extends up over the forehead.

--- SENTENCE 2104 ---
It is even more widely distributed than the *ve'a*, occurring from southern Europe through Africa and all the way to Australia and New Zealand.

--- SENTENCE 2105 ---
It is much less common than the *ve'a* in American Samoa, but occurs in low numbers almost everywhere, especially in more remote plantation areas.

--- SENTENCE 2106 ---
This bird is more often heard than seen; it gives a loud screech with an echoing, honking quality.

!

--- SENTENCE 2107 ---
[](_page_82_Picture_8.jpeg)

Like the *ve'a*, the *manu ali'i* prefers areas with thick undergrowth rather than true forest.

--- SENTENCE 2108 ---
It has a very wide diet, including many insects and much plant material.

--- SENTENCE 2109 ---
It sometimes gets into trouble with farmers because of its fondness for green bananas and taro tops.

--- SENTENCE 2110 ---
However, usually it does little damage, and repays it by eating soldier worms and other crop pests.

--- SENTENCE 2111 ---
In former times, the *manu ali'i* was sometimes hunted, and this may be why it is so shy, usually running or flying off with loud screams as soon as it sees you.

--- SENTENCE 2112 ---
Like the *ve'a*, it nests in thick vegetation, either on the ground or in a low bush.

--- SENTENCE 2113 ---
It lays 3 or 4 eggs, and the young leave the nest to follow their parents as soon as they hatch.

--- SENTENCE 2114 ---
Our third kind of rail is a real mystery bird, as shown by the fact that it has no Samoan name.

--- SENTENCE 2115 ---
In English it is called the Spotless Crake.

--- SENTENCE 2116 ---
Although its wide range in the Pacific is very similar to the *ve'a*, in American Samoa it has only been sighted on the island of Ta'u.

--- SENTENCE 2117 ---
The Spotless Crake is very small, only about half the size of a *ve'a*, and is all dark gray except for red legs and eyes.

--- SENTENCE 2118 ---
It lives in dense grass, almost never flies, and is active at dawn and dusk.

--- SENTENCE 2119 ---
All this makes it a very hard bird to find.

--- SENTENCE 2120 ---
In fact, only a single bird was seen by biologists in 1986, and another was sighted in 2001.

!

--- SENTENCE 2121 ---
There is one more kind of rail to mention, and this is the most mysterious Samoan bird of all.

--- SENTENCE 2122 ---
It is the *puna'e*, or Samoan Woodhen.

--- SENTENCE 2123 ---
This bird was first described by scientists in 1874, and last seen in 1908.

--- SENTENCE 2124 ---
In the whole world, only 11 preserved specimens of it exist.

--- SENTENCE 2125 ---
It apparently occurred only on Savai'i in western Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 2126 ---
Old stories tell that the *puna'e* lived in burrows in the mountain forests, and that it was formerly so abundant that it was hunted with nets and dogs specially trained to tell which burrows were occupied.

--- SENTENCE 2127 ---
If these stories are true, the *puna'e* was a very special bird indeed, since no other rails live in burrows.

--- SENTENCE 2128 ---
In the last ten years, there has been an unconfirmed report of the *puna'e* being seen in the mountains of Savai'i.

--- SENTENCE 2129 ---
These remote forests have been rarely visited by biologists, and we can hope that this unique bird may yet survive in the depths of that misty wilderness.

--- SENTENCE 2132 ---
Our strange winter visitor, the Cuckoo (***aleva***)**

The *aleva* or Long-tailed Cuckoo is a very odd bird, no doubt about it.

--- SENTENCE 2133 ---
For one thing, when the weather starts to get cold in its summer home, the *aleva* migrates north for the winter.

--- SENTENCE 2134 ---
For another, it has a highly unusual home life: it doesn't build its own nest or care for its own young, but abandons its eggs in the nests of other birds, to be raised not only by strangers, but by completely different kinds of birds.

--- SENTENCE 2135 ---
The *aleva* lives and breeds in New Zealand for half of the year.

--- SENTENCE 2136 ---
But when winter approaches, around March or April, the *aleva* leaves its chilly home islands, and flies north into sunny Polynesia.

--- SENTENCE 2137 ---
In fact, it

is the only land bird to migrate north to spend the winter with us (although a few *aleva* may be found here year-round).

--- SENTENCE 2138 ---
The *aleva*'s winter range extends all the way from Palau on the west to Pitcairn Island on the east, but the largest numbers winter in Fiji, Samoa, and Tonga.

--- SENTENCE 2139 ---
Here it can be found on any island, from the mountainous interiors of the largest high islands to the coastal vegetation of tiny atolls.

--- SENTENCE 2140 ---
Wherever it ends up, the *aleva* feeds mostly on large insects, including caterpillars and stick insects, but also small lizards.

!

--- SENTENCE 2141 ---
The *aleva* is a hard bird to see, usually creeping without a sound through dense foliage.

--- SENTENCE 2142 ---
In fact, we most often see *aleva* because they are being chased by other birds, especially the *iao* (Wattled Honeyeater).

--- SENTENCE 2143 ---
*Iao* seem to hate *aleva*, and chase them vigorously, with loud scolding cries.

--- SENTENCE 2144 ---
It is very interesting to try to figure out why they do this.

--- SENTENCE 2145 ---
There are at least three possibilities.

--- SENTENCE 2146 ---
It may be that *aleva* will eat eggs if they get the chance; however, this has never been observed.

--- SENTENCE 2147 ---
Second, in flight the *aleva* looks much like a hawk, with its pointed wings and long tail.

--- SENTENCE 2148 ---
Perhaps *iao* mistake the *aleva* for a hawk, and think they are chasing a dangerous predator away.

--- SENTENCE 2149 ---
But there are no hawks in Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 2150 ---
Therefore, this explanation would mean that *iao* still have an instinctive fear of hawk-like birds thousands of years after they last saw a hawk (which was probably when the ancestors of our *iao* came here from Fiji).

--- SENTENCE 2151 ---
The last explanation seems even more far-fetched.

--- SENTENCE 2152 ---
This is that the *iao* chases the *aleva* because it is afraid that the cuckoo will lay its eggs in the *iao*'s nest.

--- SENTENCE 2153 ---
To understand this possibility, we must discuss the strange breeding behavior of the *aleva*.

--- SENTENCE 2154 ---
Like many other members of the cuckoo family, the *aleva* is a nest parasite.

--- SENTENCE 2155 ---
This means that it secretly lays its eggs in other birds' nests.

--- SENTENCE 2156 ---
The unlucky host birds think that the cuckoo egg is their own, and raise it alongside their own babies.

--- SENTENCE 2157 ---
Usually the baby cuckoo is much larger than the babies of its hosts, who are unable to compete and starve to death.

--- SENTENCE 2158 ---
Many New Zealand birds, including honeyeaters related to the *iao*, recognize the *aleva* and chase it away.

--- SENTENCE 2159 ---
This makes a lot of sense in New Zealand, where the *aleva* breeds.

--- SENTENCE 2160 ---
But the *aleva* never breeds on its wintering grounds in Polynesia.

--- SENTENCE 2161 ---
Therefore, the *iao* has no reason to fear that the *aleva* will lay eggs in its nest.

--- SENTENCE 2162 ---
Could there be an instinctive hatred of cuckoos in all members of the honeyeater family?

--- SENTENCE 2163 ---
Like many questions in biology, this one would be hard to answer, but it's still fun to think about.

--- SENTENCE 2164 ---
Many older Samoans say that *aleva* used to be much more common than they are today.

--- SENTENCE 2165 ---
The *aleva* is now a rare sight on Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 2166 ---
It's hard to know just how rare the bird really is because of its secretive habits.

--- SENTENCE 2167 ---
If you see one, consider yourself lucky, and take a moment to think about the many mysteries in the life of this strange visitor to our shores.

--- SENTENCE 2169 ---
Barn owls (***lulu***) -- spirit of the night**

The barn owl or *lulu* is a highly beneficial bird in American Samoa, but some people fear it because they associate it with ghosts or *aitu*.

--- SENTENCE 2170 ---
Its ghostly white appearance and its preference for flying at night enhance its reputation as a mysterious bird.

--- SENTENCE 2171 ---
It is not uncommon when driving around Tutuila at night to see one perched on a telephone pole or gliding across the road.

--- SENTENCE 2172 ---
Its eerie screech pierces the night and can be frightening if you are not expecting it.

!

--- SENTENCE 2173 ---
[](_page_85_Picture_3.jpeg)

Owls are carnivores that usually swallow their prey whole.

--- SENTENCE 2174 ---
A few hours after swallowing a rat (*isumu*) or some other prey, owls regurgitate or spit out a pellet of indigestible bones and hair about two inches long and one inch in diameter.

--- SENTENCE 2175 ---
By examining these pellets, we can determine what the *lulu* eats.

--- SENTENCE 2176 ---
In the remains of 482 meals, rats made up most (81%) of those meals, followed by mice (9%), geckos (7%), and bones of a few birds (2%), which consisted of White Terns (*manu sina*), Wattled Honeyeaters (*iao*), mynas, and Samoan Starlings (*fuia*).

--- SENTENCE 2177 ---
No chicken remains were found, but this was not unexpected because owls do not generally attack large prey.

--- SENTENCE 2178 ---
Chickens and fruit bats (*pe'a*) are too heavy for an owl to carry and too large to swallow whole.

--- SENTENCE 2179 ---
Instead, they would typically eat the meat and viscera at the site of the kill.

--- SENTENCE 2180 ---
Soft tissues such as these would be totally digested and not be found in pellets.

--- SENTENCE 2181 ---
We have other evidence that one flying fox was killed and partially eaten by an owl.

--- SENTENCE 2182 ---
Even though *lulu* occasionally take a bird, bat or even a chicken, their value in controlling rat populations is tremendous.

--- SENTENCE 2183 ---
One pair of owls may catch 2-4 rats per day.

--- SENTENCE 2184 ---
Without owls, Tutuila and Manu'a would probably be over-run with rats.

--- SENTENCE 2185 ---
A *lulu* seen flying through a village should not be feared or hated but welcomed, because it will probably mean one less rat near your *fale*.

--- SENTENCE 2187 ---
Birds of wetland and reef: Gray Duck and Reef Heron**

Two very different birds depend on Samoa's shallow water habitats.

--- SENTENCE 2188 ---
One is the familiar *matu'u*, or Pacific Reef-Heron, a bird that is seen nearly every day by anyone visiting Samoa's shoreline.

--- SENTENCE 2189 ---
The other is Samoa's only duck, the *toloa* or Gray Duck, a bird that is so rare that most young Samoans have probably never seen one.

--- SENTENCE 2190 ---
It comes as a surprise to some people that we don't have more kinds of ducks in Polynesia.

--- SENTENCE 2191 ---
After all, ducks like water, and we've got plenty of that, right?

--- SENTENCE 2192 ---
Well, we've got plenty of salt water, but very few lakes, marshes, or large streams of fresh water.

--- SENTENCE 2193 ---
Almost all ducks prefer fresh water, and even the few kinds of "sea ducks" eat mostly seagrass and other plants that aren't found around Samoa's coral reefs.

--- SENTENCE 2194 ---
Therefore, there aren't many suitable homes for *toloa* in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 2195 ---
These suitable homes are the fresh and brackish (fresh/saltwater mix) water wetlands.

!

--- SENTENCE 2196 ---
[](_page_86_Picture_4.jpeg)

"Wetlands" is a word that we've been hearing a lot lately.

--- SENTENCE 2197 ---
It refers to areas of land that are permanently or periodically flooded or submerged in shallow water.

--- SENTENCE 2198 ---
So much wetland habitat has been lost in the United States, primarily through draining and filling, that strict federal laws have been passed protecting wetlands.

--- SENTENCE 2199 ---
Locally, the Coastal Zone Management Program is responsible for protecting our remaining wetlands, which serve vital roles as nurseries for fish and crabs, natural water purification and recharge areas, sites for taro production, and homes for rare plants and wildlife.

--- SENTENCE 2200 ---
There used to be extensive mangrove (*togo*) swamps bordering Pago Pago Bay, but these were filled in long ago, before the negative environmental consequences of such activities were understood.

--- SENTENCE 2201 ---
The largest remaining wetland areas here are the *pala* lagoons in Nu'uuli and Leone and the marshes and Pala Lake on Aunu'u Island.

--- SENTENCE 2202 ---
There are also many other small but important wetlands remaining in both Tutuila and Manu'a.

!

--- SENTENCE 2203 ---
[](_page_86_Picture_6.jpeg)

All of these areas were once home to *toloa*.

--- SENTENCE 2204 ---
However, as wetlands were filled in and as shotguns became available, this once-popular game bird became rarer and rarer.

--- SENTENCE 2205 ---
During a major survey of Samoan birds in 1986, not a single *toloa* was seen, leading to the concern that this bird might be extinct in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 2206 ---
Happily, this is not the case.

--- SENTENCE 2207 ---
A few *toloa* still live in the territory, mostly on Aunu'u, where the village leaders have taken an active role in protecting their ducks.

--- SENTENCE 2208 ---
This bird, and the wetlands it depends upon, need our continued protection if it is ever to recover to healthy population levels.

--- SENTENCE 2209 ---
The *toloa* is a "dabbling duck".

--- SENTENCE 2210 ---
This means that it doesn't dive under the water to feed, but rather tips down, with its tail in the air and its neck stretching under the water, to reach the plants and small insects and snails that it feeds on.

--- SENTENCE 2211 ---
Although it sometimes swims in the sea, it does not feed there, and, like most ducks, it does not eat fish.

--- SENTENCE 2212 ---
One interesting thing about the *toloa* is that is doesn't always nest near the water.

--- SENTENCE 2213 ---
It may build its nest in tall dense grass, or place it in a low tree hollow.

--- SENTENCE 2214 ---
Five to ten pale green eggs are laid.

--- SENTENCE 2215 ---
The journey of the young ducks from the nest to the safety of the water is a dangerous one, and usually only one or two ducklings make it.

--- SENTENCE 2216 ---
The *toloa* is a great wanderer.

--- SENTENCE 2217 ---
The bird is found across a huge geographic area, extending from Indonesia to French Polynesia, and south to New Zealand and Australia.

--- SENTENCE 2218 ---
The *toloa* is a strong, fast flier, and commonly flies between islands.

--- SENTENCE 2219 ---
It seems likely that the small population on Aunu'u, which was apparently absent in the late 1980's, may have recolonized American Samoa from western Samoa, where the duck is slightly more common.

--- SENTENCE 2220 ---
This gives us reason to hope that the *toloa* may return to areas that it formerly used, if the wetlands are preserved, and the duck is protected from hunting.

--- SENTENCE 2221 ---
So ... if you see a *toloa*, consider yourself lucky, and please don't disturb it.

--- SENTENCE 2222 ---
Perhaps one day Samoa's special duck will once again be a common sight.

--- SENTENCE 2223 ---
The *matu'u*, or Pacific Reef-Heron, is a common sight today.

--- SENTENCE 2224 ---
With its long legs and long neck, often curved in an S-shape, the *matu'u* is one of Samoa's most recognizable birds.

--- SENTENCE 2225 ---
One interesting fact about the *matu'u* is that it comes in two color forms, either dark gray or pure white, although almost all the *matu'u* in Samoa are dark gray.

--- SENTENCE 2226 ---
In other areas, particularly atolls like Swains Island or Rose Atoll, almost all the *matu'u* are white.

--- SENTENCE 2227 ---
It seems that in Polynesia the dark forms are found on volcanic islands, with dark lava rocks, while the white birds occur on atolls with white sandy shorelines.

--- SENTENCE 2228 ---
In many other parts of its range, however, including Fiji, both white and gray forms occur commonly in the same area.

--- SENTENCE 2229 ---
The reason for the *matu'u*'s two color forms remains a unclear.

--- SENTENCE 2230 ---
The *matu'u* is the master spear fisherman of the bird world.

--- SENTENCE 2231 ---
It stalks slowly across the reef flat, scanning for the slightest movement, and then throws its long neck forward as fast as lightning, catching its prey with its long sharp beak.

--- SENTENCE 2232 ---
It feeds on a wide variety of reef creatures, including fish, crabs, and snails.

--- SENTENCE 2233 ---
Although the *matu'u* usually makes its living feeding on the coral reef, it also uses the freshwater wetlands of Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 2234 ---
Mangrove areas like the Nu'uuli Pala are frequent feeding sites, and the birds will also hunt for food in freshwater streams.

--- SENTENCE 2235 ---
These rainforest stream valleys are the *matu'u*'s favored nesting areas, where the herons build their large nests high in the trees.

--- SENTENCE 2236 ---
Some *matu'u* also nest on offshore islets, and have even been reported to nest on ledges in caves.

--- SENTENCE 2237 ---
Usually three pale green eggs are laid.

--- SENTENCE 2238 ---
Strict protection of Samoa's wetlands is essential if the *toloa* is to regain its place among our familiar native birds and if the *matu'u* is to continue to be a common resident of our shoreline villages.

--- SENTENCE 2239 ---
Please help spread the message that landfills kill a very important part of Samoa's natural environment -- our wetlands.

!

--- SENTENCE 2240 ---
Shorebirds (***o tuli***): incredible voyagers**

April/May is the time of year when some familiar visitors leave American Samoa and begin an incredible and perilous journey home.

--- SENTENCE 2241 ---
These visitors are the shorebirds, or *tuli*.

--- SENTENCE 2242 ---
Three different kinds of *tuli* are common visitors here between the months of September and April: the Pacific Golden Plover, the Wandering Tattler, and the Ruddy Turnstone.

--- SENTENCE 2243 ---
During the northern summer, all three *tuli* nest in Alaska and northern Canada.

--- SENTENCE 2244 ---
The plover and the turnstone nest in the tundra, where their neighbors are caribou and grizzly bears.

--- SENTENCE 2245 ---
The tattlers nest by icy mountain streams, sharing their world with mountain sheep and golden eagles.

--- SENTENCE 2246 ---
But when the northern days grow shorter and colder, the birds probably begin to dream of the beaches and reefs of Polynesia, for they set out on one of the greatest of all animal journeys.

--- SENTENCE 2247 ---
From gathering places on the coasts of Alaska, the *tuli* take off to fly non-stop over 3,000 miles of open ocean to the Hawaiian Islands.

--- SENTENCE 2248 ---
These birds cannot land on the water and take a rest -- their feathers are not waterproof, so if they land in the water, they drown.

--- SENTENCE 2249 ---
Not only is this flight an amazing physical feat, but it requires tremendous navigational abilities to find tiny specks of land lost in the featureless sea.

--- SENTENCE 2250 ---
After a well-earned rest in Hawaii, the *tuli* take off again for another flight over thousands of miles of ocean to reach Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 2251 ---
Here, each kind of *tuli* takes up a slightly different lifestyle.

--- SENTENCE 2252 ---
The Golden Plover is often seen on the beaches, but even more commonly can be found searching for food on the short grass of *malae*, parks, the golf course, and the airport.

--- SENTENCE 2253 ---
We can only hope it will not be harmed from competing with the many myna birds that now occupy these areas.

--- SENTENCE 2254 ---
Although this plover is plain brown during most of its stay in American Samoa, it gets a beautiful new set of feathers just before it heads north: a golden-spangled back and jet black underparts.

--- SENTENCE 2255 ---
The plover is the commonest *tuli* in Samoa, and it features in many myths and legends.

--- SENTENCE 2256 ---
*Tuli* the Messenger is one manifestation of Tagaloa, the supreme god of the ancient Samoans, and in one version of the Samoan creation story, Tagaloa created the first dry land as a resting place for *tuli*.

!

--- SENTENCE 2257 ---
The Wandering Tattler is a beachcomber.

--- SENTENCE 2258 ---
It is almost always seen on beaches or the reef, using its long beak to pry small crabs, worms, and other delicacies out of the sand or coral.

--- SENTENCE 2259 ---
It is a solitary bird, soberly dressed in gray.

--- SENTENCE 2260 ---
When disturbed, the tattler's loud cries explain how it got its name.

--- SENTENCE 2261 ---
The turnstone was named for its way of feeding.

--- SENTENCE 2262 ---
These small, energetic, sociable birds like to vigorously flip over small stones, bits of seaweed, or debris in search of food hiding beneath.

--- SENTENCE 2263 ---
They can be seen either on the beach or in grassy areas.

--- SENTENCE 2264 ---
The turnstone has a beautiful pattern of black and white patches on its back when it flies, and in breeding plumage, it is a handsome bird with orange-brown, black, and white patterning.

--- SENTENCE 2265 ---
All these *tuli* can usually be seen in such places as Sliding Rock and Pala Lagoon from September to April.

--- SENTENCE 2266 ---
But in April/May, all but a few stragglers will have left Samoa to take their long, long trip back to Alaska.

--- SENTENCE 2267 ---
So, the next time you see some *tuli*, wish them a safe journey and try to imagine, maybe with a little envy, their great adventure.

--- SENTENCE 2270 ---
Samoa's seabirds:** *tava'e***,** *gogo* **and** *manu sina*

The islands of Samoa are true oceanic islands: they are not riders on the skirts of any continent, but are the tops of huge mountains, rising up through the great ocean depths.

--- SENTENCE 2271 ---
Surrounded as they are by thousands of square miles of ocean, it is not surprising that the Samoan Islands have more kinds of seabirds than any other type of native wildlife.

--- SENTENCE 2272 ---
In all, 20 species of seabirds are known to nest in Samoa (and many more can be seen passing through our area), compared to 18 kinds of resident landbirds and only three kinds of mammals (all bat species).

--- SENTENCE 2273 ---
Our seabirds are a diverse group.

--- SENTENCE 2274 ---
Some, such as shearwaters and petrels (*ta'i'o*), are rarely seen, but nest in burrows on the tops of mountains like Lata and Pioa.

--- SENTENCE 2275 ---
Their eerie calls can be heard at night in places such as Afono Pass.

--- SENTENCE 2276 ---
Others like frigatebirds (*atafa)* and boobies (*fua'o*) are seen flying around our coasts, but nest on the high cliffs of Pola or in trees on the remote north side of Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 2277 ---
The tern family has many different looking birds living here, including the solid black and brown noddies (*gogo*), the beautiful blue-grey noddy (*laia*), the white tern (*manu sina*), and the grey-backed tern (*gogo sina*).

--- SENTENCE 2278 ---
Here we will discuss the three most common and conspicuous seabirds of Tutuila: the *tava'e*, *manu sina*, and *gogo*.

!

--- SENTENCE 2279 ---
[](_page_90_Picture_4.jpeg)

The *tava'e (tava'esina)*, or White-tailed Tropicbird, is one of Samoa's most beautiful creatures.

--- SENTENCE 2280 ---
The sight of a *tava'e* soaring overhead is unforgettable, as its gleaming white feathers and graceful tail streamers blaze against the deep indigo of the Polynesian sky.

--- SENTENCE 2281 ---
Happily, it is also a familiar sight, as these birds are common all around Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 2282 ---
The *tava'e*, so graceful in the air, is comical and awkward on land, so much so that Samoan legends describe the bird as lazy or dim-witted.

--- SENTENCE 2283 ---
In fact, *tava'e* are probably as industrious and intelligent as your average bird.

--- SENTENCE 2284 ---
Their awkwardness results from the fact that their legs and feet are tiny for the size of the bird, and are placed far back on the body.

--- SENTENCE 2285 ---
Thus, all *tava'e* can do on land is to shuffle along, bumping their powerful chest muscles on the ground.

--- SENTENCE 2286 ---
Luckily for them, *tava'e* spend little time on land.

--- SENTENCE 2287 ---
They typically nest high in rainforest trees, preferring protected sites such as large hollows or clumps of ferns or other plant material.

--- SENTENCE 2288 ---
Here they lay a single large egg.

--- SENTENCE 2289 ---
At hatching, the young tropicbird is one of the cutest of all baby birds -- a completely round ball of silvery fluff.

--- SENTENCE 2290 ---
As they grow, they molt into their juvenile plumage, with black and white barred feathers across their back.

--- SENTENCE 2291 ---
This is their appearance when they leave the nest on their first flight toward the sea, often a mile or more away.

--- SENTENCE 2292 ---
The fledglings often seem to have problems with this flight, based on the number of young *tava'e* that are brought in to our office, unable to fly.

--- SENTENCE 2293 ---
These youngsters are usually very gentle, and with several days of feeding with sardines or other fish, they often regain their strength and are able to fly away.

--- SENTENCE 2294 ---
*Tava'e* are master fishermen, making plunging dives for squid and a variety of small fish.

--- SENTENCE 2295 ---
One of their favorites are flying fish -- I've often wondered whether they catch the flying fish in the air or while they're swimming.

--- SENTENCE 2296 ---
The *tava'e* is well known to Samoan fishermen, who watch its behavior at sea to help them locate schools of *masimasi* and other fish.

--- SENTENCE 2297 ---
The *tava'e* is important to human fishermen for another reason: its feathers are prized for fishing lures.

--- SENTENCE 2298 ---
The gleaming white breast feathers are tied to hooks and used to attract *malau* (squirrelfish) and other fish.

--- SENTENCE 2299 ---
It is a traditional belief that only feathers from living birds stay dry and shiny after repeated use, and it was a badge of honor for a Samoan youth to climb a *tava'e* nesting tree and pluck out the plumes.

--- SENTENCE 2300 ---
This supplied a lure while causing no harm to the birds.

--- SENTENCE 2301 ---
The *tava'e* has a close relative, the Red-tailed Tropicbird, or *tava'e'ula*, that nests on Rose Atoll.

--- SENTENCE 2302 ---
This bird is larger and heavier than the *tava'e*, with shining pinkish-white feathers and red tail plumes.

--- SENTENCE 2303 ---
It typically nests on the ground among rocks, roots, or logs.

--- SENTENCE 2304 ---
In former times, this bird apparently nested on Tutuila, but it no longer does so.

--- SENTENCE 2305 ---
The reason may be the abundance of introduced animals such as rats, cats, and dogs that attack ground-nesting birds on Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 2306 ---
Our next seabird, the *manu sina* or *manusina* (also called the white tern or common fairy tern) is perhaps Samoa's most familiar bird.

--- SENTENCE 2307 ---
At almost any time of day you can lift your eyes to the mountains and see these pure white birds circling effortlessly over the green forest.

--- SENTENCE 2308 ---
I recommend contemplation of this lovely sight to anyone feeling burned out by the noise and ugliness that sometimes surround us at eye level.

--- SENTENCE 2309 ---
It's always a nice reminder of the beauty of the natural Samoan environment.

!

--- SENTENCE 2310 ---
[](_page_91_Picture_3.jpeg)

The *manu sina* is found throughout the tropical oceans of the world and seems able to adapt to human-altered

landscapes better than do many seabirds.

--- SENTENCE 2311 ---
Those of you familiar with Honolulu may have noticed *manu sina* fluttering in the trees along Kalakaua Avenue, where they actually nest in the middle of Waikiki.

--- SENTENCE 2312 ---
The nesting behavior of *manu sina* is remarkable indeed.

--- SENTENCE 2313 ---
In fact, "nesting" is being a bit generous, since *manu sina* make no nest at all.

--- SENTENCE 2314 ---
Instead, they balance their large single egg in a knothole, stub, or other more or less precarious perch in a tree.

--- SENTENCE 2315 ---
They will even accept man-made structures like rooftops and ledges.

--- SENTENCE 2316 ---
It is amazing to see a *manu sina* stubbornly sitting on its egg as a strong tropical storm swings its branch wildly.

--- SENTENCE 2317 ---
Risky though is seems to us, this nesting strategy certainly works, as the White Tern is a common and widespread bird.

--- SENTENCE 2318 ---
*Manu sina* feed on a variety of small ocean fish, which they pluck from the surface.

--- SENTENCE 2319 ---
Unlike all the other seabirds of Samoa, the *manu sina* carries fish back to its chick in its beak, rather than swallowing them first.

--- SENTENCE 2320 ---
In fact, they have the incredible ability to capture several tiny fish in a row, shift them crosswise in their bill, and carry them back home, without dropping a one.

--- SENTENCE 2321 ---
How do they do it?

--- SENTENCE 2322 ---
The final seabird to be discussed is the *gogo*, or Brown Noddy.

--- SENTENCE 2323 ---
Like the *manu sina*, this bird is a member of the tern family, but these two cousins are different in many ways.

--- SENTENCE 2324 ---
For starters, the *gogo* is as dark as the *manu sina* is light.

--- SENTENCE 2325 ---
It is dark chocolate brown all over, except for the top of the head, which is a whitish-gray cap.

--- SENTENCE 2326 ---
It is also more sociable than the *manu sina*, usually nesting in loose groups, or colonies.

--- SENTENCE 2327 ---
The *gogo* is very flexible in its nesting behavior.

--- SENTENCE 2328 ---
On Tutuila, *gogo* usually nest high in trees in forested stream

!

--- SENTENCE 2329 ---
[](_page_91_Picture_9.jpeg)

valleys, building bulky nests of dead leaves and other plant material.

--- SENTENCE 2330 ---
They also sometimes nest on cliffs, for example at Fagatele Bay.

--- SENTENCE 2331 ---
On Rose Island and other atolls, *gogo* are happy to lay their eggs on the ground, making only a small scrape in the coral rubble, which they often decorate with empty seashells and even bones.

--- SENTENCE 2332 ---
Like other tropical seabirds, the *gogo* typically lays only a single egg.

--- SENTENCE 2333 ---
There is another *gogo* here, the Black Noddy, which is smaller and blacker than the Brown Noddy, and has a different way of flying.

--- SENTENCE 2334 ---
In good light they are separated from Brown Noddies that have a distinct brown and black pattern on the wings.

--- SENTENCE 2335 ---
Black Noddies are more often seen feeding in flocks with *manu sina* than are Brown Noddies.

--- SENTENCE 2336 ---
The diet of the *gogo* is apparently similar to that of the *manu sina*, since both birds feed on small fish and squid captured near the surface.

--- SENTENCE 2337 ---
How do different birds with such similar food habits coexist?

--- SENTENCE 2338 ---
Maybe each catches just a particular kind or size of fish, or dives to a certain depth.

--- SENTENCE 2339 ---
One of the most interesting sights in American Samoa is a foraging flock of seabirds.

--- SENTENCE 2340 ---
These groups can have from five or ten to hundreds of birds.

--- SENTENCE 2341 ---
They are a great chance to see the many different ways seabirds catch fish: *laia* flutter above the waves, sometimes dangling their feet into the water, *gogo* swoop low over the water, *fua'o* dive into the water with a huge splash, and *atafa* wait until a *fua'o* catches some fish, then chase it and take the fish from it.

--- SENTENCE 2342 ---
The birds you see in such a flock are a good indication of which fish are below the water, something Samoan fishermen use to locate schools of fish.

--- SENTENCE 2343 ---
A flock of small birds like White Terns and Black Noddies usually indicates skipjack or small tuna, while large flocks of *fua'o* indicate larger fish.

--- SENTENCE 2344 ---
As fishing partners or simply as beautiful neighbors on our islands, Samoa's seabirds are a special part of our wild heritage.

--- SENTENCE 2347 ---
The special birds of Manu'a**

The Manu'a Islands are famous for their history, culture, and spectacular beauty, but the wildlife of Ta'u, Ofu, and Olosega is also special.

--- SENTENCE 2348 ---
Given that these islands are only 60 miles east of Tutuila, you might expect that the same kinds of birds and animals would be found in both places.

--- SENTENCE 2349 ---
But in fact there are four kinds of birds living in Manu'a that do not occur on Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 2350 ---
The most beautiful of these is Samoa's only parrot, the *segavao*, or Bluecrowned Lory.

--- SENTENCE 2351 ---
This is a tiny jewel of a bird, only 7 inches long, but colored with emerald green back and wings, ruby red face and throat, and a sapphire blue crown.

--- SENTENCE 2352 ---
In historic times, these colorful feathers were used to decorate finemats.

--- SENTENCE 2353 ---
*Segavao* often come in to villages to feed on the nectar of coconut (*niu*) and coral tree (*gatae)* flowers, but they are not easy to see in spite of their bright colors.

--- SENTENCE 2354 ---
Your first clue to their presence is often their high-pitched whistles, given as they fly overhead or scramble in the top of a coconut tree.

--- SENTENCE 2355 ---
This pretty little parrot is also found in western Samoa, but does not live on Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 2356 ---
We don't know why.

--- SENTENCE 2357 ---
Tutuila seems to have some of the food *segavao* like, including *aoa* fruits, but perhaps there are not enough flowers all year round.

--- SENTENCE 2358 ---
In the forests of Manu'a, *segavao* particularly like the flowers of *Astronidium pickeringii*, a species for which we know no Samoan name.

--- SENTENCE 2359 ---
This tree of the melastome family is common in the high forests of Manu'a.

!

--- SENTENCE 2360 ---
[](_page_93_Picture_5.jpeg)

Another special bird of Manu'a is the *sega o le vau*, the Lesser (Fiji) Shrikebill.

--- SENTENCE 2361 ---
This is a charcoal-grey bird with tiny white corners on its tail, although if you get a very good look you might see a little brown along its sides, or its blue-grey legs.

--- SENTENCE 2362 ---
*Sega o le vau* are about the same size as *iao*, but are stockier and have bigger heads and wider beaks.

--- SENTENCE 2363 ---
This bird is found nowhere else in the Samoan islands.

--- SENTENCE 2364 ---
It also occurs in Fiji, but birds there look very different and are mostly brown.

--- SENTENCE 2365 ---
*Sega o le vau* can be seen on all three islands of Manu'a, but they are not closely tied to a particular habitat.

--- SENTENCE 2366 ---
They are usually heard before they are seen, as they often make a loud chatter as they poke through dead vines or *ie'ie* leaves.

--- SENTENCE 2367 ---
They also make a clear two-toned whistle, and

!

--- SENTENCE 2368 ---
[](_page_93_Picture_7.jpeg)

may answer if you imitate this call.

--- SENTENCE 2369 ---
These birds seem sociable and often move through the forest in small groups of two or three.

--- SENTENCE 2370 ---
They often ignore people, and if you sit very still they will sometimes feed within arms reach.

--- SENTENCE 2371 ---
The Spotless Crake is a small black bird that looks like a half-grown Banded Rail (*ve'a*).

--- SENTENCE 2372 ---
It is extremely rare and has only been found in American Samoa on Ta'u Island.

--- SENTENCE 2373 ---
It lives in dense grass, is active mostly at dawn and dusk, and is very shy.

--- SENTENCE 2374 ---
All these habits makes it very hard to see, and only a few birds have been sighted in recent years.

!

--- SENTENCE 2375 ---
[](_page_93_Picture_10.jpeg)

Another rare land bird in American Samoa is the *tuaimeo*, the Friendly Ground-Dove.

--- SENTENCE 2376 ---
This bird gets its name because it was first discovered in Tonga, once called the Friendly Islands.

--- SENTENCE 2377 ---
A better name would probably be the Shy Ground-Dove, as this bird will sit very still on a low branch and hope you do not notice it.

--- SENTENCE 2378 ---
Your best chance of seeing one is if it happens to be walking in the path you are using, as it may run ahead of you or fly up onto a tree limb.

--- SENTENCE 2379 ---
You may also hear its call, a single, drawn-out coo that is slightly lower and much longer than the rapid, short coos of the fruit-doves *manutagi* or *manuma*.

--- SENTENCE 2380 ---
If you are very close, you can hear a slight variation in the tone of the call.

--- SENTENCE 2381 ---
*Tuaimeo* are mostly dark brown birds, although like many doves

the males can be very pretty if seen in good light.

--- SENTENCE 2382 ---
Unlike other doves or pigeons in American Samoa, this bird spends most of its time on the ground, where it feeds on fallen seeds, fruit, and buds.

--- SENTENCE 2383 ---
*Tuaimeo* can be seen on Ofu and Olosega, as well as in Fiji, Tonga, and Samoa.

Birds are not the only unique wildlife of Manu'a.

--- SENTENCE 2385 ---
For example, Ta'u is home to American Samoa's only native

Why is the wildlife in Manu'a so different from that on Tutuila?

--- SENTENCE 2387 ---
One reason could be the wildness of Manu'a: large areas, mostly on Ta'u, are untouched by villages, plantations, and roads.

--- SENTENCE 2388 ---
Another reason may be that in Manu'a

--- SENTENCE 2389 ---

--- SENTENCE 2401 ---
The total viewer-hours were 9.5: 3.5 hours on foot and 6 hours by vehicle.

--- SENTENCE 2402 ---
Participants traveled 2.5 miles on foot and 35 miles by vehicle.

--- SENTENCE 2403 ---
Percentage of time in each habitat was: 30% in villages, 30% in nearshore/reef, 10% in plantations, 10% in montane scrub, 10% in rainforest, and 10% in shore/scrub.

--- SENTENCE 2404 ---
Four of the species recorded are introductions.

--- SENTENCE 2405 ---
The Rock Dove is not established as a breeding bird in the territory and we believe that the one observed is an escapee from a small colony of homing pigeons maintained by a private individual in Tafuna.

--- SENTENCE 2406 ---
The Red-vented Bulbul has been established on Tutuila since at least the early 1960's.

--- SENTENCE 2407 ---
Both species of mynas became established here in the 1980's and are currently undergoing rapid range expansions in urban areas.

--- SENTENCE 2408 ---
The observation of Bridled Terns is noteworthy.

--- SENTENCE 2409 ---
This species has been considered a rare visitor to American Samoa and is not a confirmed breeder.

--- SENTENCE 2410 ---
However, during the Christmas Count, we observed at least two pairs of birds repeatedly landing on ledges of the Pola Islet.

--- SENTENCE 2411 ---
This is the primary breeding area for boobies and frigatebirds on Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 2412 ---
The terns' behavior suggested breeding activity, but we could not confirm this.

--- SENTENCE 2413 ---
We have repeatedly observed Bridled Terns in Pago Pago Harbor and at various points along the coast between October and March.

## ACANTHASTER IN THE CULTURES OF HIGH ISLANDS

by

## Charles Birkeland 1/

Residents of high islands in Micronesia, Melanesia and Polynesia usually have their own special names for Acanthaster, each have similar advice on curing the sting of the spines, and each claim that Acanthaster has been abundant at certain times in the past.

--- SENTENCE 2 ---
I believe that this familiarity of Acanthaster in some high island cultures implies that outbreaks are a naturally recurring phenomenon around high islands.

--- SENTENCE 3 ---
An apparent lack of familiarity of Acanthaster in the cultures of atolls implies that outbreaks of Acanthaster are much less frequent around atolls (low islands).

--- SENTENCE 4 ---
In Palau (Micronesia), the Acanthaster is called rrusech (Birkeland, 1979) while other starfish are called btuch or tengetang.

--- SENTENCE 5 ---
At Fiji (Melanesia), Acanthaster is called bula (a homonym of "hello") while the general terms for "starfish" are gasagasan or basage (Atelaite Smalley, pers.

--- SENTENCE 6 ---
In the Cook and Society Islands (Polynesia), Acanthaster is called taramea and in Samoa and Tonga (Polynesia), Acanthaster is called alamea (Garlovsky and Bergquist, 1970; Birkeland and Randall, 1979; Flanigan and Lamberts, 1981).

--- SENTENCE 7 ---
In contrast, the languages from atolls appear to not contain terms for Acanthaster.

--- SENTENCE 8 ---
There is no special word for Acanthaster on Pingelap (Spensin James, pers.

comm.); the crown-of-thorns is merely referred to as isu, a term used for all starfish.

--- SENTENCE 10 ---
Similarly, Acanthaster is called talwalyol on Ulithi, a general term for all starfish (Eulalia Harui, pers.

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
Abo et al. (1976) list 12,000 entries with information on about 30,000 Marshallese words.

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
Many fishes, three groups of sea cucumbers, and other marine organisms are mentioned, but there was no word for Acanthaster.

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
It must not be important to the Marshallese.

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
The Gilbert Islands (Kiribati), the Ellice Islands (Tuvalu) and Fanning Island are all atolls.

--- SENTENCE 15 ---
Lobel (1978) presented a list of 407 names of fishes and 95 names of marine invertebrates used by Gilbertese and Ellice Islanders on Fanning Island, but Acanthaster was not listed.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
These inhabitants of atolls had their own specific names for many species of fishes, mollusks and crustaceans and even distinguished between three groups of holothurians, but all seastars came under one name.

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
Acanthaster may have never been abundant on these atolls.

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
There is an exception from the atoll of Mokil (near Ponape) at which the people do call Acanthaster by the name larni.

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
Of course there may be other exceptions.

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
However, there does seem to be a general presence of words for Acanthaster in high island languages and an apparent lack of words for Acanthaster in low island languages.

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
These tendencies suggest that Acanthaster may be more common around high islands.

--- SENTENCE 25 ---
Around high islands, the people remember previous outbreaks.

--- SENTENCE 26 ---
According to local fishermen, there was an outbreak in American Samoa in 1938 (Flanigan and Lamberts 1981).

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
Vine (1970) reported that fishermen in the Solomon Islands (Melanesia) remembered large concentrations of Acanthaster about 1930, forty years previous to 1970.

--- SENTENCE 28 ---
Chesher (1969) reported that Micronesians remember an outbreak on Ponape just after World War II.

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
Michael Parke talked to an old Palauan fisherman who described an extensive infestation that took place just prior to World War II.

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
According to this fisherman, the Acanthaster soon disappeared, leaving algae in the place of coral.

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
Then urchins became abundant during the early years of World War II.

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
The fisherman felt that Acanthaster were transitory and no real problem.

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
The abundance of urchins that resulted were a benefit.

--- SENTENCE 34 ---
Old people could easily collect them for food within wading depth on the reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
We have not heard of other cases of an abundance of urchins following Acanthaster.

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
It will be interesting to see if herbivorous urchins become common following the present devastation in Palau.

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
Except for areas around artificial sea walls, breakwaters and ramps, regular urchins are remarkably scarce in Palau at the time of this writing.

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
The people on high islands tell of dangers of stepping on Acanthaster when fishing at night at times when Acanthaster is abundant (Vaolui, pers.

--- SENTENCE 39 ---
Flanigan and Lamberts, 1981).

--- SENTENCE 40 ---
A cure for injury from stepping on Acanthaster is claimed by several high island cultures to be their own discovery.

--- SENTENCE 41 ---
When I was studying an Acanthaster outbreak in Palau, I accidentally jabbed my knee strongly against an Acanthaster and came to the boat with a lot of blood dripping out of six cuts in my knee.

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
The boatman, Ngirbauliad ("Yahd") Mineich, advised me to take one of the Acanthaster and place it mouth down on the bloody knee.

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
(This was tried, but was not found to be of great help.)

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
When asked if he heard of this cure from a Samoan or Fijian, Yahd said it has always been common knowledge in Palau.

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
Ramon Rechebei, another Palauan, said that he knew of this cure since he was a boy.

--- SENTENCE 46 ---
Spensin James told me that this cure had worked for him when he tried it.

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
This cure was common knowledge among Ponapean fishermen and it works if you are sure to use the same individual Acanthaster that harmed you as the individual to cure you.

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
If you are jabbed by one Acanthaster and lift another to cure your pain, it will be of no use.

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
(I am not sure I used the same individual in Palau.)

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
Laite Smalley told me that when Fijian fishermen step on bula on the reef flat, they turn over the same bula and put their food against the mouth so that the bula will suck out the poison.

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
She said this was generally known by Fijian fishermen and there is no reason to believe it was learned from the Palauans or Samoans.

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
Maybe the cure was discovered in Fiji.

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
This same cure has been known on Tonga (Richard Braley, pers.

--- SENTENCE 54 ---
, and as a proverb on Western Samoa (Garlovsky and Bergquist, 1970) and American Samoa (Birkeland and Randall, 1979; Flanigan and Lamberts, 1981).

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
The Secretariat of the British Solomon Islands Protectorate (1970) noted that this same cure by turning over and stepping on the underside of the Acanthaster was known in the Solomon Islands, New Britain, Manus Islands, and Gambier Islands.

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
The apparent history of recurring abundances of Acanthaster around high islands but not around atolls may be explained by the causal mechanism of Acanthaster outbreaks as suggested in Birkeland and Randall (1979) and Birkeland (1980).

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
Acanthaster larvae may survive in much greater abundance following heavy rainfall.

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
This might be because phytoplankton blooms are triggered by terrestrial runoff and this provides an abundant food source for Acanthaster larvae.

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
Terrestrial runoff resulting from rains on high islands trigger phytoplankton blooms (Marsh 1977), but it is doubtful that terrestrial runoffs from low, sandy atolls carry an amount of nutrients into the coastal waters adequate to trigger blooms.

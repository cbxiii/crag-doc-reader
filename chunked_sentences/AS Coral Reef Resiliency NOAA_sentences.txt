# **Coral Reef Resilience and Social Vulnerability to Climate Change**

##

--- SENTENCE 2 ---
Summary

We present an analysis of exposure, resilience, and social vulnerability to climate change threats for the coral reefs of American Samoa, relative to the rest of the U.S. Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 3 ---
We focus primarily on increases in ocean temperatures and the impact of coral bleaching on American Samoa's coral reefs and the communities that depend on them.

--- SENTENCE 4 ---
Ocean temperatures will rise across the region, with little potential for large refuge areas from warming.

--- SENTENCE 5 ---
There are, however, important differences in reefs' resilience; the ability of a reef to resist or recover from the impacts of warming and continue to provide ecosystem goods and services.

--- SENTENCE 6 ---
Our metrics of social vulnerability suggest that American Samoa's human populations may face challenges, especially from the population composition, poverty, and the structure of the labor force.

--- SENTENCE 7 ---
## Exposure

We report a coral reef sector's **Exposure** to warming as the projected year of onset of annual severe bleaching under the 'business-as-usual' emissions scenario RCP 8.5.

--- SENTENCE 8 ---
After this year, climate models suggest it is likely that high sea temperatures will trigger a severe coral bleaching event every summer.

--- SENTENCE 9 ---
Reefs are expected to have lower biodiversity after this point, and habitat quality will decline, reducing the ecosystem goods and services reefs can provide.

--- SENTENCE 10 ---
The onset of annual severe bleaching is projected for similar times across the region, with sector estimates ranging from 2035–2044, 16 to 25 years from today (2019).

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
If *Paris Accord* emissions reductions pledges become reality, corals will have 10–20 more years to adapt or acclimate to warming seas.

### Resilience

Reefs across American Samoa differ substantially in their **Resilience** to rising temperatures, both in their ability to resist temperatures and to recover from high temperature events.

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
The Resilience index combines 8 resilience factors and shows that the more isolated areas, including northeast Tutuila and the Manu'a group (see map, page 2), show higher resilience scores than the highly populated southwestern Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
The lightly populated Swains atoll shows low Resilience, likely due to low herbivorous fish biomass and high macroalgal cover.

### Social Vulnerability

The five distinct indices of **Social Vulnerability** vary across American Samoa and highlight that certain regions (e.g., Manu'a) have higher social vulnerability.

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
Where seen, high social vulnerability is largely driven by our metrics for Personal Disruption, which highlight factors relevant to the potential for unstable personal circumstances, and Labor Force Structure, which measures the strength and stability of the labor force.

# **Coral Reef Resilience and Social Vulnerability to Climate Change**

!

--- SENTENCE 26 ---
### *Coral reefs are one of our most important coastal ecosystems*.

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
They inspire us with their beauty, support diverse economies from fishing and tourism, protect our shorelines from storm waves, and house the greatest biological diversity of any marine habitat on earth.

--- SENTENCE 28 ---
*However, coral reefs are in trouble*.

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
They face local threats, such as overfishing and pollution, and increasingly face threats that lie outside of local control, such as globally warming temperatures and acidifying seas.

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
As temperatures rise, corals on reefs undergo bleaching, an acute condition that, if severe, can lead to large sections of coral reefs turning white and dying.

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
*Warming is most pressing global threat to reefs*.

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
The coral bleaching event of 2014–2016 was the third global coral bleaching event recorded, leading to extensive coral death across the globe.

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
American Samoa experienced coral bleaching each year between 2014 and 2018, with temperature rising to severe anomalies in 2014, 2015, and again in 2017.

--- SENTENCE 34 ---
Tracking the cumulative impacts of all these events will continue to be a focus of re-surveys efforts.

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
While this pattern of repeated, severe seasonal warming is anomalous, models of future temperatures suggest that this annual bleaching could become the new normal in as little as 20 years.

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
This grave situation raises a critical question:

How can citizens and reef managers respond locally to counter global threats to reefs?

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
*To manage reefs effectively, we need to know which areas are least vulnerable to climate change*.

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
Planning local responses to globally rising temperatures requires we understand: (1) *Exposure* — when will a given reef face potentially catastrophic warming?

--- SENTENCE 39 ---
(2) *Resilience* — how likely is a given reef to resist or recover from high temperature events?

--- SENTENCE 40 ---
(3) *Social Vulnerability* — how difficult will it be for the communities that depend on reefs to reduce their reliance on reef resources?

--- SENTENCE 41 ---
We present the best available data to answer these questions in a coherent way across American Samoa and all other US Affiliated Pacific Islands.

**Coral Reef Resilience RECOVERY**

## Coral competitiveness

*Coral Reef Resilience* is a function of both how likely a system is to *Resist* stress before showing negative impacts, and how likely a system is to rapidly *Recover* from impacts once they occur.

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
The eight reported resilience factors will each have different impacts on resistance and recovery.

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
To highlight these distinctions, we break the eight factors into three groups:

- ✧ *Coral Competitiveness* primarily describes a reef's *Recovery*,
- ✧ *Other Stressors* affect both *Resistance* and *Recovery*, and
- ✧ *Bleaching Resistance* primarily describes a reef's *Resistance*.

!

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
This aggregate index primarily describes a region's capacity for corals to compete for benthic space, and includes **Coral Diversity**, **Herbivorous Fish Biomass**, **Macroalgal Cover**, and **Juvenile Coral Density**.

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
A primary driver in maintaining resilient corals reefs is whether coral populations can maintain a competitive edge over other benthic species like turf algae and macroalgae.

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
High Coral Diversity supports this competiveness through a range of different growth forms and life histories.

--- SENTENCE 54 ---
High Herbivorous Fish Biomass supports high grazing rates on corals' primary competitors, turf algae and macroalgae.

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
Low Macroalgae Cover indicates that corals are maintaining this competive edge.

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
High Juvenile Coral Density also suggests that corals are managing to successfully recruit to the area, ensuring a continual supply of new corals to the population.

## Other stressors Bleaching resistance

!

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
This aggregate measure of **Bleaching Resistant Corals** and **Thermal Variability** highlights areas likely to better *resist* high water temperatures.

--- SENTENCE 67 ---
Factors that support recovery can be distinct from those that promote resistance to the stress in the first place.

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
Both the proportion of **Bleaching Resistant Corals** and a reef's history of high **Temperature Variablity** have been shown to help a reef resist high temperature bleaching.

# **Social Vulnerability**

While **Exposure** and **Resilience** play important roles in a coral reef's ecological responses to warming temperatures, human **Social Vulnerability** can indicate a community's ability to handle changes in their resources.

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
We present five aspects of Social Vulnerability of the communities living near these reef resources: **Personal Disruption, Population Composition, Poverty, Labor Force Structure,** and **Housing Characteristics** (following Jepson and Colburn 2013;

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
Kleiber et al. 2018).

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
In the descriptions below, the ↑ symbol indicates that an increase in a particular variable increases vulnerability, while ▶ indicates that the increase in the variable decreases vulnerability.

!

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
[](_page_5_Figure_2.jpeg)

The Personal Disruption index includes variables that might indicate unstable personal circumstances, including poverty  $(\uparrow)$ , unemployment  $(\uparrow)$ , and low educational attainment  $(\uparrow)$ .

!

--- SENTENCE 76 ---
The Poverty Index highlights the percentage of the total population in poverty  $(\uparrow)$ , as well as the percentage in poverty of other vulnerable groups, such as children (under 18)  $(\uparrow)$ , families with children under 5  $(\uparrow)$ , and single-female headed families  $(\uparrow)$ .

!

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
The Housing Characteristics index highlights the character of housing available within a community and measures the median rent  $(\Psi)$ , median number of rooms in family dwellings  $(\Psi)$ , and the percentage of houses that lack plumbing facilities  $(\uparrow)$ .

!

--- SENTENCE 78 ---
The Population Composition index describes demographic variables identified as indicators of socially vulnerable populations, including the percent of young children  $(^{\land})$ , the percent of female-headed households  $(^{\land})$ , and the percent of people without a Bachelor's degree  $(^{\land})$ .

!

--- SENTENCE 79 ---

#### ABSTRACT

Pala Lagoon accommodates the largest remaining stand of mangroves in the Territory of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
To provide current data on fish harvests in Pala Lagoon, a year-long study on the human use was initiated in 1991.

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
The results of the study showed that this predominantly subsistence/recreational fishery harvested a total of 23,800 lb of fish and invertebrates (excluding bivalve shells) from the lagoon.

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
The annual catch, when converted to its market value, was worth approximately \$66,000.

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
The catch was comprised of 45 species, or species groups, with fish contributing 62% to the total harvest and invertebrates contributing the remaining 38%.

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
Leading contributors to the catch were two species of bivalves (23%), mullet (22%), mangrove crabs (14%), and jacks (11%).

--- SENTENCE 22 ---
The results of this study were compared to a similar study conducted on the nearby coastal reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 23 ---
Nearly all of the species caught in the lagoon were also caught on the coastal reef.

--- SENTENCE 24 ---
The notable exceptions were the mud clam (<u>Gafrarium timidum</u>), and mangrove crabs (<u>Scylla serrata</u>), both of which rely on mangrove habitat for all or part of their life history.

--- SENTENCE 25 ---
Only approximately 50% of the fish caught on the reef were also caught in Pala Lagoon.

--- SENTENCE 26 ---
CPUE (catch per unit effort) in the lagoon ranged from 2.0 lb/gear-hour for throw netting to 0.02 lb/pot hour for crab pots.

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
Although CPUE was an order of magnitude lower than on the nearby coastal reef flat for most fishing methods, people still chose to fish Pala Lagoon, because 1) it was a traditional fishing ground of the families that reside adjacent to the lagoon, 2) Lion's Park provides easy public access to the lagoon for people from outside the area, and 3) the lagoon provides access to many of the species prized by the Samoan people in an area that is placid compared to the often treacherous reef flat and front.

#### INTRODUCTION

--- SENTENCE 28 ---
The importance of mangroves as a nutrient source for fishery resources has been well documented in tropical and subtropical regions throughout the world (Lal et al. 1984, Davie 1985).

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
Mangrove lagoons are also widely accepted as critical nursery habitat for a variety of coastal reef species.

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
An estimated 80% of all marine species of commercial or recreational importance in Florida are dependent on mangrove estuarine areas for at least some stage of their life cycles.

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
In Fiji, approximately 60% of the entire commercial catch is composed of species dependent upon mangrove estuarine areas (Hamilton and Snedaker 1984).

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
In spite of this, mangrove habitat worldwide is being lost at an alarming rate.

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
Pala Lagoon in Nu'uuli, the largest enclosed bay in American Samoa, has the largest remaining stand of mangroves (123 acres) left in the Territory.

--- SENTENCE 34 ---
However, that wetland is being lost at a rate of over 2 acres/year due to illegal filling (BioSystems Analysis, Inc. 1992).

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
In addition to acreage lost to illicite activities, the lagoon has been the subject of several feasibility and impact studies for aquaculture and development projects, some of which revealed that proposed developments would have detrimental effects on the lagoon habitat.

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
It is important that decisions with respect to the wetland habitat of Pala Lagoon be made with as complete an understanding as possible of the value of the area in its present state.

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
The present study was designed to quantify the human use of the Pala Lagoon in terms of fishery activities and harvests.

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
Catch and effort data were collected over a 1-yr period to determine annual harvests of fish and shellfish.

--- SENTENCE 39 ---
Sociological data were also collected to provide a profile of the fishery users.

--- SENTENCE 40 ---
In addition to the present study of fish harvests in Pala Lagoon, a complimentary study of the lagoon's fish resources was conducted by Knudsen (1992).

--- SENTENCE 41 ---
Unfortunately, the results of that study were not yet available when the present study was written.

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
# Study Area

Pala lagoon is a large, protected mangrove lagoon on the southern coastline of Tutuila Island (Fig 1).

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
It is roughly circular, approximately 1 mile in diameter and has a surface area of about 1 square mile.

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
The study area consisted of the entire lagoon to a line drawn from the end of the airport runway to the southeastern tip of Coconut Point.

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
The lagoon is bordered on the north shore by a mud-bottomed swamp vegetated with red (Rhizophora mangle) and oriental (Bruguiera gymnorrhiza) mangroves.

--- SENTENCE 46 ---
Coconut Point, the sandy peninsula separating the lagoon from the coastal waters and forming the eastern shore, is vegetated with mangroves interspersed with houses.

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
Lion's Park, a public park, established on landfill, borders the western side.

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
The runway of the Pago Pago International Airport, also built on fill, makes up the southern shoreline.

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
A 0.25-mile gap between the tip of Coconut and the runway provides entrance to the lagoon for coastal waters.

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
The lagoon is shallow, with depths generally ranging from 1 to 5 feet.

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
The area adjacent to the airport has been extensively dredged for fill material, leaving a series of borrow pits up to 10 feet deep in that area.

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
Bottom substrate is organic mud, gradually shifting to silty sand from the north to south shores.

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
Overlying waters are generally turbid, and turbidity levels appear to vary with rainfall and tidal magnitude.

#### METHODS

To estimate the amount of fishing effort and resultant catch for Pala Lagoon, data were collected over a 1-year period, January to December 1991.

--- SENTENCE 61 ---
The study was designed was to systematically subsample the total time in the year, and to expand this subsample estimate the entire year's catch and effort.

--- SENTENCE 62 ---
# Field Sampling

Sampling was conducted in 8-hr shifts which were stratified to sample the 24-hour-a-day, 7-day-a-week fishery.

--- SENTENCE 63 ---
Typically, two sampling shifts were scheduled per week, during which a census was made of all fishing and swimming activity within the study area.

--- SENTENCE 64 ---
At the beginning of a shift, a thorough search of the study area for fishing trips in progress and for passive gear such as gill nets and crab pots was conducted.

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
After that, the lagoon was monitored from a vantage point at the south end of Lion's Park from which most of the lagoon could seen.

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
The lagoon circumnavigated by kayak once every 1 to 2 hours to account for activity in areas which were hard to see from there.

--- SENTENCE 67 ---
As each trip began, the start time, fishing method employed, number of gear units (e.g. number fishing rods, throw nets, number and length of gillnets etc.), number of people, and location within the study area were recorded.

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
End times were recorded as each fishing trip was completed.

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
Only the portion of the trip which occurred during the sampling shift was recorded for trips that were in progress at the onset and/or end of the shift.

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
As the study was monitored for the movement of fishing parties into and out of the lagoon, catch data were gathered from as many of the fishing parties as was physically possible, which ranged from 50 to 100% of the activity occurring during the shift.

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
Ideally, interviews were conducted as the trip ended so that it would represent the entire extent of the fishing effort.

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
However, some interviews were conducted while the trip was in progress to

maximize the number of interviews conducted.

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
In such a case, the fishing party was sampled as far into the trip as possible, and no interviews were conducted on trips that were shorter than half an hour.

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
Data recorded for each catch interview included start time, interview time and elapsed fishing time for the trip, fishing method, gear units, number of people by age and sex category (male adult, female adult, male child, female child), location within the study area, home village of the participants, and whether the catch was to be sold or kept.

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
The catch was then sorted by species or species group, and weighed on a spring scale to the nearest ounce.

--- SENTENCE 76 ---
Occasionally, a fishing party was sampled that could not wait for the catch to be sorted by species.

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
Rather than discarding those data, the catch was weighed in aggregate and recorded as mixed fish.

--- SENTENCE 78 ---
### Data Analysis

To clarify some of the analysis procedures, it is necessary to first introduce some of the assumptions, conventions and units of measurement used in the analysis.

--- SENTENCE 79 ---
It has been observed that levels of fishing effort differ between day and night, and between weekends and weekdays (Wass 1980, Ponwith 1991).

--- SENTENCE 80 ---
To allow comparisons to be made among these temporal groupings, data were stratified into four time periods, hereafter referred to as time strata: weekday day (Sunday-Friday 0600-1800), weekday night (Sunday-Thursday 1800-0600), weekend day (Saturday and non-religious holidays 0600-1800), and weekend nights (Friday, Saturday and non-religious holidays 1800-0600).

--- SENTENCE 81 ---
Sundays were treated as weekdays because effort levels more closely resembled that of a weekday than a Saturday.

--- SENTENCE 82 ---
Total estimates for catch and effort are therefore the sum of the weighted estimates for each strata.

--- SENTENCE 83 ---
Fishing methods used in Pala Lagoon were split into two categories:
1) active methods, where the participant worked the gear throughout
the extent of the trip (rod and reel, bamboo pole, handline,
clamming, diving, throw net, active gill net and crab lift net) and
2) passive methods, where the gear was set, left to fish for a
period of time and then collected (gill net and crab pot).

--- SENTENCE 84 ---
Effort was expressed as lb/gear-hour, which differs from the more commonly used unit of lb/person-hour.

--- SENTENCE 85 ---
In Samoa, it is common for a fishing party to include people who are not actively harvesting fish.

--- SENTENCE 86 ---
For example, one person may fish with a throw net or rod and reel while another merely holds the bag containing the catch.

--- SENTENCE 87 ---
In such a case the lb/gear-hour unit more closely describes the fishing power for that trip than pounds per person-hour.

--- SENTENCE 88 ---
One exception was made in the case of active gill net fishing.

--- SENTENCE 89 ---
In this method, a gill net is set in a deep semi-circle and a number

of people approach the open side of the semi-circle while flailing the water with palm fronds, sticks, or their arms, in an attempt to drive fish into the net.

--- SENTENCE 90 ---
While the length of the net may have had some influence on the catch rate for this method, it seemed that the number of people pounding the water in front of the net had a greater influence.

--- SENTENCE 91 ---
Therefore, effort for this method was expressed in terms of the number of people participating, i.e. lb/person-hour.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
Because the length of the net greatly influenced catch levels, passive gill net effort was expressed as lb/100 foot-hour.

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
Clamming was a popular harvest method used to collect two species of clams from two different areas in the lagoon.

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
Rather than risk sampling the two types of clamming disproportionately (which would bias the species composition of the catch) clamming was split into two fishing methods based on the target species.

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
Four main steps were required to expand the sample data to represent the entire fishery: 1) participation expansion, 2) CPUE calculation, 3) catch expansion, and 4) species composition calculation.

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
The first step was to expand the participation data to represent all hours in the year-long study period.

--- SENTENCE 97 ---
To accomplish this, the total amount of participation observed was first divided by the number of hours of observation to calculate the mean amount of effort per hour for each of the four time strata by fishing method.

--- SENTENCE 98 ---
The expansion was made by multiplying these mean values by the respective total number of hours within each of the time strata in the entire year.

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
Recreational swimming was treated as method so observed person-hours of swimming could also be expanded to an annual estimate.

--- SENTENCE 100 ---
A preliminary analysis of participation data after 6 months of data collection showed that very little fishing effort took place on weeknights after 2200 hours.

--- SENTENCE 101 ---
A decision was made to shift sampling effort away from that time period to increase sampling efficiency.

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
Effort estimates for the period between 2200 and 0600 hours on weekday nights were made by multiplying the mean amount of effort per hour based on the data from the first six months by the total number of hours between those times in the entire year.

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
Next, CPUE values were calculated for each method by two time strata, day and night.

--- SENTENCE 104 ---
Separate CPUE values for weekday and weekend time periods were not calculated because an assumption was made that CPUE was the same on weekdays as on weekends.

--- SENTENCE 105 ---
The sum of the number of pounds caught was divided by the sum of the effort for each fishing method to give a mean CPUE value for each method by day and by night.

--- SENTENCE 106 ---
In the third step, catch expansions were produced by multiplying the expanded effort values by their respective CPUE values to get expanded pounds landed.

--- SENTENCE 107 ---
The last step was to break the expanded catch down into species composition estimates.

--- SENTENCE 108 ---
Percent contribution for each species or species group in the observed catch was calculated for each fishing method by day and by night.

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
The observed proportions were then expanded by multiplying them by the expanded catch for each strata.

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
Similar expansions of the raw data for the sociological data collected were not made because it would have resulted in an unwieldy number of strata.

--- SENTENCE 111 ---
However, since interviews were collected randomly, the raw (unexpanded) data should be a fair representation of the fishing population.

--- SENTENCE 112 ---
From the raw data, the age and sex composition of the participants, percent of the catch sold, and percent of the participants from villages adjacent to the Pala Lagoon were calculated.

### RESULTS

### Fishery Profile

A variety of fish and invertebrate species were harvested from the Pala Lagoon using several different fishing methods, which often were used in distinct areas of the lagoon (Fig. 2).

--- SENTENCE 114 ---
Three hookand-line methods were used (rod and reel, handline, and bamboo pole) to catch fish along the lagoon shoreline, primarily along Lion's Park, the runway and at the tip of Coconut Point.

--- SENTENCE 115 ---
The rare fishing party that fished from a boat, fished the area between the runway and Coconut Point almost exclusively.

--- SENTENCE 116 ---
Clamming was a popular fishing method.

--- SENTENCE 117 ---
In virtually all cases, the target species was either tugane (Gafrarium timidum) or pipi (Asaphis deflorata).

--- SENTENCE 118 ---
Clamming for tugane took place primarily in the muddy substrate along the north shore of the lagoon but also along the northern end of Coconut Point in both sand and mud substrates.

--- SENTENCE 119 ---
Clam were harvested from exposed sandy areas by probing suspected syphon holes with a knife.

--- SENTENCE 120 ---
The mud, being too soft to show the syphon marks of the clams, was searched for clams Most clams were harvested while the mud was with bare feet.

--- SENTENCE 121 ---
exposed at low tide, although some people preferred to avoid the difficult walk through the mud by clamming at mid to high tide.

--- SENTENCE 122 ---
Their strategy was to float above the clam beds while feeling for the clams with bare feet and reaching down to collect them once located.

--- SENTENCE 123 ---
Size measurements were not taken on individual clams.

--- SENTENCE 124 ---
However, when time allowed, both the count and weight of the catches were recorded, allowing for a rough estimate of average weight to be made.

--- SENTENCE 125 ---
On the average, tugane ran 11 clams to the pound, including shell weight.

--- SENTENCE 126 ---
Clamming for pipi took place mainly at low tide in sandy areas along the south end of Lions Park and along the runway.

--- SENTENCE 131 ---
Three fishing methods involved the use of nets.

--- SENTENCE 132 ---
Gill nets ranging from 50 to 300 feet in length, with stretched mesh ranging from 1.5 to 4.5 inches, were used in one of two ways.

--- SENTENCE 133 ---
The more conventional use was as passive gear, where the net was set by anchoring it to the bottom and then checked intermittently for fish.

--- SENTENCE 134 ---
This method was used throughout the lagoon.

--- SENTENCE 135 ---
Gill nets were also used as active gear as was described earlier.

--- SENTENCE 136 ---
The number of people in an active gill net fishing party ranged from 3 to 8 and these groups would cover large areas of the lagoon, moving to a new area for each set.

--- SENTENCE 137 ---
The other net method, throw netting, employed a circular net ranging from 5 to 8 feet in diameter, which was weighted around the perimeter and had a mesh size of 0.5 to 3 inches.

--- SENTENCE 138 ---
The net was thrown out and then retrieved by a line secured at its center.

--- SENTENCE 139 ---
Most throw netting was done from the shoreline around the perimeter of the study area although some individuals waded or boated into the center of the lagoon to fish that area as well.

--- SENTENCE 140 ---
Because of the lagoon's turbidity, only a limited amount of spear diving was observed within the study area.

--- SENTENCE 141 ---
Divers were generally equipped with swimming goggles as eye protection and either a 3-pronged spear or a steel rod with which to spear fish.

--- SENTENCE 142 ---
All of the dive activity was observed in the area between the runway and the end of Coconut Point where the water was clearer and coral cover was higher than in the inner region.

--- SENTENCE 143 ---
Two methods were used to harvest mangrove crabs from the lagoon.

--- SENTENCE 144 ---
Crab pots, made from rat wire which was sewn in the shape of a pillow ranging from 2xl ft to 3x2 ft in size, were used as passive gear (i.e., deployed and retrieved later).

--- SENTENCE 145 ---
These were baited with partially decayed crabs hung in a corner of the trap.

--- SENTENCE 146 ---
Crabs gained entrance through a hole, roughly 6 inches square, in the top panel of the pot.

--- SENTENCE 147 ---
A collar around the hole, which extended nearly to the bottom panel of the pot enhanced retention of the crabs once they entered the pot, and a length of line extending from the pot to a small float served as a means of relocating the pot once deployed.

--- SENTENCE 148 ---
Crab pots were deployed using paopaos (outrigger canoes), several of which were va'a apa (boats made from a sheet of tin roofing material folded in half lengthwise and sealed at the ends with lengths of 2x4 lumber and tar, to which an outrigger was attached).

--- SENTENCE 149 ---
The number of individuals who participated in crab pot fishing throughout the year ranged from 2 to 6.

--- SENTENCE 150 ---
The number of pots used by a fisherman depended on the size of his pots and boat.

--- SENTENCE 151 ---
The number of pots fished ranged from 4 to 13, with 10 being about the average.

--- SENTENCE 152 ---
The north shore area along the margin of the mangrove stands was fished the heaviest for crabs, but pots were deployed in the open water offshore of the mangroves as well.

--- SENTENCE 153 ---
Generally, pots were checked between 0600 and 0700 and were pulled if they needed fresh bait.

--- SENTENCE 154 ---
Pots were redeployed anywhere from 1400 to 1800 depending on the tide.

--- SENTENCE 155 ---
Active crab fishing also employed handmade lift nets quite different in design from the passive gear.

--- SENTENCE 156 ---
Purse seine netting was attached to the perimeter of metal band approximately 1 ft in diameter and 4 in high so the netting hung like a basket below the band.

--- SENTENCE 157 ---
A 3 to 5 ft stick was suspended just above the level of the lower edge of the band by heavy gauge wire which was secured to the top of the band.

--- SENTENCE 158 ---
At the lower end of the stick, a bait bag made of screen material and containing small, partially decayed crabs was hung.

--- SENTENCE 159 ---
Crab fishermen loaded anywhere from 5 to 12 of these lift nets onto a paopao and deployed them within and along the edges of the mangrove stands.

--- SENTENCE 160 ---
The fisherman then paddled among the lift nets, watching for movement at the top of the stick, an indication that a crab was tugging at the bait on the lower end.

--- SENTENCE 161 ---
When motion was observed, the fisherman paddled to the net and lifted it abruptly from the water, causing the crab to drop into the basket below the level of the metal band.

--- SENTENCE 162 ---
The crab was then dumped into the boat and the claws tied to its body with strands of inner tube rubber.

### Catch, Effort and CPUE

Estimates of catch, effort and CPUE were based on 565 hours of observation which equates to 6% of all hours in the 1-year study.

--- SENTENCE 163 ---
Sampling rates among the time strata ranged from 3% for weekday nights to 15% for weekend days.

--- SENTENCE 164 ---
At total of 207 catch interviews were conducted which represented 849 gear-hours of active fishing effort, 3999 pot-hours of crab pot effort and 1220 100'-hours of passive gill net effort.

--- SENTENCE 165 ---
The 1991 annual harvest of fish and shellfish from Pala Lagoon was 23,800 lb without clam shells (Table 1) or 33,900 lb with the shells.

--- SENTENCE 166 ---
Fish weights are expressed as whole fish.

--- SENTENCE 167 ---
Clam shell weights were not weighed, but assumed to be 0.7 of the tugane total weight and 0.5 of the pipi weight.

--- SENTENCE 168 ---
Hereafter, all bivalve shell weights will be excluded from all totals unless otherwise noted.

--- SENTENCE 169 ---
Catch and effort levels varied among the different fishing methods.

--- SENTENCE 170 ---
The passive gill netting method had the highest contribution to total catch (Fig. 3), and the combined contribution of the two gill net methods, active and passive was 39% of the total catch.

--- SENTENCE 171 ---
Clamming had the second highest contribution to catch, followed by throw net, crab pot, active gill net methods.

## Species Composition

In total, 45 species or species groups were represented in the catch in Pala Lagoon (Table 3).

--- SENTENCE 197 ---
Fish contributed 62% to the total catch of fish and invertebrates combined.

--- SENTENCE 198 ---
Fish from the family mugilidae (mullets) comprised the greatest portion of the fish catch (18%), and carangids (jacks) were the only other species group that contributed greater than 10% to the total fish catch (Table 4).

--- SENTENCE 199 ---
The fish catch was spread among numerous species with no major contributor (Table 3).

--- SENTENCE 200 ---
Of the fish that were identified to species, the bluespot mullet (Valamugil seheli) was the only species to contribute more than 10% to the total fish catch.

--- SENTENCE 201 ---
Invertebrates, mainly clams and crabs, comprised 38% of the total catch (Table 3).

--- SENTENCE 285 ---
People using the other methods fished in those areas as well, but were not as restricted to them by the distribution of their desired species.

--- SENTENCE 286 ---

--- SENTENCE 287 ---
The crab pot fishing method is clearly a commercial enterprise.

--- SENTENCE 288 ---

Clamming for pipi and tugane had the next highest proportions of the catch sold.

--- SENTENCE 293 ---

--- SENTENCE 296 ---
Only two methods which harvest fish, active gill net and throw net, had a portion of their total catch sold.

#### DISCUSSION

--- SENTENCE 323 ---
Prior to the present study and that by Knudsen (1992), there was little quantitative information on the fishery or fishery resources in Pala Lagoon available.

--- SENTENCE 324 ---
However, comparisons of the results from this study with anecdotal information, historical literature, and with the shoreline fishery on the nearby reef habitat (Ponwith 1991) provide a means to examine changes or trends in the fishery.

## Comparison with Historical Information

٠,٠٠٠ ا

The only historical information available on the fishery in Pala Lagoon was based the results of a voluntary questionnaire sent out to 150 residents of the lagoon area in 1985 (Yamasaki et al. 1985).

--- SENTENCE 325 ---
Unfortunately, the catch and effort estimates from that study are not directly comparable to those of this study due to the differences in methodology (questionnaire versus creel survey).

--- SENTENCE 326 ---
The results compiled from the 28 responses to the 1985 survey showed that an average of 22 fishing trips per day were made by members of families who lived in villages adjacent to the lagoon.

--- SENTENCE 327 ---
Presently, average of approximately 22 trips per day on weekends and 15 trips per day weekdays are made, but that includes both residents of the area and people from outside the area.

--- SENTENCE 328 ---
Some changes in species composition of the harvests could be detected by comparing the two studies.

--- SENTENCE 329 ---
Of the species listed by Yamasaki et al. (1985) as having been important in the fishery in 1985, 5 were either missing or were extremely rare in the current catches.

--- SENTENCE 330 ---
Jelly fish (<u>Cassiopeia</u> sp.) were commonly caught in 1985

none was seen or harvested in 1991.

--- SENTENCE 331 ---
To verify this change, two local individuals were questioned regarding this Samoan delicacy.

--- SENTENCE 332 ---
Both agreed that jelly fish had been harvested in great quantities in times past but that they were no longer present in any great abundance.

--- SENTENCE 333 ---
A major decline in the harvest of the sandworm (<u>Sipunculus rotumanus</u>) also occurred.

--- SENTENCE 334 ---
No catches were sampled which contained the worm in 1991.

--- SENTENCE 335 ---
Harvests of sandworms in Western Samoa have apparently also declined (L. Zann, pers.

--- SENTENCE 336 ---
A third species, the mantis shrimp, was extremely rare in the 1991 catch, but was formerly common in the lagoon (Helfrich 1975, Yamasaki 1985).

--- SENTENCE 337 ---
Two other species, rays and silversides were previously present in unknown levels in 1985 catches, but were absent in the 1991 catch.

--- SENTENCE 338 ---
However, rays were occasionally observed in the lagoon during the present study.

--- SENTENCE 339 ---
According to Yamasaki et al. (1985), the general impression of fishery participants in 1985 was that the fishery in the lagoon was in a state of decline.

--- SENTENCE 340 ---
That is still true today.

--- SENTENCE 341 ---
The consensus among present-day participants is that catch, CPUE and species diversity have declined from their earlier fishing days in the lagoon.

## Comparison with Adjacent Coastal Reef

During approximately the same time period that this study was conducted, another similar study was conducted on the coastal coral reefs adjacent to Pala Lagoon (Ponwith 1991).

--- SENTENCE 342 ---
It is therefore of interest to compare the harvests from the lagoon with that of the adjacent coral reefs.

--- SENTENCE 343 ---
The shoreline fishery exploited reef fish and invertebrates from the reef flat and reef front zones of the narrow fringing reef which surrounds the island.

--- SENTENCE 344 ---
The habitat differences between the two study areas were pronounced.

--- SENTENCE 345 ---
The reef habitat was generally comprised of consolidated coral, coral rubble, sand and live coral substrate in very clear water with good flushing rates.

--- SENTENCE 346 ---
On the other hand, lagoon habitat was characterized by organic mud and sandy substrates with a limited area of live coral, eutrophic waters with poor flushing rates and high temperatures.

--- SENTENCE 347 ---
The two fisheries shared some common features: 1) multiple methods were used to harvest an array of fish and invertebrate species, 2) both fisheries operated 24 hours a day, 7 days a week with fishing activity proportionally highest on weekends, 3) roughly 25% of the catch from both fisheries was sold, and 4) juvenile fishes were common in catches in both areas.

--- SENTENCE 348 ---
**CPUE**

There were some distinct differences between the two fisheries, most notably the difference in CPUE (Table 6).

--- SENTENCE 349 ---
For most methods, CPUE in the mangrove lagoon habitat was at least an order of magnitude lower than of the nearby coral reef areas.

--- SENTENCE 350 ---
A factor which heavily influenced CPUE, which was based on the weight of the catch) was that much of the lagoon catch was comprised of juveniles.

--- SENTENCE 351 ---
As is true of the shoreline fishery in American Samoa, people who harvested fish and invertebrates in Pala Lagoon were totally indiscriminant with respect to the size and species of fish retained.

--- SENTENCE 352 ---
Not one case of a fish being returned to the water as undesirable with respect to size or species wasrecorded during the year-long study period, and only two cases of juvenile mud crabs (Scylla serrata) being thrown back were observed.

--- SENTENCE 353 ---
# Species Diversity and Composition

Species diversity was lower in the lagoon catch than in the reef catch.

--- SENTENCE 354 ---
Accounting as closely as possible for the number of species which were lumped into species groups in both the studies, approximately 55 species of fish were observed in the Pala Lagoon catch compared to roughly 110 species from the reef catch.

--- SENTENCE 355 ---
Likewise, around 10 species of invertebrates were identified in the lagoon catch versus approximately 20 from the reef catch.

--- SENTENCE 356 ---
Catch composition by family was compared between the two areas.

--- SENTENCE 357 ---
First, the seasonal migrant, atule (<u>Selar crumenophthalmus</u>), which accounted for 46% of the total fish catch from the reef area, was subtracted from the catch to allow the contribution of habitatresident fish to be compared.

--- SENTENCE 358 ---
The adjusted contributions showed mullet and jack species to be top contributors to both fisheries, accounting for 54% and 39% of the non-atule catch in the lagoon and reef areas, respectively (Table 7).

--- SENTENCE 359 ---
The mullet family was the top contributor in the lagoon while carangids were top contributors on the reef.

--- SENTENCE 360 ---
Landings of acanthurids (surgeonfish), were notably lower in the lagoon (3% compared to 21%) as would be expected because acanthurids generally inhabit coral reef areas, and live coral is limited to the area near the mouth of the lagoon.

--- SENTENCE 361 ---
The remaining catch was distributed among numerous other families, all of which contributed less than 10% to the total catch in either of the areas.

# Importance and Value of the Fishery

With catch, CPUE, and yield being so much lower in the lagoon than that of the nearby reef area fishery, the question arises as to why people would choose to fish in Pala Lagoon rather than on the nearby reef.

--- SENTENCE 377 ---
Three possible explanations to that question are based on conversations with people who fish in the lagoon.

--- SENTENCE 378 ---
First, people who are residents of the area, especially those who live directly adjacent to the lagoon, fish there because that is their traditional fishing ground.

--- SENTENCE 379 ---
Second, those who are not from the lagoon fish there because of the public access provided by Lion's Park.

--- SENTENCE 380 ---
The third reason is a safety factor; the lagoon is not subject to the high surf and currents common to the reef flat areas, making it a safer place to fish for both the participants and their gear relative to the reef flats along the open ocean.

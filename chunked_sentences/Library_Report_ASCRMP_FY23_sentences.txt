#### <span id="page-3-0"></span>*Introduction*

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
The American Samoa Coral Reef Monitoring Program (ASCRMP) has two main goals: (1) to track the distribution and abundance of reef organisms in American Samoa and (2) to supply pertinent information to environmental managers and decision-makers for the management of coral reefs in the Territory.

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
The primary aim of ASCRMP is to record changes occurring at monitoring locations throughout the Territory, which include variations in species composition, population sizes, and the physical structure of the reefs.

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
By documenting these changes, the program seeks to improve the understanding of reef ecosystem dynamics and the underlying factors influencing them.

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
The long-term monitoring program by the American Samoan Governor's Coral Reef Advisory Group (CRAG) aims to address management questions at three spatial scales: site level, primary reef type level, and island level.

--- SENTENCE 15 ---
Previous research by Houk et al. (2013) identified significant biological differences in benthic, coral, and fish assemblages between TutuilA's north and south sides, influenced by environmental factors such as wave energy and geology.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
Methods have been refined to maintain comparability with island-wide surveys conducted from 2003 to 2016, enhancing the understanding of spatial and temporal trends in coral and fish assemblages by integrating past and modern data.

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
CRAG selected eleven sites around Tutuila Island for long-term monitoring, representing various reef types and incorporating federal, Territory, and community-based Marine Protected Areas (MPAs).

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
Among these, three sites (Sailele, Fagasa, and Fagaalu (CFMP updated since 2024) are part of community-based MPAs under the Department of Marine and Wildlife Resources' Community-based Fisheries Management Program.

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
In comparison, two sites (Fagatele and Tafeu) are designated as federal MPAs.

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
The selection process for monitoring sites considered both watershed sizes and human usage.

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) categorizes watersheds into four classes: "pristine," "minimal," "intermediate," and "extensive" (DiDonato, 2004).

--- SENTENCE 22 ---
Among the 11 monitoring sites, three are classified as "extensive" (Leone, Nu'uuli, and Faga'alu), three as "intermediate" (Amaua, Aoa, and Fagasa), one as "medium" (Vatia), and three as "pristine" (Fagatele, Tafeu, and Fagamalo).

--- SENTENCE 23 ---
In 2007, an additional site, A'asu (Massacre Bay), was introduced to improve the geographical balance between the north-south and east-west orientations, ensuring even representation across the four quadrants of Tutuila (NW, NE, SE, and SW).

--- SENTENCE 24 ---
Since 2017, standardized methods have been utilized at these sites to monitor benthic substrates, coral, and fish, allowing for the detection of changes in coral reef health over time.

--- SENTENCE 25 ---
This monitoring builds on data from earlier programs that started in 2003 and has led to numerous publications that illustrate the connections between watersheds and reefs while addressing the impacts of climate change.

### <span id="page-3-1"></span>*Climate Change*

Changes in composition and resilience of the coral reef ecosystem due to disturbance are observed globally.

--- SENTENCE 26 ---
In American Samoa, reef recovery is site specific with some sites being dominated by stress-tolerant coral species (Graham et al., 2020), which can modify the reef structure.

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
Several recent climate-change-associated heat-stress events have occurred in American Samoa (Figure 1).

--- SENTENCE 28 ---
The first significant heat-stress event was observed in Tutuila in 2015.

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
Degree Heating Weeks (DHW) indicates accumulated heat stress and acts as a metric for assessing thermal stress in the reef environment.

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
The first event was characterized by heat stress exceeding six degree heating weeks (DHW), or a threshold considered to be associated with mild-to-moderate bleaching.

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
Subsequently, in 2017, a significant event occurred with DHW near eight, which is the threshold for major bleaching.

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
In 2020, a DHW of 7.5 was reported.

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
None of those events had been extreme.

--- SENTENCE 34 ---
However, since November/December 2023, along with the warmer season and El Nino event, high record temperatures have been registered in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
By April 2024, we had reached an Alert Level 2 heat stress with 8-degree heating weeks (DHW), or a threshold considered to be associated with bleaching and mortality likely (Figure 2).

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
With the longest cumulative heat stress on record for the archipelago, NOAA confirmed the fourth global mass coral bleaching event, marking the second such event in the past decade.

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
It is unknown how sequential moderate DHW events may impact reefs.

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
Within this report, we attribute the rise in DHW intensity to variations in coral cover and the ratio of calcifying-to-uncalcifying benthic substrates, trends that are furthered within.

#### <span id="page-8-0"></span>*Benthic Surveys*

Until 2014, the line-point intercept method (LPI) was utilized to assess benthic cover.

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
Four 50 m transects were laid out along a depth contour of 8-10 m, and benthic categories were recorded every 50 cm (100 data points/transect).

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
In addition, a roving dive was conducted to record species diversity recorded on the DACOR scale.

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
For more information on these methods, see reports from 2005 to 2013 compiled by Doug Fenner.

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
In 2015, the methods were altered to increase data quality, add additional data, and make it comparable to data collected by other monitoring programs.

--- SENTENCE 76 ---
The LPI method was changed to photo quadrats to increase the data points collected and decrease the time spent collecting data.

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
Additionally, coral demographic data is collected along the transect to make inferences about size distribution and recruitment.

--- SENTENCE 78 ---
Two 100-meter tapes are placed end to end along the 30-foot depth contour on the reef slope, divided into six sections, or considered as six replicate transects (0-25m, 30-55m, 60-85m, 90-115m, 120-145m, 150-175m).

--- SENTENCE 79 ---
Each of the six sections is marked with a rebar at the start (0, 30, 60, 90, 120, and 150m).

--- SENTENCE 80 ---
Benthic photos are taken every meter using a 1 m2 monopod (26 photos per section; 156 photos per site).

--- SENTENCE 81 ---
These photos are currently analyzed with CoralNet, a web-based platform using machine learning for coral reef image classification, replacing the previous software used for photo analyses called Coral Reef Point Count or CPCe.

#### <span id="page-9-0"></span>*Coral Surveys*

Coral surveys have been conducted opportunistically when taxonomic expertise has existed within the broader ASCRAG network.

--- SENTENCE 82 ---
While no taxonomic expertise currently exists, we briefly mention protocols.

--- SENTENCE 83 ---
A 1 x 1 m quadrat is randomly placed on the reef every 20m (10 per site).

--- SENTENCE 84 ---
Each coral colony within the quadrat is identified by genus, growth form assigned, and its largest diameter and width measured.

#### <span id="page-9-1"></span>*Coral Bleaching Monitoring*

Coral bleaching monitoring is conducted annually in October /

--- SENTENCE 85 ---
November before the warmer season to survey reefs before a potential bleaching event.

--- SENTENCE 86 ---
Survey sites include twelve reef slope sites (five of which overlap with existing ASCRMP sites), reef flat sites (seven in Tutuila:

--- SENTENCE 87 ---
Sailele, Auto, Onesosopo, Utulei, Gataivai, Nuuli, Amanave and four in Manu'a), and three lagoon pools (Fagaalu, Airport pools and Alofao).

--- SENTENCE 88 ---
At each location, two HOBO® Pendant temperature loggers (www.onsetcomp.com) are retrieved from the previous year and replaced with new loggers.

--- SENTENCE 89 ---
In the past, loggers have been reused by replacing batteries.

--- SENTENCE 90 ---
However, there was a high failure rate and data loss.

--- SENTENCE 91 ---
During 2019, 75% of the new HOBO loggers deployed that were retrieved at the end of the year were flooded, and therefore data was unable to be downloaded.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
As a result, it was decided to upgrade the HOBO devices to a more robust version.

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
It is slightly more costly but is also used by the National Park of American Samoa's marine team to collect water temperature data at their monitoring sites.

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
This will ensure a more robust data collection method and allow more effective calibration and comparison between CRAG and NPSA datasets.

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
The approximate depth range of the reef slopes is 30 ft.

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
Two 100 m tapes were laid out end to end on the 30 ft depth contour along the reef slope consisting of six individual transects (0-25 m, 30-55m, 60-85m, 90-115m, 120-145m, 150-175m).

--- SENTENCE 97 ---
The approximate depth range of the reef flats is 3ft-5ft depth and in the pools is 6ft-13ft; Three 25 m transects were laid out parallel to each other with a 10-20 m distance between them.

--- SENTENCE 98 ---
On the slope, the beginning of each transect is permanently marked with a rebar (six rebars total at 0, 30, 60, 90, 120, and 150m).

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
In pools and on reef flats, each transect's beginning and end are marked with a rebar (six rebars total, two rebars/transect at 0 and 25m).

--- SENTENCE 100 ---
For each transect, benthic photos are taken every meter using a 1m long monopod on slopes and a 0.75m monopod on reef flats and in pools (26 photos per transect; a total of 156 images per site on reef slopes and a total of 78 photos per site on reef flats).

--- SENTENCE 101 ---
These photos are then analyzed using CPCe and Coral Net software to obtain coral cover estimates for the site.

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
The coral cover estimates can then be compared to cover data after a bleaching event to assess the impact on the reef assemblage.

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
Two temperature loggers are installed at each site near two stakes using zip ties.

--- SENTENCE 104 ---
The logger (length 5 cm) is attached to an area of the reef (solid rock) away from living coral.

--- SENTENCE 105 ---
Although not covered by this agreement, during a bleaching event, these additional survey steps would be undertaken as per the "American Samoa Coral Bleaching Response Protocol" (see Table 2): Boat snorkel tow surveys will be conducted to assess the spatial extent and identify critical areas for more detailed surveys.

--- SENTENCE 106 ---
At critical sites, a belt survey will be conducted to obtain data on the percentage of bleached coral and how different genera are affected.

--- SENTENCE 107 ---
Every coral colony within a 1 m wide belt along each transect will be identified to genus and assigned a bleaching severity ranging from 0-4 (0 = no bleaching; 1 = partially bleached; 2 = white; 3=bleached and partly dead; 4 = recently dead; see Marshall & Schuttenberg 2006).

--- SENTENCE 108 ---
In addition, both divers will estimate the general proportion of coral affected by

bleaching for the reef as a whole using a scale from 0-4 (0 = <1% - no bleaching; 1 = 1-10% - mild bleaching; 2=10-50% - moderate bleaching; 3 = 50-90% - high bleaching; 4= >90% - extreme bleaching; see Marshall & Schuttenberg 2006).

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
Additionally, aerial images from the reef flats in different priority sites around the Island have been captured using a drone.

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
In 2015, the program staff initiated snorkel baseline monitoring surveys on the shallow reef flats (3ft-5ft) and pools on Ofu and Olosega islands to provide information on temporal and spatial trends within the islands.

--- SENTENCE 111 ---
Rose and Swains atolls are monitored when conditions and funds allow.

--- SENTENCE 112 ---
Different survey schedules are recommended depending on the event classification, habitat types, and available resources.

--- SENTENCE 113 ---
The survey methods (Figure 4) should be organized in a tiered layout, starting with the basic large-scale survey and developing progressively into more intensive small-scale surveys (see Table 2).

#### <span id="page-11-0"></span>*Monitoring Protocols*

Once the Tier 1 survey is completed and resources allow, progress to Tier 2 surveys at priority sites and/or random sites within the island's four quadrants (NE, NW, SE, and SW).

--- SENTENCE 130 ---
If resources allow, surveying the start, middle, and end of the bleaching event is advantageous to collect information on bleaching progression through time.

--- SENTENCE 131 ---
If resources are limited, carrying out Tier 2 surveys towards the peak of the bleaching event is recommended to collect the most meaningful information (i.e., functional groups affected, severity, and mortality).

--- SENTENCE 132 ---
Once Tier 2 surveys are completed and resources allow, progress to Tier 3 surveys at priority sites.

#### <span id="page-11-1"></span>*Reef flat and Pool habitats*

For all event classifications for reef flats and pools, move straight to Tier 2 surveys in priority areas.

--- SENTENCE 133 ---
Then, if resources (time, observers) allow, carry out Tier 3 surveys at priority sites.

#### <span id="page-11-4"></span>*Fish Surveys*

Until 2013, the fish survey methods included recording all diurnally active, non-cryptic reef fish to species level using belt transects.

--- SENTENCE 138 ---
Six replicate 30 m transects were sampled at each site, with several

passes and widths being used to sample different groups.

--- SENTENCE 139 ---
The first pass was used to sample larger, more mobile species 7.5 m on either side of the transect as the transect is laid out.

--- SENTENCE 140 ---
On the second pass, parrotfish were recorded 5 m on either side of the tape.

--- SENTENCE 141 ---
The third pass surgeonfish were recorded at 2.5 m on either side of the tape.

--- SENTENCE 142 ---
Every other species was recorded at 2.5 m on either side of the tape on the fourth pass, except damselfish, which was recorded on the fifth at 1m on either side.

--- SENTENCE 143 ---
Each fish was counted, and an estimation of length was recorded.

--- SENTENCE 144 ---
The fish surveyor and the benthic surveyor conducted their surveys separately, and at times, the distance between the divers underwater could be up to 150 meters.

--- SENTENCE 145 ---
More recently, updated fish survey protocols have been tested and adopted between January 2013 and September 2014 due to low statistical power associated with past surveys.

--- SENTENCE 146 ---
Survey methods were designed to increase the statistical power of the data to answer questions at multiple spatial scales and make it more comparable to past data collected by the ASEPA coral reef monitoring program between 2003 and 2016 (Houk et al., 2013), as well as the NOAA CREP protocols (Heenan et al., 2014).

--- SENTENCE 147 ---
The subordinate phase starts at 0 m, and every 20 m, a Stationary Point Count (SPC) is conducted in a 7.5 m radius from the diver for 3 minutes.

--- SENTENCE 148 ---
A total of 12 replicate SPCs are completed along the transect tape.

--- SENTENCE 149 ---
Each SPC is a visually estimated 15 m diameter cylindrical plot extending from the substrate to the limits of vertical visibility.

--- SENTENCE 150 ---
Each count consists of two components.

--- SENTENCE 151 ---
The first is a 1-minute species enumeration period in which the diver records the taxa of all species observed within their cylinder.

--- SENTENCE 152 ---
At the end of the 1 min period, the diver begins the tallying portion of the count, systematically working through their species listing and recording each fish's number and estimated size (total length, TL, to the nearest cm).

--- SENTENCE 153 ---
The tallying portion is conducted as a series of rapid visual sweeps of the plot, with one species-grouping counted per sweep.

--- SENTENCE 154 ---
To the extent possible, the diver remains at the six centers of their cylinder throughout the count.

--- SENTENCE 155 ---
However, small, generally site-attached, and semi-cryptic species, which tend to be underrepresented in counts made by an observer remaining in the center of a 7.5 m radius cylinder, are left to the end of the tally period.

--- SENTENCE 156 ---
At this time, the observer swims through their plot area, carefully searching for those species.

--- SENTENCE 157 ---
Surveys are not conducted if horizontal visibility is < 7.5 m, i.e., when observers cannot distinguish the edges of their cylinder.

--- SENTENCE 158 ---
Biomass per fish is then calculated using the standard length-weight equation.

--- SENTENCE 159 ---

#### <span id="page-13-0"></span>*Data Analyses*

The present report provided a trend analysis for all benthic and fish data in the long-term ASCRMP database.

--- SENTENCE 165 ---
We first examined benthic data aggregated by major category groupings (coral, crustose coralline algae, fleshy coralline algae, turf algae, and sand).

--- SENTENCE 166 ---
These generalized graphs depict the overall trends in reef health by comparing calcifying substrates (coral and crustose coralline algae) to others for long periods.

--- SENTENCE 167 ---
Hence, we can evaluate whether reefs have changed in calcification rates and the overall impacts on corals from recent heat-stress events.

--- SENTENCE 168 ---
Subsequently, we examined coral assemblages across major taxonomic families (Acroporidae, Poritidae, Merulinidae, and others).

--- SENTENCE 169 ---
These families depict significant reef structures (Acroporidae and Merulinidae in particular) and whether stress-tolerant corals have begun to emerge from heat stress events (Porites and other corals to a lesser extent).

--- SENTENCE 170 ---
We examined trends at the island scale, the major reef type scale, and the individual site scale to highlight generalized trends and uniqueness.

--- SENTENCE 171 ---
Finally, we examined fish assemblages to indicate current status and trends concerning major function groups and dominant species.

--- SENTENCE 172 ---
These analyses help to understand the status of MPA, reef-type trends, and changes associated with heat stress and coral loss.

--- SENTENCE 173 ---
Beyond examining trends, we lastly performed regression analyses to understand the drivers of fish trends.

--- SENTENCE 174 ---
We examined watershed influences (size, population, nitrogen loading from ASEPA data) and indicators of fishing access (wave energy, boat distances from major ports, and road distances from major populations).

--- SENTENCE 175 ---
From these regression plots, we can appreciate potential drivers of the fish populations and understand where management or enforcement needs may exist.

--- SENTENCE 176 ---
Technical details of the regression analyses follow.

--- SENTENCE 177 ---
Regression analyses were performed using R base packages to build multiple regression models.

--- SENTENCE 178 ---
All potential predictors were standardized, and best-fit models were indicated by the highest explanatory power (R2 values) while maintaining normally distributed residuals.

--- SENTENCE 179 ---
These models will continue to be updated as new data emerge.

--- SENTENCE 182 ---

#### <span id="page-65-0"></span>Fish Surveys

Fish data were first examined by averaging data for each site and reef type across all years and subsequently analyzed for temporal trends.

--- SENTENCE 408 ---
Fish data averaged across all years enabled a comparison between sites to identify significant differences or similarities in the main components of fish biomass.

--- SENTENCE 409 ---
In the northern region, characterized by low human population densities and isolated villages, Fagamalo and Tafeu exhibit high fish biomass, averaging 7.5 kg/m².

--- SENTENCE 410 ---
Conversely, in the southern area, Nu'uuli (8 kg/m²) stands out, closely followed by Amaua (7 kg/m²) and Leone (6.5 kg/m²).

--- SENTENCE 411 ---
Vatia, however, displays comparatively lower fish biomass at 5 kg/m².

--- SENTENCE 412 ---
Among the wave-protected sites, Aoa and Fagasa demonstrate similar fish biomass levels, averaging 5 kg/m².

--- SENTENCE 413 ---
Interestingly, these trends follow the benthic and coral trends, similarly identifying locations with the highest and lowest fish and corals.

--- SENTENCE 414 ---
The dominance of small herbivores, including species with estimated sizes at maturity less than 30 cm, is evident across all sites.

--- SENTENCE 415 ---
These include small-bodied snapper, small-bodied acanthurids, and small-bodied parrotfish.

--- SENTENCE 416 ---
Other fish guilds also exhibited low numbers, consistent across all sites.

!

--- SENTENCE 417 ---
Analysis of fish biomass averaged across different time points and sites.

--- SENTENCE 418 ---
Temporal trends were similar for the north and south reefs between 2008 and 2015, whereby a generally slow decline in the biomass of large-bodied fish was found.

--- SENTENCE 419 ---
However, following climate-inducted heat stress events, fish biomass nearly doubled.

--- SENTENCE 420 ---
Heat stress removes corals and creates an abundance of non-calcifying turf and macroalgae that provide food for small-bodied parrotfish and surgeonfish.

--- SENTENCE 421 ---
These

two fish functional groups were the primary response to climate disturbances between 2015 and 2021.

--- SENTENCE 422 ---
These broad trends have been seen elsewhere across the Pacific so were expected, however, the length of time increased fish biomass remains following heat-stress may be related to reef resilience.

--- SENTENCE 423 ---
Responses to heat stress were greatest for the north and south reef types and limited for the wave-protected reef types (Figure 17).

--- SENTENCE 424 ---
These temporal trends underscore the complex dynamics of fish populations following environmental disturbances such as coral bleaching, where different fish functional groups exhibit diverse trends in biomass changes.

--- SENTENCE 425 ---
Figures 18 and 19 offer a more site-specific insight into fish trends.

--- SENTENCE 426 ---
Across all north sites, there has been a positive response from heat stress since 2015 and 2016.

--- SENTENCE 427 ---

While fish biomass appears to be sufficient to support reef recovery following heat stress for most major reef types, the composition of mainly small-bodied herbivores is a potential indication of fishing pressure across the island, as well as the gradual decline in fish biomass between 2008 and 2015 before heat stress occurred (see regression analyses below).

#### <span id="page-69-0"></span>*Fish regression analyses with environmental variables*

We calculated a metric previously used to summarize the overall condition of fish assemblages (see Houk et al. 2013).

--- SENTENCE 441 ---
The fish assemblage metric represents the combined values of fish biomass, predator biomass, fish size, and fish diversity (Figure 21).

--- SENTENCE 442 ---
By combining these factors, we generated an overall fish assemblage score that could be examined in relation to environmental factors.

!

--- SENTENCE 443 ---
Fish condition scores that were used to evaluate the influence of environmental factors on the fish assemblages.

--- SENTENCE 444 ---
For both north and south reef types, the condition of fish assemblages was similarly limited by two key metrics that limit fishing access: distance from the main marina at Pago Pago and wave energy.

--- SENTENCE 445 ---
High wave energy associated with trade winds potentially restricts boats and fishers' ability to access sites,

while far distances limit access because of fuel costs.

--- SENTENCE 446 ---
Wave energy was calculated based on long-term 10-year wind records translated to wave heights previously defined by Houk et al. (2013).

--- SENTENCE 447 ---
High wave energy also has the potential to influence benthic assemblages by limiting the development of reef framework for fish habitat.

--- SENTENCE 448 ---
Thus, while wave energy represented a natural factor limiting fish abundance due to habitat and increasing fish assemblage conditions due to limited access, the regression analyses revealed a positive influence of wave energy that was potentially related to fishing access.

--- SENTENCE 449 ---
In addition, distance and fuel costs are known to limit fishing access.

--- SENTENCE 450 ---
Analyses revealed a positive relationship with higher wave energy, further distances from the main harbor of Pago Pago, and better fish assemblage condition scores (Figures 22 and 23).

--- SENTENCE 451 ---
Sita, Masausi, Maloata, and Amouli represent more remote locations with higher wave energy and the highest fish assemblage scores.

--- SENTENCE 452 ---
Notable for the south reefs, Alega, despite being near Pago Pago harbor, exhibited relatively high fish assemblage composition based upon relatively high wave energy.

--- SENTENCE 453 ---
Conversely, Pago Pago harbor experiences low wave energy immediate access and low water quality, potentially resulting in the lowest fish scores.

#### <span id="page-72-0"></span>*Discussion*

The analysis of benthic cover trends across the monitored sites reveals that most sites exhibit net positive growth.

--- SENTENCE 465 ---
This trend becomes particularly evident when the blue colors representing calcifying substrates and coral cover cross the black dashed line, signifying the 50% threshold.

--- SENTENCE 466 ---
However, certain sites show negative growth, including Vatia in a sandy reef environment, Fagasa in wave-protected areas, Faga'alu in the southern region, and Fagamalo in the north.

--- SENTENCE 467 ---
Despite these findings, most sites demonstrate either stability or recovery of calcifying substrate and coral cover, especially following the major bleaching events in 2015 and 2017 and fluctuations in earlier years.

--- SENTENCE 468 ---
Upon examination of benthic data focusing on coral trends, we can identify the predominant coral types in each site.

--- SENTENCE 469 ---
With shifting reef dynamics, we anticipate gradual changes in coral communities from *Acroporidae* species to *Poritidae* species like *Porites rus*, influenced by local stressors and historical heat stress.

--- SENTENCE 470 ---
Although there has been a decline in *Acroporidae*, a crucial reef-building coral, signs of recovery are noticeable in 2021, suggesting optimism for reef ecosystem resilience.

--- SENTENCE 471 ---
However, given that heat stress events had become more frequent and severe, for instance, the unprecedented heat stress event experienced during 2024 triggered the fourth mass coral bleaching event.

--- SENTENCE 472 ---
It is necessary to continue with the monitoring program and the data collection to fully understand the impacts of such events on the coral reef of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 473 ---
Fish biomass, averaged across various time points and sites, provides valuable insights into long-term trends and ecosystem health, facilitating comparisons between sites and identifying significant differences or similarities in fish biomass dynamics across locations.

--- SENTENCE 474 ---
Small-bodied herbivores, such as the parrotfish species *Chlorurus japanensis*, *Chlorurus spilurus, Scarus oviceps, and Scarus spitticus,* and the surgeonfish *Ctenochaetus striatus,* dominate across all sites, while other fish guilds consistently exhibit low numbers across all sites, in particular large-bodied fish species.

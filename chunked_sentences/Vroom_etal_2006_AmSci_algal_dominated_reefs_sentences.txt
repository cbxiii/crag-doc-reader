# Algae-Dominated Reefs

*Numerous reports suggest that reefs must be dominated by coral to be healthy, but many thriving reefs depend more on algae*

Peter S. Vroom, Kimberly N. Page, Jean C. Kenyon and Russell E. Brainard

T he words coral and reef go together in everyday language like peanut hutter and jelly.

--- SENTENCE 2 ---
In fact, many people think that coral—colonial marine organisms that secrete a skeleton made of calcium carbonate—means reef.

--- SENTENCE 3 ---
In reality, though, a reef is just any ridge that runs near the surface of the ocean.

--- SENTENCE 4 ---
But one without coral sounds like a reef verging on death, whether you ask someone off the street, most any biologist or even many ecologists.

--- SENTENCE 5 ---
This idea—that only a reef covered in coral is healthy—came along fairly recently.

--- SENTENCE 6 ---
More than half a century ago, some scientists knew that healthy reefs could be covered in more than coral.

--- SENTENCE 7 ---
On reefs around Hawaii in the early 1930s, Paul C. Galstoff collected algae—acellular, unicellular or multicellular organisms that lack true tissues and organs and that use light energy to create food via photosynthesis—and he reported that

the algae played an equal if not greater role on the reefs than coral.

--- SENTENCE 8 ---
Over the years, other tropical-reef hiologists also noted the importance of algae in many reef systems; some scientists even created the name *hiotic reefs* for those dominated by algae.

--- SENTENCE 9 ---
Over the past few years seemingly every publication—from newsstand magazines and newspapers to peerreviewed journals and textbooks—has reported on the degradation of "coral" reefs.

--- SENTENCE 10 ---
For example, in the March 18, 2005, *Science,* John Pandolfi of the University of Queensland in Australia and his colleagues wrote: "Coral reefs provide ecosystem goods and services worth more than \$375 billion to the global economy each year.

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
Yet, worldwide, reefs are in decline."

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
Although many reefs have declined in health, these authors lament the devastating degree of coral-reef degradation on all reefs and equate high algal cover with decreased reef health.

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
Even if humans had never walked the Earth, though, there would still be natural successional and geographic gradients with some reefs dominated by coral and some dominated by algae.

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
In some cases, algae do take over reefs once dominated by coral.

--- SENTENCE 15 ---
Environmental stresses—such as hurricanes, mass bleaching or diseases—can trigger such phase shifts, which are generally regarded as a sign of reef degradation.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
This phenomenon no doubt fostered a general impression that algae-dominated reefs are unhealthy ones.

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
As this article explains, we disagree.

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
Algae can dominate a healthy reef, where all of the essential ecological processes are intact.

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
After five years of monitoring relatively undisturbed, central-Pacific reefs, we have found that coral dominance is relatively limited.

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
Algae, on the other hand, can be fairly common.

**Divers with Diversity**

CRED relies on a multidisciplinary, ecosystem-based approach to science, using individuals who are trained to observe different types of organisms or processes.

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
With that approach, CRED scientists record the "big picture" on reefs, rather than only studying specific groups, such as fish or coral.

--- SENTENCE 61 ---
Anecdotal conversations with investigators who have been studying reef systems for decades indicate that many—although not all—past coralreef studies included coral and fish biologists but few scientists from other disciplines.

--- SENTENCE 62 ---
Consequently, research teams naturally gravitated toward reef systems dominated by coral.

--- SENTENCE 63 ---
CRED research programs take an ecosystem-scale perspective by monitoring multiple habitats.

--- SENTENCE 64 ---
At each longterm monitoring site, we collect specieslevel data concurrently for a variety of biological populations.

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
Additionally, we amass a suite of txreanographic and mapping data from around the islands to provide an ecosystem-level understanding of the regions.

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
We collect these data through a variety of techniques.

--- SENTENCE 67 ---
For example, we employ a sonar system to collect highre.solution imagery of the seafloor for mapping purposes, normally at depths of 20 to 250 meters below sea level.

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
We also document the seafloor with a photoquadrat technique that photographs areas of known size—in our case, 0.18 square meter.

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
Photoquadrats provide one means of capturing data about the prevalence of organisms on a reef.

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
A diver places a standardsized frame on the seafloor along a transect on the reef's surface and takes a digital photograph *(above).

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
At right, the authors show three images from different reefs, and graphs indicate the percentage of surface that different types of organisms cover.

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
As these data show, the coverage of coral and the main categories of algae—macroalgae, turf algae and coralline algae—vary considerably from one site to another.

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
quadrats are taken at randomly selected points along an underwater transect, essentially a measuring tape that we roll out along the seafloor.

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
From each photograph, we determine the percentage of the surface that individual organisms cover.

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
Some of our data also come from dynamic approaches.

--- SENTENCE 76 ---
In video transects, for example, a diver takes videos—holding the camera at a standard distance from the seafloor—while swimming along the transects just mentioned.

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
Then, we analyze random frames from the videotapes and calculate the percent cover of individual organisms.

Tropical reefs naturally contain myriad algal species that are essential for healthy ecosystem dynamics.

--- SENTENCE 111 ---

--- SENTENCE 112 ---

--- SENTENCE 114 ---

--- SENTENCE 115 ---

--- SENTENCE 116 ---

--- SENTENCE 117 ---

--- SENTENCE 118 ---

--- SENTENCE 119 ---

--- SENTENCE 120 ---
Current observations, though, show just the opposite for some tropical species.

--- SENTENCE 121 ---

--- SENTENCE 122 ---

--- SENTENCE 123 ---

--- SENTENCE 124 ---

--- SENTENCE 125 ---
Crustose coralline red algae produce a chemical that triggers settlement in coral and other invertebrate lar\'ae, which creates a vital link between algal and coral communities.

--- SENTENCE 126 ---

## **Calculations of Coverage**

For comparative purposes, we determined the percent coverage of organisms inhabiting the seafloor from eight reefs studied by CRED.

--- SENTENCE 149 ---
These reefs represent a broad geographic area within the Pacific Ocean, including islands in American Samoa (11-14 degrees south latitude), the U.S. Line Islands (0-7 degrees north latitude), the U.S. Phoenix Islands (on the equator), the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands (14-20 degrees north latitude) and the Northwestern Hawaiian Islands (23-29 degrees north latitude).

--- SENTENCE 150 ---
Except for Swains Island in American Samoa, with fewer than 10 residents, no humans inhabit the studied islands or atolls, and they are located hundreds to thousands of kilometers away from population centers.

--- SENTENCE 151 ---
Thus, adjacent human activities do not currently impact these islands, and they have historically received U.S. government protection as wildlife refuges.

--- SENTENCE 152 ---
At each of the eight islands, a multidisciplinary team selected sites that reflect dominant types of habitats.

--- SENTENCE 153 ---
On each reef, the team surveyed enough sites, 4 to 28, to ensure roughly similar sampling densities.

--- SENTENCE 154 ---
Except for some shallow backreef—the island side of a reef—or lagoonal areas, we collected data from depths of about 15 meters.

--- SENTENCE 155 ---
Our analysis shows that algae dominate many reefs.

--- SENTENCE 156 ---
At all but one of our 94 sites, algae took up more space than coral.

--- SENTENCE 157 ---
As many tropical-reef ecologists might expect, crustose coralline red algae—the ones responsible for reef growth and cementation—covered 40 times more surface area than coral at 65 percent of our sites.

--- SENTENCE 158 ---
Similarly, turf algae occupied more area than coral up to 600 times more—at 69 percent of the sites.

--- SENTENCE 159 ---
Moreover, macroalgae took up as much or more room than coral at 46 percent of the sites.

--- SENTENCE 160 ---
This last finding is a huge surprise because many of the leading researchers in coral-reef ecology have stated that macroalgae in reef systems indicates decreased ecosystem health.

--- SENTENCE 161 ---
Nonetheless, we found macroalgae dominating many healthy reefs that were far removed from direct anthropogenic influence.

--- SENTENCE 162 ---
Even more surprising, the majority of macroalgae occupying the seafloor on healthy reefs aren't the sand-producing *HaUmeda.*

--- SENTENCE 163 ---
Although *HaUmeda* is a common component of reef communities at all of the islands that we visited, it made up less than 20 percent of the macroalgae recorded at 65 percent of the sites.

--- SENTENCE 164 ---
Fleshy algae made up the dominant macroalgae, with species composition varying across geographic location.

--- SENTENCE 165 ---
The prevalence of non-invasive fleshy macroalgae in tropical-reef systems opposes current images of a healthy reef.

--- SENTENCE 166 ---
Perhaps most important of all, our data show that different reefs support different organisms.

--- SENTENCE 167 ---
In fact, even reefs that surround a single island can exhibit considerable heterogeneity.

--- SENTENCE 168 ---
The different oceanographic and geomorphic conditions surrounding each island probably account for the variation in the percentage of surface that functional groups cover on different reefs.

--- SENTENCE 169 ---
For example, French Frigate Shoals is a large, subtropical-atoll system that exhibits wide habitat heterogeneity.

--- SENTENCE 170 ---
Forereef regions—essentially the ocean side of reefs—stretching over 20 kilometers along the eastern side of this atoll experience moderate to heavy waves and contain dense beds of the green alga *Mkrodktyon;* in most cases, coral covers only 2 to 9 percent of the surface.

--- SENTENCE 171 ---
Alternatively,

regions within the large lagoon exhibit high variability: Coral dominates some sites, crustose coralline red algae dominates others, and turf algae take over still other spots.

--- SENTENCE 172 ---
In contrast, algae cover the geographically small Rose Atoll in American Samoa in different ways.

--- SENTENCE 173 ---
For example, crustose coralline red algae dominate forereef sites, which also experience moderate to high waves.

--- SENTENCE 174 ---
On Rose Atoll's small and enclosed lagoon, however, algal turfs cover most of the surface.

--- SENTENCE 175 ---
Most of the macroalgae occur on the eastern and southeastern sites, but they do not cover significantly more space than coral.

--- SENTENCE 176 ---
Overall at Rose Atoll, coral takes up only 3 to 26 percent of the space.

--- SENTENCE 177 ---
Our data suggest that each island ecosystem exhibits characteristics that set it apart from geographically close or geomorphically similar ecosystems.

--- SENTENCE 178 ---
This confounds generalizations about reef attributes within and among island groups.

--- SENTENCE 179 ---
Nevertheless, some regional differences appear in the ratios of algal cover to coral cover.

--- SENTENCE 180 ---
As previously noted, the subtropical Northwestern Hawaiian Islands contain reefs dominated by a combination of turf and macroalgae.

--- SENTENCE 181 ---
Of the 28 sites sampled at French Frigate Shoals and the 22 sites examined at Pearl and Hermes Atoll, 32 percent and 59 percent of sites, respectively, contained significantly more space covered by macroalgae than coral.

--- SENTENCE 182 ---
At sites sampled on central and south Pacific islands, however, it was rare for macroalgae to cover significantly more space than coral, although macroalgae covered as much or more surface than corals at 33 percent of sites.

--- SENTENCE 183 ---
When we found dense coverage by coral, it tended to be localized, instead of covering huge expanses of a reef.

--- SENTENCE 184 ---
This underscores the need for increased protection of coral-dense areas.

#### Can We Rate a Reef?

Reef managers often ask us one question: Are my reefs healthy?

--- SENTENCE 185 ---
Without a set of globally accepted indices that define a healthy reef, such questions lack definitive answers.

--- SENTENCE 186 ---
Still, some scientists use our data to better understand what they consider to be healthy tropical-reef ecosystems.

--- SENTENCE 187 ---
According to others, our data indicate more degradation in Pacific-reef systems.

--- SENTENCE 188 ---
At present, CRED scientists deliver results as scientifically as possible in the hope of discovering meaningful indices to describe ecosystem health.

--- SENTENCE 189 ---
Policy managers can put the interpretative spins on how our data should be presented.

--- SENTENCE 190 ---
After thousands of hours of fieldwork over the past five years, there is little doubt among our multidisciplinary team that algae occur naturally in relatively high abundance around many tropical Pacific islands that receive no direct human impact.

--- SENTENCE 191 ---
Moreover, the coverage of different organisms varies with geographic location, geologic history and the age of the reef system.

--- SENTENCE 192 ---
Like the stock market, long-term trends provide one of the best ways to judge the health of a reef ecosystem.

--- SENTENCE 193 ---
On the remote islands that we study, natural or indirect human activities can trigger massive die-offs of coral.

--- SENTENCE 194 ---
For example, coral bleaching occurs when symbiotic zooxanthellae—single-celled algae that typically reside inside coral—disappear, usually in response to physical stress.

--- SENTENCE 195 ---
Typically, these algae give coral its color, and the expulsion of this algae leaves a white coral skeleton that is visible through the now-transparent tissue, giving the coral a white, or bleached, appearance.

--- SENTENCE 196 ---
Coral can also die because of disease or outbreaks of crown-ofthorns starfish.

--- SENTENCE 197 ---
These phenomena can greatly reduce coral cover and increase algal cover.

--- SENTENCE 198 ---
The main question on the minds of scientists revolves around whether the effects of such phenomena are short-term and whether the reef systems will be able to recover.

--- SENTENCE 199 ---
So far, we lack the data to answer those questions.

--- SENTENCE 200 ---
After half a decade of dives, however, we can turn to a fundamental question: Do the romanticized visions of coral reefs held by the general public hold true for reef systems in the tropical Pacific?

--- SENTENCE 201 ---
In certain cases, absolutely!

--- SENTENCE 202 ---
But many other habitats exist, and algae dominate many of these reefs.

--- SENTENCE 203 ---
Without algae there would be no tropical-reef ecosystem, yet marine algae are among the least-studied and least-understood organisms on the reef.

--- SENTENCE 204 ---
So, we continue cataloging and quantifying the species on reef systems around the Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 205 ---
Effective management and conservation of these Pacific ecosystems—and reef systems worldwide—require a better understanding and full appreciation of algae's role in reef health.

--- SENTENCE 206 ---
So we'll continue diving in paradise.

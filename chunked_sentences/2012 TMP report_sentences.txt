## **Executive Summary**

- Most indices continue to show that the reefs of American Samoa are in relatively good condition.
- Average coral cover is now 36%, higher than the averages for the Pacific, South Pacific, U.S. Pacific, Indian Ocean, Great Barrier Reef, and Caribbean.

--- SENTENCE 10 ---
The latter two have just 10% and 8-18% coral cover left, respectively.

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
Coral cover is lower than it was here before crown-of-thorns starfish ate almost all the coral in 1978, and lower than it was in the past in the Pacific, Indian Ocean and Caribbean.
- Coral cover has increased over the 8 years of this monitoring program, while coral cover has decreased in the Caribbean, Pacific, Indian Ocean, Red Sea, and Great Barrier Reef.
-

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
There are very few dead corals, fewer than in the South Pacific, Indonesia, the Philippines, the Indo-Pacific, and world.

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
- Coralline algae, which is considered good, is plentiful, and macroalgae, which is considered bad, is rare most places.

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
Most of the reef is covered by corals and coralline algae, both of which help build the reef, which is good.

--- SENTENCE 15 ---
- Water on the reef slopes is relatively clear, and has remained so, indicating relatively good water quality.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
- Coral cover and the number of coral species in transects correlate with visibility, which is the best measure of water quality.

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
This supports the view that pollution runoff is impacting the reefs.
- Reefs inside the harbor are in poor condition, likely due to sediments, nutrients, and chemical pollution.

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
Coral diversity on slopes inside the harbor is lower than outside, probably due to nutrients and/or pollution.

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
Water quality is low at the head of the harbor, indicated by murky water.

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
- Vatia was badly damaged by the tsunami in the inner bay and by Hurricane Heta in the outer bay.

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
The outer bay is recovering, but the inner bay is not, due to nutrients fueling turf algae.

--- SENTENCE 22 ---
Fagatele Bay was damaged by the tsunami but began recovering immediately.
-

--- SENTENCE 23 ---
The coral reefs of American Samoa are in relatively good condition, much better than in the Caribbean and many other places.

--- SENTENCE 24 ---
There are many features to consider.

--- SENTENCE 25 ---
Coral cover is moderately good, not as good as it once was, it is increasing, most of it is alive, coralline algae is high, macroalgae is low, and water quality is fairly good.

--- SENTENCE 26 ---
The largest fish species are very low due to overfishing, but there are good numbers of small fish and most herbivores (but not the largest).

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
The harbor is in very poor condition.

## **Abstract**

 Benthic cover of corals, coralline algae, and turf all vary greatly from one site to another around Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
Coral cover averaged 36%.

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
Mean coral cover has increased from 25.5% in 2005 to 36.3% in 2012.

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
Crustose coralline algae is now the second largest cover category, followed by turf.

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
The north side of Tutuila has higher coral cover than the south side, the south side has more crustose calcareous algae (as reported in previous annual monitoring reports), and the north has more turf than the south side.

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
American Samoa reef slopes have higher coral cover than many other places presently, including the entire Pacific, and the South Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 34 ---
However, it has lower coral cover than the Caribbean had in 1977, the Pacific had in 1980, and two visual estimates of coral cover in American Samoa before the crown-of-thorns outbreak in 1978.

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
It is, however, similar to some averages for near-pristine reefs now.

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
American Samoa has coral cover measured by towboard that is as high or higher than the near-pristine areas of the Marianas and Hawaii, but less than the US Pacific remote islands.

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
It is also much more than the latest report from the Great Barrier Reef, which reports cover now of only about 10%.

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
The percentage of all corals in American Samoa which are dead is much lower than all other available comparison areas, and is steady.

--- SENTENCE 39 ---
About 75% of the reef area is covered by calcifying organisms (which is very high) and shows no trends.

--- SENTENCE 40 ---
At individual sites, coral cover has been steady at four sites, has increased at five sites, and decreased at only one site.

--- SENTENCE 41 ---
The site that decreased is Vatia, which was badly damaged by the tsunami of 2009 and Hurricane Wilma in early 2010.

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
As reported before, encrusting corals dominate the reef slopes with columnar corals second most common.

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
*Acropora* is the genus that is most speciose, though *Montipora* covers the largest area.

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
*Montipora grisea* is the coral species that covers the largest area, followed by *Porites rus* and *Pavona varians*.

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
A size distribution of the table coral, *Acropora hyacinthus*, was produced, and it has a peak at the second smallest size category, 10-19 cm diameter.

--- SENTENCE 46 ---
The sponge, *Stylissa* sp., was the most common invertebrate recorded, followed by another sponge, *Dysidea* sp

.

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
Like reef slopes, benthic cover varies considerably between outer reef flat sites.

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
The average coral cover on outer reef flats was 32%.

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
This is not much less than on the reef slopes.

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
Coral cover increased steadily from about 21% in 2007 to about 32% now.

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
Thus, both the reef slopes and the reef flats have increased in coral cover over the monitoring period.

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
There are very few dead corals on the reef flats just as on the reef slopes.

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
The total cover of calcifying organisms on the reef flats is not as high as on the reef slopes, but was moderately high and increased over the monitoring period.

--- SENTENCE 54 ---
Coral cover increased at five reef flat sites, was steady at three reef flat sites, and decreased at two reef flat sites, supporting the view that coral cover was increasing on reef flats.

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
The lifeform with the most cover on reef flats was encrusting as it was on the reef slopes.

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
*Acropora* had the most cover on reef flats, and the most species.

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
Encrusting *Montipora* was the species with the most cover, followed by the table coral, *Acropora hyacinthus*.

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
Water clarity on reef slopes was fairly high at 22 meters, with no trends over time.

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
Coral cover and the number of coral species in transects correlated well with water clarity on the individual sites.

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
These variables were also correlated in 2008, supporting the view that runoff from land impacts the coral communities.

## **Methods**

The 12 reef slope sites are shown in the map below (Figure 1).

--- SENTENCE 62 ---
All are on Tutuila and nearby Aunu'u.

!

--- SENTENCE 63 ---
Map of Tutuila with the core slope sites shown.

--- SENTENCE 64 ---
The benthic methods were the same as in 2011.

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
In the core monitoring, four 50-m tapes were laid on a depth contour between 8 and 10 m deep.

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
A space between them of about 10 m was kept.

--- SENTENCE 67 ---
Benthic categories were recorded under each 0.5 m point on the tape.

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
Benthic categories included live coral, dead coral, dead coral with algae, crustose calcareous algae, branching coralline algae, fleshy macroalgae, turf algae, rock, sand, rubble, soft coral, and sponge.

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
"Branching coralline algae" included a soft feathery species that was the most common in that category.

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
That species is *Cheilosporum spectabile*.

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
Any rock that is not white has turf on it, and was recorded as turf.

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
Corals were identified to lifeform, genus, and species when possible, and if the macroalgae was *Halimeda* or *Dictyota*, or something else that was identifiable, that was recorded in as

much detail as possible (usually genus).

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
Soft corals were recorded to genus when possible.

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
Hard coral lifeforms included encrusting, massive, foliose, branching, columnar, submassive, mushroom, *Millepora*, *Acropora* branching, *Acropora* table, *Acropora* digitate, and *Acropora* encrusting.

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
Only the top visible layer was recorded of any multilayer formations such as corals or macroalgae, so all categories of cover add up to 100%.

--- SENTENCE 76 ---
Diurnal, non-cryptic macroinvertebrates were counted in a half-meter wide belt transect beside each 50 m tape.

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
Invertebrates were identified to the most detailed level possible.

--- SENTENCE 78 ---
Spaces between coral branches were not searched.

--- SENTENCE 79 ---
Hard and soft corals were not counted.

--- SENTENCE 80 ---
Horizontal visibility was recorded using the tape.

--- SENTENCE 81 ---
Two transect tapes were recorded on the first dive, and an additional two tapes were done on the second dive.

--- SENTENCE 82 ---
Sites were re-located using the GPS and markers as indicated in the 2005 report.

--- SENTENCE 83 ---
One day was required for each site.

--- SENTENCE 84 ---
In 2008, a total of 12 sites were recorded, including the original 11 plus Masacre Bay.

--- SENTENCE 85 ---
For 2011,10 sites were recorded since the lack of a working boat near the end of the year restricted monitoring fieldwork.

--- SENTENCE 86 ---
Damage to boat ramps were repaired early in the year, facilitating monitoring work.

--- SENTENCE 87 ---
As in 2007-2012, the rugosity measurements were omitted, because a third team member was not available and when included it lengthened dive times to the point where running out of air was a distinct possibility, thus reducing the margin of safety.

--- SENTENCE 88 ---
Further, it appears that the measurement depends primarily on exactly where the chain falls, and that changes in rugosity caused by coral growth will take quite a few years before they would be detectable.

--- SENTENCE 89 ---
A hurricane could make changes in rugosity quickly by removing corals, and if significant hurricane damage occurs, the rugosity measurements can be repeated.

--- SENTENCE 90 ---
Until changes in coral cover or other rugosity changes are apparent, repeating the measurement of rugosity is not worth the increased risk of running out of air.

--- SENTENCE 91 ---
In future years it is hoped that an additional team member can record the rugosity measure, or additional boat dives are available to take the rugosity measure.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
In the meantime, it will be considered a lower priority item, and will be done on an opportunistic basis.

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
When laying the tape, the primary consideration is to keep the tape between 8 m and 9 m deep.

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
The tape is passed along the sides of projections, including live corals such as *Pocillopora* and table corals, which usually have an overhanging side.

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
If it is passed around first one side of one projection and then the other side of another, it is anchored securely from wave action moving it either way at that point.

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
An attempt is made to anchor the tape in this fashion as often as possible, but in some areas there is little to anchor the tape on.

--- SENTENCE 97 ---
A continuing problem is what to do about clefts in the reef.

--- SENTENCE 98 ---
A cleft that is narrow and deep is crossed straight to an anchoring point on the other side.

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
If it is large, then the tape may be laid along one side of it, going up toward shallower water but staying at 8-9 m depth, and then when the bottom rises to that depth, crossing to the other side and continuing on that side out of the canyon.

--- SENTENCE 100 ---
The principle problem with that is finding an anchoring point near the head of the canyon that can hold the tape at the head.

--- SENTENCE 101 ---
The tape is read at each point by reading the substrate under the point at the time at which the diver is directly above the point.

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
A string and weight are not used, as surge and the movement of the tape in the surge makes that a much more difficult and slow procedure.

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
If the tape is stretched between two points far apart and the surge is heavy, the tape can move a meter or more in either direction with each wave.

--- SENTENCE 104 ---
This opens up an opportunity for bias, as the point on the tape sweeps across a variety of benthic patches.

--- SENTENCE 105 ---
If the point on the bottom is recorded that is first seen from a vertical viewpoint, then bias is

minimized.

--- SENTENCE 106 ---
An attempt is made to minimize bias in laying the tape by choosing a route based on depth and anchoring points for the tape, not the substrate.

--- SENTENCE 107 ---
The direct observation underwater of what is under points makes it easier to identify species, and so allows greater taxonomic resolution than video techniques.

--- SENTENCE 108 ---
For coral biodiversity, one hour search dives were conducted at each site.

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
The dive begins at the bottom of the reef (but always well above 30 m deep) and continues as a roving dive as the diver ascends up the slope, searching for as many coral species as can be found.

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
The presence of coral species is recorded underwater, and once out of the water, estimates of abundance of each species are recorded on a 0-5 ("DACOR") scale, with the names "not found," "rare," "uncommon," "common," "abundant," and "dominant."

--- SENTENCE 111 ---
Rare was defined as just 1-2 colonies, and dominant was defined as composing more than half of all corals.

--- SENTENCE 112 ---
The other categories were intermediate values, but not defined as individual corals were not counted, since that would greatly slow the survey and reduce the number of species found.

--- SENTENCE 113 ---
This technique compliments the transect tapes since it covers the entire depth range of the slope, and produces a much larger sample that includes much rarer species than the transect tapes which only produce data on 100 points per tape.

--- SENTENCE 114 ---
So the sample is much larger than the transects, but the quantitative accuracy is much lower.

--- SENTENCE 115 ---
It compliments but does not replace the transects.

--- SENTENCE 116 ---
Sites inside the harbor were added this year in addition to the usual sites outside the harbor.

--- SENTENCE 117 ---
Data collection on reef flats was continued, using transects.

--- SENTENCE 118 ---
In addition, coral diversity data from roving search snorkels on reef flats was carried out for the first time.

--- SENTENCE 119 ---
The methods for both are similar to that on reef slopes.

--- SENTENCE 120 ---
Reef flats are quite different from reef slopes, are a large and important part of the reefs, and are subject to different disturbances than reef slopes, such as low tide events that have no effect on reef slopes.

--- SENTENCE 121 ---
Monitoring reef flats is an important compliment to monitoring reef flats.

### **Reef Slopes**

 Benthic cover on each of the 11 sites is shown in Figure 2 below.

--- SENTENCE 148 ---
There are large differences between sites.

--- SENTENCE 149 ---
Coral cover is highest at Aunu'u, Fagamalo, Leone, Aoa, and Fagatele.

--- SENTENCE 150 ---
It is lowest in Vatia, Amaua, and Faga'alu.

--- SENTENCE 151 ---
Coral cover ranged between 13% and 59%, with a mean of 36%.

!

--- SENTENCE 152 ---
[](_page_9_Figure_4.jpeg)

Trends in benthic cover are shown in Figure 3 below.

--- SENTENCE 153 ---
There has been an over all increase in mean coral cover from 25.5% in 2005 to 36.3% in 2012.

--- SENTENCE 154 ---
There was a slight decrease in the first three years, but steady increase since then.

--- SENTENCE 155 ---
The greatest increase was between

2011 and 2012, when it increased 5.3%.

--- SENTENCE 156 ---
That is a substantial increase for just one year, so perhaps it should be treated with some caution until we get next year's data.

--- SENTENCE 157 ---
But in any case, the increase in coral cover appears to be solid.

!

--- SENTENCE 158 ---
The increase in coral cover has come at the expense of coralline algae, which has decreased.

--- SENTENCE 159 ---
Branching coralline algae and turf appear to be relatively stable, though turf increased some in the last two years.

--- SENTENCE 160 ---
Macroalgae also appears to be relatively stable, and averaged 3% cover over the 7 years of data.

--- SENTENCE 161 ---
The Caribbean had between 2% macroalgal cover in the Caribbean in the 1970's (Côté et al. 2005; Schutte et al. 2010; Bruno et al. in press) and 6% cover (Bruno et al. 2009), and the Great Barrier Reef had about 4-9% macroalgal cover in 1996-2006, while the Caribbean had about 40% macroalgal cover in 1996-2006, and Florida had about 13% macroalgal cover at the same time (Bruno et al. 2009; Hughes et al. 2010).

--- SENTENCE 162 ---
Bruno et al. (in press) reviewed the problem of baselines for macroalgae, concluding that the Caribbean was likely not a representative location in the 1970's and showing that even remote, near-pristine Pacific areas have much higher macroalgae than the early Caribbean.

--- SENTENCE 163 ---
They also show that Tutuila and most of American Samoa has about the same macroalgae cover as the US remote Pacific Islands and the uninhabited islands in the Northern Marianas, and less macroalgae than the Northwest Hawaiian Islands (Vroom et al. 2006; Vroom, 2010; Bruno et al. in press).

--- SENTENCE 164 ---
A comparison of benthic cover on the north side of Tutuila compared to the south side is shown below in Figure 4.

--- SENTENCE 165 ---
The north side has higher coral cover than the south side, the south side has more crustose calcareous algae (CCA) than the north side, the north side has more turf than the south side, the south side has more branching coralline algae (BCA) than the north side, the north side has more sand than the south, the north

has more corallimorph than the south side, and the north has more macroalgae than the south side.

--- SENTENCE 166 ---
The causes of most of these differences is not known, and some of the smaller differences may be due to the chance locations of the sites.

--- SENTENCE 167 ---
However, the higher crustose calcareous algae on the south side is likely due to the fact that for half the year the wind comes from the east and so waves provide more water motion on the south side, since the island is at an angle so waves from the east strike the south side.

!

--- SENTENCE 168 ---
The coral cover on the slopes of American Samoa compare favorably with averages for most areas of the world, as shown in Figure 5, based on transect data.

--- SENTENCE 169 ---
The coral cover on American Samoa is higher than the averages on the South Pacific (SPC, 2005; Bruno and Selig, 2007)

--- SENTENCE 170 ---
the whole Pacific (Bruno and Selig, 2007), the Great Barrier Reef, the Caribbean (Gardiner et al 2003; Jackson et al in press) and Florida.

--- SENTENCE 171 ---
New data from Houk and Musberger (2013) are added for the near-pristine Rongelap Atoll and populated Majuro Atoll in the Marshall Islands (there had been informal claims that Rongelap had 100% coral cover).

--- SENTENCE 172 ---
American Samoa has 36% coral cover compared to only about 8- 18% in the Caribbean and 4% in Florida.

--- SENTENCE 173 ---
Coral cover dropped drastically in the Indian Ocean in 1998, with places such as the Maldives, Seychelles and Chagos reported to have mortality as high as 90%, but a few other places had little mortality, like Rodriques.

--- SENTENCE 174 ---
The Maldives are reported to be showing recovery, while the Seychelles are not.

--- SENTENCE 175 ---
The Indian Ocean had an average of about 38% coral cover before the 1998 El Nino mass bleaching, then dropped to an average of 10%, and then recovered to about 30% cover

(Ateweberhan et al. 2011).

--- SENTENCE 176 ---
American Samoa presently has higher coral cover than most major regions of the world.

!

--- SENTENCE 177 ---
However, coral cover has declined in most parts of the world, and the available information indicates that most were originally higher than the present cover in American Samoa (Figure 6).

--- SENTENCE 178 ---
The Caribbean in 1977, the Pacific in 1980, and the Indian Ocean in 1977-1993 have all been reported to have had higher coral cover than Tutuila now (Gardiner, 2003; Bruno and Selig, 2007; Schutte et al. 2010;

--- SENTENCE 179 ---
Ateweberhan et al. 2011; Jackson et al. in press).

--- SENTENCE 180 ---
Outer reef slopes at Rongelap (with no occupants for 57 years) have about 65% coral cover, but that is based on just a few sites (P. Houk, personal comm.).

--- SENTENCE 181 ---
Scotts Reefs in northwestern Australia had about 45-60% coral cover before the El Nino 1998 mass coral bleaching event, and about 38-45% after it recovered (Gilmore et al. 2013;

--- SENTENCE 182 ---
Rupert et al. 2013).

--- SENTENCE 183 ---
Rowley Shoals, also in northwestern Australia, had about 30% coral cover before cyclone damage in 1994 and also after recovery (Rupert et al. 2013).

--- SENTENCE 184 ---
There are two estimates of coral cover in American Samoa before the outbreak of crown-of-thorns in 1978 ate much of the coral (Wass, 1982; Maragos).

--- SENTENCE 185 ---
Both are above the present coral cover, but they are estimates not quantitative measures, and may well have been from sites selected to be better than average.

--- SENTENCE 186 ---
A survey by John McManus of

the available literature on near-pristine reefs produced an average of about 40% coral cover (McManus et al. 1995), while the Coral Reef Ecosystem Division of NOAA produced an average of 35% cover for the many near-pristine reefs of the U.S. Pacific (Fenner et al. 2008).

--- SENTENCE 187 ---
Most of these reference coral cover levels are higher than the present cover in American Samoa, but some (including the Pacific, Indian Ocean and McManus's value for pristine reefs) are not much higher and the CRED value is slightly lower.

--- SENTENCE 188 ---

### **Table Coral Size Distribution**

 There has been little or no demographic work on corals in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 372 ---
We don't know much about the age and size structures of the populations of different species, for instance.

--- SENTENCE 373 ---
The life history and life cycles of corals are more complex than those of vertebrates, since they are not only capable of sexual reproduction, growth and death, but also of partial death, fragmentation leading to partial death or to founding new colonies which are clone mates of the parent colony, fusion of colonies that are clonemates and so on.

--- SENTENCE 374 ---
The complicated life history makes it difficult to impossible to determine from the shape and size of a coral, what its history is.

--- SENTENCE 375 ---
Did the colony begin as a sexually produced larva that settled, or as an fragment that attached and grew as asexual reproduction?

--- SENTENCE 376 ---
Usually we don't know.

--- SENTENCE 377 ---
With table corals, however, there is a distinctive sequence a newly settled colony goes through.

--- SENTENCE 378 ---
First it spreads out on the substrate, then it builds a column about 3-10 cm high, and then it begins to spread at the top of the column to build the table top.

--- SENTENCE 379 ---
Fragmentation of tables leads to distinctive table-top fragments that are easily distinguished from growing sexual recruits.

--- SENTENCE 380 ---
Further, fusion would be easy to detect as would be partial mortality.

--- SENTENCE 381 ---
Some of these things are pretty easy to tell in massive corals as well, but branching corals are particularly hard to tell, foliose corals it may be hard to tell, and it may also be hard to tell in encrusting corals.

--- SENTENCE 382 ---
Dr. Domingo Ochavillo suggested that I record colony sizes for a species, since that kind of data can be used for population study in ways that have been worked out in fisheries.

--- SENTENCE 383 ---
Table sizes were recorded at Utulei Beach, out where the reef gets nearest to the surface.

--- SENTENCE 384 ---
It isn't really reef flat there, it isn't close enough to the surface for low tides to kill coral and to form a flat.

--- SENTENCE 385 ---
But there are a fair number of *Acropora hyacinthus* there of a variety of sizes, and most are not close enough together to form tiers.

--- SENTENCE 386 ---
Tiers make it quite difficult to determine where one colony ends and another begins, unless the colonies are different colors.

--- SENTENCE 387 ---
Tiers also seem to form most often when colonies are crowded together and can't expand laterally.

--- SENTENCE 388 ---
If that is the case, then table diameter would be a poor proxy for age.

--- SENTENCE 389 ---
In a colony of *A. hyacinthus* that is not restrained by neighboring colonies, table top diameter is proportional to colony age.

--- SENTENCE 390 ---
Colonies add a fixed amount to their diameter each year.

--- SENTENCE 391 ---
Sometimes, colonies can actually have slight concentric circles on the table top, as the angle of outward growth varies slightly with season, probably due to varying light levels.

--- SENTENCE 392 ---
Such concentric circles are not visible on *A. hyacinthus*, but they are visible on *Acropora clathrata* in deeper water, such as out on banks.

--- SENTENCE 393 ---
They may be visible on the *A. clathrata* because the colony is in deeper water where light is less intense and more limiting, so outward growth angle depends slightly on light intensity (Stimson, 1996).

--- SENTENCE 394 ---
Table diameters were measured with a measuring tape, and the greatest diameter of each table was measured.

--- SENTENCE 395 ---
A table that has not had mechanical injuries will have a nearcircular outer edge, but one that has had some mechanical injuries along the edge will have chunks knocked out of it.

--- SENTENCE 396 ---
The edges where chunks have not been knocked out will reflect the age of the table accurately, but where they have been knocked out will not.

--- SENTENCE 397 ---
So a diameter taken at the maximum diameter will almost always reflect the age best.

--- SENTENCE 398 ---
Table

maximum diameters were measured to the nearest centimeter for tables under 20 cm diameter, and to the nearest 5 cm for tables larger than that.

--- SENTENCE 399 ---
Fragments of tables (which were rare) were not measured, nor were overturned tables (which were also rare).

--- SENTENCE 400 ---
The diameters of 166 tables were measured in one snorkel, working from one end of the roughly linear reef top area towards the other, to avoid double-counting any colonies.

--- SENTENCE 401 ---
The resulting distribution can be seen in Figure 35 below.

### **Reef Flats**

The first scientists to study coral reefs, such as Charles Darwin, could walk on reef flats but could not dive on reef slopes, and had few other ways of studying reef slopes.

--- SENTENCE 422 ---
So the earliest studies of coral reefs were generally carried out on reef flats.

--- SENTENCE 423 ---
After SCUBA began to be used for scientific reef studies, most of the studies have been carried out on reef slopes, and fewer on reef flats, with reef flats often forgotten.

--- SENTENCE 424 ---
The area of reef flats can be readily measured from satellite photos, while the area of reef slopes cannot.

--- SENTENCE 425 ---
Estimates of reef slope area based on typical slopes and maximum depths have shown that reef flats are far larger in area than reef slopes (Vecsei, 2004).

--- SENTENCE 426 ---
Thus, reef flats are important for coral reefs in general.

--- SENTENCE 427 ---
Reef flats generally have less coral than reef slopes.

--- SENTENCE 428 ---
because coral growth is limited by exposure to air during low tides.

--- SENTENCE 429 ---
Low tides kill coral that grow above a certain level, due to exposure to air, much like a lawnmower cuts grass blades that grow above a certain level.

--- SENTENCE 430 ---
This effect is why lawns and reef flats appear relatively flat.

--- SENTENCE 431 ---
Low tides also usually restrict coral cove on reef flats to low levels.

--- SENTENCE 432 ---
Reef flats are exposed to different disturbance events and so may have different trends in their communities, so it is important to monitor reef flats as well as reef slopes, although reef flats are generally not monitored.

--- SENTENCE 433 ---
In 2012, reef flats were surveyed near the outer edge of the reef flats, except at Amaua, where inner reef flat was surveyed as in previous years.

--- SENTENCE 434 ---
Heavy surf makes surveying the outer reef flat there unfeasible.

--- SENTENCE 435 ---
Benthic cover on reef flats was highly variable from site to site.

Visibility**

Visibility is a relatively easily obtained indicator of water quality.

--- SENTENCE 522 ---
Low visibility is caused by such things as sediment and plankton, both of which are indicators of poor water quality.

--- SENTENCE 523 ---
A large study of indicators of water quality on the Great Barrier Reef reported that water clarity is the best single indicator of water quality (Fabricius et al. 2012).

--- SENTENCE 524 ---
Visibility estimates were taken using the transect tapes and sighting the end of the tape.

--- SENTENCE 525 ---
The tape was stretched horizontally out from the reef, at the transect depth.

--- SENTENCE 526 ---
Figure 61 shows trends in mean visibility on the reef slope sites.

--- SENTENCE 527 ---
There is no increasing or decreasing trend apparent.

--- SENTENCE 528 ---
Water clarity is relatively good on the reef slopes, much better than in the harbor, but not as good as out at the banks where influence from the island is much less.

!

--- SENTENCE 529 ---
Water clarity at each of the sites is shown in Figure 62 below.

--- SENTENCE 530 ---
Leone, Fagatele, Aunu'u and Tafeu had the best clarity, in that order.

--- SENTENCE 531 ---
Vatia and Fagasa have the worst visibility, in that order.

--- SENTENCE 532 ---
Leone, Fagatele, Aunu'u and Tafeu are some of the best sites for coral cover, and Vatia is now the worst and Fagasa used to be one of the worst.

--- SENTENCE 533 ---
It appears that water clarity may be correlated with coral cover.

!

--- SENTENCE 534 ---
Interestingly, the South side has higher average visibility than the north side, as shown in Figure 63.

--- SENTENCE 535 ---
However, this is not significant (t-test, p = 0.18).

--- SENTENCE 536 ---
The N is too small (5).

--- SENTENCE 538 ---
Bays almost always have streams, and narrow bays don't get flushed as well as areas outside of bays.

--- SENTENCE 539 ---
This may account for the difference.

--- SENTENCE 557 ---

--- SENTENCE 558 ---
This is because particles washed off of land, and plankton fertilized by nutrients washed off land, reduce the water clarity.

--- SENTENCE 559 ---

--- SENTENCE 562 ---

--- SENTENCE 563 ---
The fact that macroalgae cover is low is consistent with the Great Barrier Reef data indicating that 23 m visibility should be correlated with low macroalgae cover.

--- SENTENCE 724 ---
The Introduced and Invasive Soft Coral, *Carijoa riisei*

 About November, 2012, a DMWR diving team including the author surveyed an area in front of the cannery in the harbor where there will be a construction project.

--- SENTENCE 725 ---
During the survey, Alice Lawrence took a couple pictures at about 50 feet deep of something she had not seen before.

--- SENTENCE 726 ---
Later she showed them to the author, and it was clear that it was *Carijoa riisei*.

--- SENTENCE 727 ---
This is a type of soft coral which has been found in both the Caribbean and Indo-Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 728 ---
It was known from the Caribbean, and then it was found in Hawaii.

--- SENTENCE 729 ---
It was found first in Hawaii in Pearl Harbor, and since then it has spread to all the other Main Hawaiian Islands.

--- SENTENCE 730 ---
In recent years, it has been found growing on black corals and killing them.

--- SENTENCE 731 ---
The most recent study found that the genetics indicates that the *Carijoa* in Hawaii came from somewhere else in the Pacific, and the *Carijoa* in the Caribbean was introduced from the Indo-Pacific (Concepcion et al 2010).

--- SENTENCE 732 ---
*Carijoa riisei* was not found in American Samoa in an extensive survey for introduced species a decade ago (Coles et al, 2003).

--- SENTENCE 733 ---
Most introduced marine species are found in harbors, where they have been introduced by ships.

--- SENTENCE 734 ---
Only a very small proportion are invasive and expand outside the harbors and cause problems.

--- SENTENCE 735 ---
Dr. Posa Skelton (personal comm.) reports it is now common in Apia Harbor, (independent) Samoa, on the docks.

--- SENTENCE 736 ---
The fact that it has only been found in the harbor here so far, and that it was not found in a previous survey of the harbor, is consistent with the hypothesis that it is an introduced species.

--- SENTENCE 737 ---
Removing it appears to be a wise precautionary measure, and we plan to do that soon.

--- SENTENCE 738 ---
DMWR also will be surveying the main docks in the harbor in 2013, during which we will be looking for this species.

## **Detailed Report**:

--- SENTENCE 801 ---
Although the health of American Samoa's coral reefs has been summarized recently (PIFSC, 2011; Fenner, 2013), more extensive reviews are based on more dated information (Brainard et al. 2008; Fenner et al. 2008; Fenner, 2011).

--- SENTENCE 802 ---
Of the seven islands of American Samoa, Tutuila is by far the largest, and has most of the human population.

--- SENTENCE 803 ---
The reefs of all seven islands have been studied, but the reefs of Tutuila have received the most study.

--- SENTENCE 804 ---
The reefs of Tutuila consist of several zones: backreef pools that were dug out of the reef flat to obtain landfill for villages and the airport runway, the reef flat which is very shallow, reef slopes which extend from the reef flat down to a depth of about 50-100 feet, a mile wide shelf that varies from about 100 to 300 feet deep, and a ring of banks which in places reach as shallow as 30 feet deep, on the outer part of the shelf.

--- SENTENCE 805 ---
Ofu-Olosega is geologically younger than Tutuila, and has a narrower shelf, and Ta'u is the youngest volcanic island and has no shelf.

--- SENTENCE 806 ---
The reef flats have moderate cover which is less than on the reef slope, because low tides kill corals on the reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 807 ---
The reef slopes and outer banks have the highest coral cover.

--- SENTENCE 808 ---
Most or perhaps nearly all of the shelf is covered with sand or rubble.

--- SENTENCE 809 ---
**Pago Pago harbor construction and water quality**.

--- SENTENCE 810 ---
The reefs in Pago Pago harbor are in much worse shape than those on the reefs outside of the harbor.

--- SENTENCE 811 ---
Many of the reefs in the harbor have been built on top of, because the harbor area had very little flat land and the need for flat land was great as the harbor is the finest harbor in the South Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 812 ---
The U.S. Navy put fill on top of the reef flat on a section of the reef flat on the north side of the harbor, and built storage facilities on that new land.

--- SENTENCE 813 ---
After the Navy left in 1950, canneries were built on that land.

--- SENTENCE 814 ---
A section of reef flat on the north side of the harbor near the mouth of the harbor at Onososopo was filled in, and became a sports field.

--- SENTENCE 815 ---
A small section east of the cannery was filled out on the reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 816 ---
Most of the Rainmaker hotel on the south side of the harbor was built on top of reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 817 ---
The tank farm and Utulei area was built on reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 818 ---
The main docks on the south side were probably also built on reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 819 ---
Some areas of reef flat in the harbor were also excavated to provide fill, perhaps to fill in the areas on top of the reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 820 ---
A fairly large area between Gataivai and Utulei was excavated, and a smaller area in Aua.

--- SENTENCE 821 ---
The canneries released their effluent directly into the water at the canneries until a pipeline was constructed in 1991 out to the mouth of the harbor and sludge taken by boat and dumped 5 miles out to sea.

--- SENTENCE 822 ---
Nutrient levels in the harbor declined quickly in the year following the diversion of the outfall (Craig et al 2005).

--- SENTENCE 823 ---
The first coral reef monitoring transects in the Pacific (and second in the world by only one year) were first done in the harbor in 1917.

--- SENTENCE 824 ---
Most of the transect locations have subsequently been filled or dredged, however, one survives at Aua and another at Utulei.

--- SENTENCE 825 ---
In 1917, there were many coral colonies over all but the very inner section of the transect at Aua, which went from shore to the reef crest.

--- SENTENCE 826 ---
Now, only the reef crest has coral on it, the rest is dead rubble (with a tiny amount of live coral).

--- SENTENCE 827 ---
The reef flat from the canneries nearly to Onososopo is in the same condition as at the Aua transect.

--- SENTENCE 828 ---
The transect in Utulei had a high soft coral cover in 1917, and now has no soft coral cover (Cornish and DiDonato, 2004; Craig et al. 2005).

--- SENTENCE 829 ---
Soft corals remain uncommon to rare throughout the harbor.

--- SENTENCE 830 ---
There is excellent coral in a small island of undisturbed reef flat on the outer edge of the reef flat at Gataivai near the sewage pipe, and an excellent area of coral on the reef flat at Onososopo.

--- SENTENCE 831 ---
Coral has increased dramatically over the sewage pipe area at Gataivai since 2005.

--- SENTENCE 832 ---
Both these locations are near the harbor mouth.

--- SENTENCE 833 ---
Water quality in the harbor is best near the harbor mouth and worst near the head of the harbor, as indicated by water clarity.

--- SENTENCE 834 ---
The water near the head of the harbor is green, indicating phytoplankton, which in turn indicates high nutrients.

--- SENTENCE 835 ---
A small oyster grows near the water line in the harbor, and growth is dense in the inner and middle harbor, and they do not grow near the mouth of the harbor.

--- SENTENCE 836 ---
Oysters are filter feeders that feed on plankton, so they indicate plankton which in turn indicates nutrients.

--- SENTENCE 837 ---
Flushing is greatest at the mouth of the harbor and least at the head of the harbor, so nutrients that wash into the head of the harbor accumulate, while at the mouth of the harbor they are quickly washed away.

--- SENTENCE 838 ---
Coral diversity in the harbor is lower than outside the harbor.

--- SENTENCE 839 ---
Coral diversity declines with increasing pollution (Edinger et al. 1998).

--- SENTENCE 840 ---
It is not clear whether reefs inside the harbor are currently stable, improving, or worsening.

--- SENTENCE 841 ---
The harbor reefs in 1917 were described as having sufficient reef fish for a fleet of small traditional canoes (pao pao) that were used to fish the harbor reefs.

--- SENTENCE 842 ---
Now, fish populations inside the harbor appear to be low, and there is little fishing inside the harbor.

--- SENTENCE 843 ---
There were several periods around 2007-2009 when the inner harbor or whole harbor turned red from a non-toxic red tide caused by a dinoflagellate bloom.

--- SENTENCE 844 ---
The new soccer field at the head of the harbor was being heavily fertilized, and recommended a reduced fertilization plan which would still stimulate the grass.

--- SENTENCE 845 ---
That was carried out, and there have been no red tides since then (Morton et al. 2011).

--- SENTENCE 846 ---
In sum, the reefs and reef fish of Pago Pago harbor are highly degraded, due to human activities.

--- SENTENCE 847 ---
**Outside the harbor: construction**.

--- SENTENCE 848 ---

**Reef flats and low tides**.

--- SENTENCE 858 ---
The reef flats of Tutuila generally have low coral cover near shore and more coral cover farther from shore, with an average of about 10% coral cover mid-flat and 30% near the reef crest.

--- SENTENCE 859 ---
Coral cover on the reef flat is primarily regulated by low tides.

--- SENTENCE 860 ---
When unusually low tides occur, they expose living corals on the reef flat to air for a period of time longer than the coral can survive, and the portions of the coral that are exposed for too long die.

--- SENTENCE 861 ---
In periods between unusually low tides, corals increase in live cover (Brown et al. 2007; Scopélitis et al. 2011).

--- SENTENCE 862 ---
Corals in shallow pools (often around 30 cm deep) in the reef flat survive these low tides, and so continue to grow and fill in those low areas.

--- SENTENCE 863 ---
The reef flat is flat because of these processes.

--- SENTENCE 864 ---
Coral cover on reef flats has increased over the last 9 years.

--- SENTENCE 865 ---
This may be a cyclical effect, with increases happening during periods of fewer unusually low tides, and may reverse in the future when lower tides occur.

--- SENTENCE 866 ---
Sea level rise in the South Pacific is currently about 2 mm a year, while it is about 3 mm per year globally.

--- SENTENCE 867 ---
Coral reefs can easily keep up with this, since reef growth rates of 3-6 mm a year are typical (Montaggioni, 2005).

--- SENTENCE 868 ---
Currently, low tide events primarily affect stands of two staghorns corals, *Acropora aspera* and *A. muricata*.

--- SENTENCE 869 ---
*A. aspera* is the more common of the two on reef flats, and forms thickets in shallow water on reef flats as well as somewhat deeper.

--- SENTENCE 870 ---
*A. muricata* is less common on reef flats because it needs a bit more water depth than *A. aspera*.

--- SENTENCE 871 ---
It is most common at Lauli'I, Faga'alu, Onososopo, and Faga'itua Bay.

--- SENTENCE 872 ---
For both species, a low tide event may kill all the coral except for around the edges of thickets, or it may just kill the top ends of branch tips, depending on how low the water goes and whether live coral remains below the lowest level of the water.

--- SENTENCE 873 ---
These are fast growing species, and thickets often grow back quickly, in a few years.

--- SENTENCE 874 ---
Since the advent of SCUBA diving in scientific research, reef slopes have often been considered the heart of the reef, since coral cover, diversity, and fish populations, are often greatest there.

--- SENTENCE 875 ---
Reef flats actually cover a much larger area on reefs around the world, however (Vecsei, 2004).

--- SENTENCE 876 ---
Reef slopes outside the harbor have an average of about 36% **coral cover** recorded in transects now.

--- SENTENCE 877 ---
Variation from site to site is high (Fenner, this report, Figure 2), as is typical of many coral reefs.

--- SENTENCE 878 ---
Coral cover in towboard surveys is closer to 27% (PIFSC, 2011)

--- SENTENCE 879 ---
but values closer to 17% have also been recorded (Vroom, 2010).

--- SENTENCE 880 ---
Towboard surveys commonly produce lower coral cover values because they cover the whole reef or a much larger part of the reef, including areas that don't have the best coral, such as rubble, bare rock, and/or sand.

--- SENTENCE 881 ---
Reefs are naturally very patchy, which means they have high spatial variability, and that includes areas of high coral cover, low coral cover, bare rock, and sand.

--- SENTENCE 882 ---
Reefs produce sand, which tends to accumulate in pockets.

--- SENTENCE 883 ---
Transect locations are rarely chosen randomly (Nat Parks transects were chosen randomly within a specified depth range), and so the locations chosen often have higher coral cover than the average substrate.

--- SENTENCE 884 ---

--- SENTENCE 885 ---

--- SENTENCE 886 ---
Swains has higher coral cover on slopes than the other islands, and Rose Atoll has lower cover than the other islands (Vroom, 2010; PIFSC, 2011).

--- SENTENCE 887 ---
The coral cover of reef slopes on Tutuila, currently about 36% on the average, is higher than many current comparison averages for reef areas.

--- SENTENCE 888 ---
So it is higher than averages for the South Pacific, for the whole Pacific, and much higher than the Caribbean.

--- SENTENCE 889 ---

--- SENTENCE 890 ---

--- SENTENCE 891 ---
In the Caribbean and Indian Ocean, people would love to have reefs anywhere close to as healthy as ours.

--- SENTENCE 892 ---
On the other hand, the average coral cover on Tutuila is not as high as some figures for near-pristine reefs (McManus et al. 1995; Vroom, 2010) , and not as high as visual estimates taken on Tutuila before the outbreak of crown-of-thorns starfish in 1978.

Tutuila once had more coral cover than it does now.

--- SENTENCE 898 ---
Better than many, not as good as it could be.

--- SENTENCE 914 ---

--- SENTENCE 974 ---
**Macroalgae** has been reported to increase and dominate reefs during "phase shifts."

--- SENTENCE 975 ---
Phase shifts happen when a reef relatively suddenly goes from being dominated by coral to being dominated by something else.

--- SENTENCE 976 ---
The best known thing dominating reefs after phase shifts is macroalgae.

--- SENTENCE 977 ---
Other terms for macroalgae include "fleshy algae" and "frondose algae."

--- SENTENCE 978 ---
Basically, it is algae with thalli that look like leaves, stipes that look like stems, and holdfasts that look like roots.

--- SENTENCE 979 ---
They are distinct from what is often called "turf" or "filamentous algae" which are made of filaments which are strings of single cells.

--- SENTENCE 980 ---
Filamentous algae grow fast and produce no defensive chemicals or structures (like tough cellulose or calcium) which deter herbivores feeding on them.

--- SENTENCE 981 ---
Macroalgae generally grow slow and invest in chemical and/or structural defenses.

--- SENTENCE 982 ---
As a result, most herbivorous fish readily feed on filamentous algae, but not on macroalgae.

--- SENTENCE 983 ---
The primary defense of filamentous algae is its ability to grow

very fast and replace losses.

--- SENTENCE 984 ---
Herbivory on undisturbed reefs is very intense, with something on the order of 25,000 bites per square meter per day.

--- SENTENCE 985 ---
But almost all herbivory is directed at filamentous algae.

--- SENTENCE 986 ---
There are a few fish that do eat macroalgae, like the batfish discovered to eat *Sargassum* in one experiment (Bellwood et al. 2006) and the unicorn surgeonfish found to eat the same algae in another experiment (Hoey and Bellwood, 2009).

--- SENTENCE 987 ---
Macroalgae compete with corals for space and can inhibit the settlement of new recruits (Kuffner, et al. 2006; Box and Mumby, 2007; Birrell et al. 2008).

--- SENTENCE 988 ---
Some macroalgae may harbor diseases, and when the rub against corals and injure the tissues, the disease can enter the tissue and the coral can get the disease (Nugues et al. 2004).

--- SENTENCE 989 ---
Further, the toxic chemicals in macroalgae can cause bleaching or dead in areas in which the algae are in contact with the coral (Rasher and Hay, 2010).

--- SENTENCE 990 ---
As a result of these considerations, macroalgae is generally considered bad.

--- SENTENCE 991 ---
However, there are some types of macroalgae that have rarely been implicated in phase shifts or blooms.

--- SENTENCE 992 ---
The genus *Halimeda* is one of those, though erect calcifying green algae like *Halimeda* have now been reported to have been increasing in a variety of locations (Bruno et al. in press).

--- SENTENCE 993 ---
Tutuila has remarkably little macroalgae.

--- SENTENCE 994 ---
Typically it covers about 2-3% or less of the substrate in transects on slopes, and almost all that is recorded is *Halimeda*.

--- SENTENCE 995 ---
There are a few places with much more.

--- SENTENCE 996 ---
Large areas of the shelf are covered with *Halimeda* growing on sand (some of which it produces).

--- SENTENCE 997 ---
The slope on the inner side of an island at the mouth of Masafau Bay has very high *Halimeda* cover at 15-18 m depth.

--- SENTENCE 998 ---
Neither of those appears to be a problem.

--- SENTENCE 999 ---
*Halimeda* actually produces calcium in the form of little flakes inside the thallus, which adds to sand when the algae dies.

--- SENTENCE 1000 ---
So *Halimeda* actually contributes to building the reef, especially sand.

--- SENTENCE 1002 ---

--- SENTENCE 1003 ---

--- SENTENCE 1009 ---

Village elders said that there used to be much less algae and more fish on that reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 1012 ---

--- SENTENCE 1014 ---
Compared to that, our reef flats look clean and healthy.

--- SENTENCE 1015 ---
One caveat to all this is that remote reefs that have almost no impact from humans often have significant amounts of algae, often including significant amounts of macroalgae (Vroom et al. 2006).

--- SENTENCE 1016 ---
Moderate amounts of algae, including macroalgae, are natural parts of coral reefs, and do not always indicate poor reef health.

--- SENTENCE 1017 ---

--- SENTENCE 1019 ---

--- SENTENCE 1022 ---

--- SENTENCE 1023 ---
**Coralline algae** is a living algae that produces hard calcium underneath a thin coat of living algae.

--- SENTENCE 1024 ---
A common form of it is crustose coralline algae, which grows as an encrusting sheet on the reef.

--- SENTENCE 1025 ---
It is a significant contributor to calcium building the reef.

--- SENTENCE 1026 ---
Coralline algae can easily be smothered by sediment accumulation or by other algae growing on it.

--- SENTENCE 1027 ---
So herbivores that remove algae from its surface, clear water with little sedimentation, and water motion that removes sediment, provide good conditions for its growth.

--- SENTENCE 1028 ---
Coralline algae can actually remove small amounts of algae and sediment by shedding the surface layer of the algae.

--- SENTENCE 1029 ---
Also, some coralline algae can grow in reduced light levels, so they are often an understory under larger algae such as macroalgae, and they can be found living in shady places such as under overhangs or under plate corals (Connell, 2003).

--- SENTENCE 1030 ---
In some places if you remove turf or filamentous algae from the bottom, you will find coralline algae underneath, so it is not absolutely impossible for them to live under algae and some sediment.

--- SENTENCE 1031 ---
Around Tutuila, crustose coralline algae is most abundant on upper reef slopes and outer reef flats.

--- SENTENCE 1032 ---
Both of these areas have greater wave surge than lower on the slope or the inner reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 1033 ---
In addition, the south side of Tutuila has more coralline algae than the north, and the south side has steady wave surge from the east half the year, while the other half of the year wind direction and wave surge change frequently so the north side does not have an extended period of strong wave surge.

--- SENTENCE 1034 ---
American Samoa has large amounts of coralline algae compared to many other reef areas (Vroom, 2010).

--- SENTENCE 1035 ---
Coral larvae are attracted to settle by a chemical emitted by bacteria living on the surfaces of coralline algae, and coralline algae has been called flypaper for coral larvae (Morse and Morse, 1996).

--- SENTENCE 1036 ---
Although this has only been demonstrated with a few coral species so far, it is likely to be true of many coral species.

--- SENTENCE 1037 ---
The reason is likely to be because coralline algae need the same type of habitat corals need: clear water, low sedimentation, and strong herbivory controlling algae.

--- SENTENCE 1038 ---
Thus, there is selection pressure for coral larvae to choose a settlement location where there is coralline algae.

--- SENTENCE 1039 ---
So coralline algae both attract coral larvae and add to the reef, both considered good things.

--- SENTENCE 1040 ---
Further, they are an indicator (like coral) of good conditions.

--- SENTENCE 1041 ---
So the large amount of coralline algae on the reefs here supports the view that the reefs are relatively healthy.

--- SENTENCE 1042 ---
The one down side to coralline algae is that a few species can grow over living corals.

--- SENTENCE 1043 ---
While that has been observed here, it does not appear to be common.

--- SENTENCE 1044 ---
Coral and coralline algae, both considered good on reefs, occupy much more area on the reef slopes than do macroalgae and turf put together.

--- SENTENCE 1045 ---
Further, most of the reef is covered with calcifying organisms (coral, coralline algae and *Halimeda*).

--- SENTENCE 1046 ---

fact that bioeroders are uncommon to rare (see below) indicates that the reef is being added to in a healthy fahion.

--- SENTENCE 1055 ---

--- SENTENCE 1056 ---

--- SENTENCE 1067 ---

--- SENTENCE 1070 ---

--- SENTENCE 1071 ---

--- SENTENCE 1072 ---

--- SENTENCE 1073 ---

**Crown-of-thorns starfish** (COTS) eat coral tissues, and commonly undergo huge fluctuations in abundance, from rare most of the time to huge population explosions that kill much of the coral.

--- SENTENCE 1074 ---
In American Samoa there was a huge outbreak of millions of starfish in 1978 which killed about 90% of the coral.

--- SENTENCE 1075 ---
A previous outbreak is reported to have occurred in 1948.

--- SENTENCE 1076 ---

--- SENTENCE 1077 ---
The total numbers on Tutuila are not high, but it is a clear increase from low background levels.

--- SENTENCE 1078 ---
There are enough of them in an area off of Fagamalo to have killed half or more of all the corals in a large area.

--- SENTENCE 1079 ---
Numbers of COTS appear to be increasing.

--- SENTENCE 1080 ---
The only time when removals can be effective is when there are moderate numbers, since in the outbreaks the numbers are too large for removals to have any effect.

--- SENTENCE 1081 ---

--- SENTENCE 1082 ---

--- SENTENCE 1083 ---

--- SENTENCE 1084 ---
In any case, increases in their numbers present a worrying trend.

--- SENTENCE 1085 ---

--- SENTENCE 1086 ---

--- SENTENCE 1087 ---

--- SENTENCE 1088 ---
The situation in American Samoa is vastly better than that on the Great Barrier Reef, but the present increases are quite worrying.

**Bioeroders** are organisms that erode reef limestone in a variety of ways.

--- SENTENCE 1090 ---
Examples include sea urchins which grind away the reef with their hard teeth as they eat filamentous algae, boring sponges which use acid to etch out tiny chips of reef rock or coral skeleton and blow the chips out into the water onto the reef.

--- SENTENCE 1091 ---
The boring sponges bore into the rock in order to live inside the rock, in a protected location.

--- SENTENCE 1092 ---
The small openings of some boring sponge species, and encrusting areas of other boring sponges, are easy to spot in Florida and the Caribbean, plus some reefs in Florida now have a fine white dust coating on the reef from the tiny chips that boring sponges emit.

--- SENTENCE 1093 ---
The author has never been able to find an encrusting boring sponge or boring sponge openings in American Samoa, in spite of trying to find them and a lot of time examining reef substrate.

--- SENTENCE 1094 ---
Further, the author has not seen the fine white dusting.

--- SENTENCE 1095 ---
The author has broken dead staghorn branches that have been dead long enough to have eroded surfaces and be covered with a mix of coralline algae and turf.

--- SENTENCE 1096 ---

--- SENTENCE 1097 ---
Dead staghorns do not appear to collapse for many years.

--- SENTENCE 1098 ---
All of which indicates that sponge bioerosion in American Samoa is at low levels.

--- SENTENCE 1099 ---

--- SENTENCE 1103 ---

basket stars, and so on.

--- SENTENCE 1109 ---

--- SENTENCE 1110 ---

--- SENTENCE 1112 ---
Sea urchins also appear to be uncommon in American Samoa, with two exceptions.

--- SENTENCE 1114 ---

--- SENTENCE 1115 ---

--- SENTENCE 1116 ---

--- SENTENCE 1118 ---

--- SENTENCE 1119 ---
They do not seem to continually grind away more reef than in their single burrow, which they are always in.

--- SENTENCE 1120 ---
The urchin *Echinometra*, which can do considerable bioerosion, appears to be rare.

--- SENTENCE 1121 ---
Further, there are none of the scalloped reef surfaces commonly seen in a place like Hawaii where urchin bioerosion is significant.

--- SENTENCE 1122 ---
Some herbivorous fish are also bioeroders.

--- SENTENCE 1123 ---
Parrotfish can be divided into grazers, scrapers, and excavators.

--- SENTENCE 1124 ---
Grazers do not erode the reef, scrapers only remove very small amounts of substrate, but excavators remove large amounts of substrate.

--- SENTENCE 1125 ---

--- SENTENCE 1126 ---
Bumphead parrotfish are now very rare with only about one individual sighted per year, and probably near local extinction.

--- SENTENCE 1127 ---
So parrotfish do very little bioerosion in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1128 ---
In summary, bioeroders, including urchins, excavator parrotfish, and all filter feeders, are uncommon to rare in Tutuila outside the harbor, but a couple types of filter feeders are abundant in the harbor.

--- SENTENCE 1129 ---
The low levels of bioeroders are consistent with the view that the reefs are relatively healthy.

--- SENTENCE 1130 ---
Most **invertebrates** on the reefs of America are small and uncommon, with a few rare exceptions like the oysters and sponges in the harbor.

--- SENTENCE 1136 ---

--- SENTENCE 1140 ---

--- SENTENCE 1141 ---

--- SENTENCE 1142 ---

--- SENTENCE 1143 ---

--- SENTENCE 1144 ---

--- SENTENCE 1145 ---

--- SENTENCE 1146 ---

--- SENTENCE 1147 ---

**Water clarity** has recently been found to be the best indicator of water quality around coral reefs (Fabricius et al. 2012).

--- SENTENCE 1149 ---
Luckily, it is also very easy to measure, and has been measured in the Territorial Monitoring Program as well as surveyed in the harbor once.

--- SENTENCE 1150 ---
Water clarity is low at the head of the harbor (about 2 meters) and increases to the mouth of the harbor where it is about 25 meters, similar to other reef slope sites outside the harbor.

--- SENTENCE 1151 ---
Water clarity is even greater farther offshore on the banks, though the author has not measured it there yet.

--- SENTENCE 1152 ---
The values at the reef slope are fairly good for reefs near an inhabited high island, generally around 30 meters visibility is considered good in the dive industry.

--- SENTENCE 1153 ---
On the Great Barrier Reef, visibility of over 10 m was sufficient to have low macroalgae and high coral diversity (De'ath and Fabricius, 2010).

--- SENTENCE 1154 ---
There is no trend in water clarity at the Territorial Monitoring Program sites.

--- SENTENCE 1155 ---
The fairly good water clarity on reef slopes is consistent with the view that the reefs are relatively healthy.

--- SENTENCE 1156 ---
However, Houk et al. (2010) have found evidence that coral diversity correlates with human population density, supporting the view that non-point pollution from human activities is impacting the reef slopes in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1157 ---
The Territorial Monitoring Program data did not show a correlation between human population density and coral cover or diversity (Fenner, 2008), however, it does show a correlation between water clarity and coral cover and diversity (Fenner, 2013).

--- SENTENCE 1158 ---
Thus, there are at least two lines of evidence that support the view that non-point pollution is impacting coral reef slopes in Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 1159 ---
So while the water clarity is fairly good, there is also evidence of impact.

--- SENTENCE 1160 ---
Water quality measures have another problem, which is that water runoff quality varies greatly over time, since there are intense pulse rain and runoff events from time to time which contain most of the sediment runoff (and may contain much of the nutrient runoff) onto the reefs.

--- SENTENCE 1161 ---
In order to catch those events, stream quality must be recorded continuously, as it is now at Faga'alu stream.

--- SENTENCE 1162 ---
The water quality measurements of the Territorial Monitoring Program are taken along with monitoring data, only on one day a year.

--- SENTENCE 1163 ---
Further, monitoring data is not taken when runoff is intense, since water clarity on the reef slope may be reduced.

--- SENTENCE 1164 ---
So the runoff events are actively avoided in this data collection.

--- SENTENCE 1165 ---
On the other hand, streamwater

during runoff events can carry heavy sediment loads which make the path of the water visible.

--- SENTENCE 1166 ---
It is easy to see with most streams that there is usually an ava near the stream mouth and water rushes out the ava as long as waves pump water over the crest.

--- SENTENCE 1167 ---
So the muddy stream water is drawn directly out the ava.

--- SENTENCE 1168 ---
Further, the fresh water containing the silt, nutrients, and chemical pollution floats on top of the salt water because fresh water floats.

--- SENTENCE 1169 ---
So it passes rapidly over the reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 1170 ---
Once out the ava, wave action tends to mix it with the larger body of ocean water there.

--- SENTENCE 1171 ---
Drifters show that once water goes out the ava, it stops, and may even be drawn in over the reef crest again and go out the ava again.

--- SENTENCE 1172 ---
The mixing without strong movement delivers a diluted concentration of the stream water contents to the reef slope.

--- SENTENCE 1173 ---
This is the reason that the reef slope does not have as high clarity as the offshore banks or oceanic water.

--- SENTENCE 1174 ---
Although the water on reef slopes is not nearly as bad as at the head of the harbor, it does have enough sediment, nutrients, and/or chemical pollution in it to have some impact on the reef.

--- SENTENCE 1175 ---
Thus, water quality is a "glass half full-glass half empty" situation for reef health.

--- SENTENCE 1176 ---
Another complication is that corals can take much higher levels of chronic sedimentation than they can in an acute sedimentation event.

--- SENTENCE 1177 ---
Thus, a big runoff event can kill most of the corals on a reef, while you can also find reefs with very high coral cover and good diversity in areas with huge amounts of sediment around the coral.

--- SENTENCE 1178 ---
Further, different species of coral have different thresholds of tolerance for sediment, so as sediment increases, species may drop out one at a time.

--- SENTENCE 1179 ---
Vatia Bay is a narrow bay like the harbor, but smaller in size and with fewer people around it.

--- SENTENCE 1180 ---

--- SENTENCE 1182 ---
The murkier water at the head of the bay shows lower water quality.

--- SENTENCE 1183 ---

--- SENTENCE 1184 ---

--- SENTENCE 1185 ---
There was damage to the outer bay as well, but there was less filamentous algae.

--- SENTENCE 1186 ---

--- SENTENCE 1187 ---
In the outer bay, the substrate appears clean and corals have begun to grow back.

--- SENTENCE 1188 ---
Thus, the inner bay shows classic signs of lack of resilience, and the outer bay is showing signs of resilience.

--- SENTENCE 1189 ---
The difference is surely due to the poor water quality at the head of the bay.

--- SENTENCE 1190 ---
It is quite possible that at the head of the bay humans are not putting any more nutrients or sediment in than they do elsewhere, but the nutrients that do run into the bay build up at the head of the bay because the water is not flushed.

--- SENTENCE 1191 ---
So the shape of the bay makes the head much more vulnerable to nutrient runoff than the mouth.

**Rugosity** or complexity refers to the physical shape of the reef, the roughness.

--- SENTENCE 1193 ---
Fish need hiding places, and a reef with a rugged surface provides more hiding places and may have more fish as a result.

--- SENTENCE 1194 ---
Some kinds of coral such as branching, foliose and tables, provide high rugosity.

--- SENTENCE 1195 ---
So rugosity should depend on both coral cover and which kinds of coral are present.

--- SENTENCE 1196 ---
Some natural disturbances like hurricanes can reduce rugosity, while crownof-thorns starfish remove coral tissue without reducing rugosity, as does mortality from bleaching or disease.

--- SENTENCE 1197 ---
Dead skeletons eventually collapse, reducing rugosity.

--- SENTENCE 1198 ---
Rubble

beds and sand flats have very low fish abundances compared to areas of coral.

--- SENTENCE 1199 ---
Coral cover in American Samoa of 36% is moderate, but the most abundant coral is encrusting (*Montipora grisea*), which does not provide hiding places for fish.

--- SENTENCE 1200 ---
The second most abundant coral, *Porites rus*, has thin horizontal plates and vertical columns, so it does produce good rugosity.

--- SENTENCE 1201 ---
Tutuila reefs also have high crustose coralline algae cover, which is encrusting, not adding to rugosity.

--- SENTENCE 1202 ---
In many places the reef matrix itself has holes in it, providing some hiding places for fish.

--- SENTENCE 1203 ---
Rugosity was recorded in the 2005 Territorial Monitoring Program, but not in subsequent years.

--- SENTENCE 1204 ---

--- SENTENCE 1205 ---
Abundances of small fish on the reefs are good, suggesting that the reef rugosity is sufficient for fish populations.

--- SENTENCE 1209 ---
Total **reef fish** biomass in American Samoa is about one third that on remote unfished reefs.

--- SENTENCE 1210 ---
One third of virgin biomass is the approximate level at Maximum Sustainable Yield (MSY) for fishing.

--- SENTENCE 1211 ---
So from a fisheries science view, this would appear to be about right.

--- SENTENCE 1212 ---
However, that is the biomass of all fish species put together, and fishing does not impact all species equally.

--- SENTENCE 1213 ---
For instance, none of the smallest species are fished at all.

--- SENTENCE 1214 ---
Thus, while some species will not be overfished, others must be overfished in order to have the average be close to MSY.

--- SENTENCE 1215 ---
Thus, the one third level of the biomass of all fish together indicates that some species must be overfished.

--- SENTENCE 1216 ---
Fishing generally removes the largest fish first, then the medium size fish next, and the smallest fish last.

--- SENTENCE 1217 ---
The relative abundances of the largest fish, medium fish, and small fish, can provide a rough indicator of fishing pressure, as can simply observing the number of people fishing.

--- SENTENCE 1218 ---
Fishing pressure in American Samoa was very high in the past.

--- SENTENCE 1219 ---
At one point in the late 1970's, data Richard Wass took produced the highest fish catch weight per unit area of reef reported at that time anywhere in the world (Dalzell, 1996).

--- SENTENCE 1220 ---
Now, few fishers are commonly seen on the reefs at least in daytime, and fishing pressure is fairly low.

--- SENTENCE 1221 ---
The reduction in fishing effort has come because of rising incomes which allow the purchase of store-bought package food, which has replaced much of the fish diet (Sabater and Carroll, 2009).

--- SENTENCE 1222 ---
Surprisingly few fishers are required to reduce the populations of at least some species of fish.

--- SENTENCE 1223 ---
Houk and Musberger (2013) reported that just 40 people fishing to feed themselves on Rongelap are sufficient to reduce fish abundances where the people live.

--- SENTENCE 1224 ---
American Samoa has just 4-8% of the sharks that would be here if there were no people (Nadon et al. 2012).

--- SENTENCE 1225 ---
The biomass of a fish species at Maximum Sustainable Yield is on the order of 33%, so 4-8% indicates that the sharks are overfished.

--- SENTENCE 1226 ---
Sharks are among the most sensitive reef fish to fishing, with surprising large numbers found on remote reefs without fishing.

--- SENTENCE 1227 ---
American Samoa does have a small number of reef sharks, but they are clearly reduced numbers.

--- SENTENCE 1228 ---
At the other end of the size spectrum are species like gobies, blennies, and damsels, which are not fished because they are too small.

--- SENTENCE 1229 ---
In the size range just above damsels, surgeon fish and soldier fish are a large part of the catch.

--- SENTENCE 1230 ---
The most common fish on Tutuila is the surgeonfish *Ctenochaetus striatus*.

--- SENTENCE 1231 ---
There is a quantitative measure of the impact of fishing on

different species, called "vulnerability," which has been detailed in peer-reviewed publications (Cheung et al. 2007).

--- SENTENCE 1232 ---

--- SENTENCE 1233 ---
Scores can range from zero to 100.

--- SENTENCE 1234 ---
Large reef fish like sharks commonly have vulnerabilities to fishing in the range of 80 to 90.

--- SENTENCE 1235 ---

--- SENTENCE 1236 ---
*C. striatus* has a vulnerability of 7*.

--- SENTENCE 1237 ---
C. striatus* can take heavy fishing pressure, but sharks can't.

--- SENTENCE 1238 ---

--- SENTENCE 1239 ---

--- SENTENCE 1240 ---

--- SENTENCE 1241 ---

--- SENTENCE 1242 ---
The combined evidence is powerful that fishing is the cause of the low levels of large reef fish in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1243 ---

--- SENTENCE 1244 ---
It takes much less fishing pressure to keep highly vulnerable large fish species like sharks at low levels, than it would to reduce abundances of the small, invulnerable species.

--- SENTENCE 1245 ---
Further, it takes time for species to recover when fishing is reduced.

--- SENTENCE 1246 ---
Small fish which mature quickly can recover quickly from fishing.

--- SENTENCE 1247 ---
Large fish which have late maturity take much longer to recover.

--- SENTENCE 1248 ---
The large fish on our reefs, sharks, humphead wrasse, and bumphead parrotfish are all protected now.

--- SENTENCE 1249 ---
Each of these three eats a different thing, sharks mostly eat fish, humphead wrasse are invertebrate eaters, and bumphead parrotfish eat algae and coral.

--- SENTENCE 1250 ---
Reducing the abundance of these three species could affect a wide variety of species on our reefs.

--- SENTENCE 1251 ---

--- SENTENCE 1252 ---
In general, the removal of predatory fish may have effects on reefs, but disastrous effects have not been documented.

--- SENTENCE 1253 ---
There is a body of literature documenting phase shifts where algae take over reefs when corals die.

--- SENTENCE 1254 ---
There is also a body of evidence showing that herbivores have effects on abundances of algae (as do nutrients).

--- SENTENCE 1255 ---
Many people have deduced that removal of herbivorous fish by fishing may make reefs vulnerable to phase shifts.

--- SENTENCE 1256 ---

--- SENTENCE 1257 ---
Still, as a precautionary measure it seems wise to try to have good populations of herbivorous fish.

--- SENTENCE 1258 ---

--- SENTENCE 1260 ---

--- SENTENCE 1261 ---
Batfish are also known to eat macroalgae, and they are present around Tutuila in at least small numbers.

--- SENTENCE 1262 ---
At this point we don't know whether our batfish populations are similar to those on unfished reefs or whether we have enough of them.

--- SENTENCE 1263 ---
Further, we have little information on medium-size fish, and thus don't know if they are overfished or not.

--- SENTENCE 1264 ---
Each species is very likely to be in a different condition, and that condition can't be predicted easily.

--- SENTENCE 1265 ---
A good example is presented by the results of

the only full stock assessments of coral reef fish ever done, done by Gerry Ault's group in Florida and the Caribbean.

--- SENTENCE 1266 ---
Each of the 35 species assessed was in a different condition, some overfished, some not.

--- SENTENCE 1267 ---
It is also quite possible to have undesirable ecosystem effects of fishing even though a stock is not overfished.

--- SENTENCE 1268 ---
For instance, fishing herbivorous fish down to MSY may greatly reduce a reef's resilience to phase shifts.

--- SENTENCE 1269 ---
Further, a recent paper (Rupert et al. 2013) documents that removing sharks leads to reductions in herbivorous fish, because the sharks eat medium size predators, and when there are few sharks there are more medium size predators which then reduce the populations of herbivores.

--- SENTENCE 1270 ---
There is a risk in removing sharks that the trophic cascade will result in fewer herbivores, leaving the reefs more vulnerable to phase shifts.

--- SENTENCE 1271 ---
Coral reefs in the Caribbean and Red Sea have large amounts of African or Middle-Eastern **dust** settle on them, carrying pollution, toxic chemicals, and disease organisms, at least one of which causes sea fan disease.

--- SENTENCE 1272 ---
Even Hawaii gets some dust from Asia.

--- SENTENCE 1273 ---
American Samoa, however, is far from any deserts, and has some of the cleanest air in the world coming onto it.

--- SENTENCE 1274 ---
**Impacts** and **threats** are terms used to describe the effects of humans on coral reefs.

--- SENTENCE 1275 ---
I use the term "impacts" to indicate things that have already happened to the reefs, so for instance, building the airport runway had an impact on the reefs.

--- SENTENCE 1276 ---
I use the term "threats" to indicate things that may have effects on reefs in the future.

--- SENTENCE 1277 ---
Reefs at Risk concludes that the greatest local impacts on reefs world wide have been overfishing and destructive fishing, sedimentation, nutrients, and chemical pollution.

--- SENTENCE 1278 ---
Other important impacts have come from coral diseases, crown-of-thorns starfish, introduced species, and African dust.

--- SENTENCE 1279 ---
In the case of coral diseases and crown-of-thorns, it is not clear what proportion of the causes are natural and what proportion due to humans, but it is likely that humans are part of the cause.

--- SENTENCE 1280 ---
Among the global impact factors, mass coral bleaching and acidification have already had impacts on world coral reefs.

--- SENTENCE 1281 ---
In 1998, the strongest El Nino on record, on top of global warming, heated the Indian Ocean to record levels, and caused the most severe coral mass mortality due to bleaching yet recorded, up to 90% mortality on some reefs, and mean coral cover for the Indian Ocean decreased from 38% to 20%.

--- SENTENCE 1282 ---
Acidification may already be slowing coral growth.

--- SENTENCE 1283 ---
All of these factors are threats for the future.

--- SENTENCE 1284 ---
The consensus among coral reef scientists at this point is that the greatest future threat to world reefs including American Samoa, is from mass coral bleaching caused by high water temperatures.

--- SENTENCE 1285 ---
Within 3 decades, annual summer water temperatures will have reached coral bleaching thresholds.

--- SENTENCE 1286 ---
It is not clear how fast or how much corals can acclimate or adapt.

--- SENTENCE 1287 ---
It is likely that some or many coral species will survive, but coral reefs as we know them, dominated by corals, will become rare, having been replaced by algae dominated areas.

--- SENTENCE 1288 ---
This has already happened in Florida and much of the Caribbean.

--- SENTENCE 1289 ---
The evidence presented above indicates that on the whole the reefs of American Samoa are now **healthier** than many of the other reefs of the world, but are not as good as they once were.

--- SENTENCE 1290 ---
In addition, there are several significant impacts on American Samoa reefs, probably the greatest of which was the 1978 crown-of-thorns outbreak, followed by the construction activities of dredging and filling around the 1940's, and overfishing of the largest reef fish.

--- SENTENCE 1291 ---
Earlier, the difference in **resilience** between the head and mouth of Vatia Bay was mentioned.

--- SENTENCE 1292 ---
Other examples of resilience or lack thereof include Fagatele Bay.

--- SENTENCE 1293 ---
The tsunami mobilized swaths of rubble, which after the tsunami were bare.

--- SENTENCE 1294 ---
Within 6 months the entire rubble flows were 100% covered with coralline algae.

--- SENTENCE 1295 ---
By now, they have many small corals (most less than 20 cm diameter) on the flows, showing that corals are now recovering rapidly.

--- SENTENCE 1296 ---
In contrast, one site monitored by Dr. Alison Green on the north side was completely covered with coralline algae but had no corals many years after its last disturbance.

--- SENTENCE 1297 ---
Coral cover appears to have recovered perhaps 2/3 of the way to the original level before the COTS outbreak in 1978, and continues to rise.

--- SENTENCE 1298 ---
If the coral community composition is not like it was before 1978, perhaps it is still slowly recovering.

--- SENTENCE 1299 ---
The great variety of information on resilience suggests that some areas are resilient, others are not, and many areas may be intermediate.

--- SENTENCE 1300 ---
The question arises, why are the American Samoa reefs healthier than average?

--- SENTENCE 1301 ---
A few management actions have been helpful, one of which was the ban on scuba spearfishing.

--- SENTENCE 1302 ---
The diversion of the cannery effluent outside the harbor caused an immediate drop in excess nutrients in the harbor.

--- SENTENCE 1303 ---
The building of a sewage system with two treatment plants, and the EPA project to reduce runoff from piggeries are other notable efforts that have helped reduce nutrient flow onto the reefs.

--- SENTENCE 1304 ---
The effort to reduce over-fertilization of the Pago soccer field was successful at ending red tides in the harbor.

--- SENTENCE 1305 ---
The work of the PNRS system has surely led to less runoff into the ocean than there would have been without PNRS.

--- SENTENCE 1306 ---
These and other actions have helped keep the reefs relatively healthy.

--- SENTENCE 1307 ---
In addition, the island is by pure chance nearly ideal for limiting human impacts on the reefs.

--- SENTENCE 1308 ---
The islands are extremely steep, with very little flat land.

--- SENTENCE 1309 ---
The forests are not commercially valuable.

--- SENTENCE 1310 ---
As a result, there is no logging industry, and the slopes are almost completely covered with the original forest.

--- SENTENCE 1311 ---
In addition, there is relatively little agriculture.

--- SENTENCE 1312 ---
Both of these mean that sediment and nutrient runoff is very minor compared to islands where there are large areas of logging and/or agriculture.

--- SENTENCE 1313 ---
In addition, we are a tiny set of islands in a giant sea, so runoff is diluted relatively quickly.

--- SENTENCE 1314 ---
Further, increasing support by the U.S. has meant that people have shifted away from fishing to eating store bought food, and fishing pressure is now relatively light.

--- SENTENCE 1315 ---
Likely, the relatively healthy condition of the reefs is due to the combination of no logging, little agriculture and light fishing with the management actions that have been taken.

Coral bleaching patterns among coral species**

Douglas Fenner, 2013

 There are several papers that report differences between species in susceptibility to bleaching.

--- SENTENCE 1447 ---
In general, they have reported that *Acropora*, *Millepora*, and *Pocillopora* are among the genera most vulnerable to bleaching.

--- SENTENCE 1448 ---
*Porites* is among the genera less subject to bleaching.

--- SENTENCE 1449 ---
Branching species are generally more subject to bleaching than massives.

--- SENTENCE 1450 ---
This fits with my observations in the back reef pools in the annual summer bleaching.

--- SENTENCE 1451 ---
*Acropora* and *Millepora* both bleach, while *Porites* doesn't bleach.

--- SENTENCE 1452 ---
Surprisingly, the *Pocillopora damicornis*, which is fairly common in some pools, doesn't bleach when *Acropora* and *Millepora* are bleached.

--- SENTENCE 1453 ---
*Millepora dichotoma*, of which there is one patch I know of in the airport pool, bleaches along with the *Acropora*.

--- SENTENCE 1454 ---
All species of *Acropora* seem to be roughly similar in how much they bleach, though *A. muricata* generally bleaches more than *A. pulchra*.

--- SENTENCE 1455 ---
When I first snorkeled in the Ofu back reef pools in early 2004, there were completely dead branching colonies that looked to me like they were *Millepora dichotoma*.

--- SENTENCE 1456 ---
All of it was dead, though I found a few tiny spots that were alive.

--- SENTENCE 1457 ---
Now, there are *Millepora dichotoma* colonies all over.

--- SENTENCE 1458 ---
I think that bleaching killed the colonies, most likely in early 2003 or 2002.

--- SENTENCE 1459 ---
No other colonies were dead back then.

--- SENTENCE 1460 ---
Now, there is at least one area in which almost all staghorn is dead in the Hurricane House pool, to the left from the entry point.

--- SENTENCE 1461 ---
Since other species were not affected and they are not broken, it was almost surely due to bleaching.

--- SENTENCE 1462 ---
People say that corals in the Ofu pools don't bleach but I have often seen some of the *Acropora* lightly frosted with white, and I am pretty sure the *M. dichotoma* and staghorns were killed by bleaching.

--- SENTENCE 1463 ---
So although they may be more temperature tolerant, some have been killed by bleaching.

--- SENTENCE 1464 ---
At least twice I have seen very mild bleaching on reef slopes at Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 1465 ---

--- SENTENCE 1466 ---

--- SENTENCE 1467 ---

--- SENTENCE 1468 ---

--- SENTENCE 1469 ---

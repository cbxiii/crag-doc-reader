**American Samoa is experiencing rapid relative sea level rise due to increases in global sea level and signifcant post-2009 earthquake land subsidence, endangering homes and critical infrastructure.

--- SENTENCE 4 ---
Wave and water-level observations collected over a fringing reef at Faga'itua Bay, American Samoa, in 2017 reveal depth-limited shoreline sea-swell wave heights over the range of conditions sampled.

--- SENTENCE 5 ---
Using feld data to calibrate a one-dimensional, phase-resolving nonhydrostatic wave model (SWASH), we examine the infuence of water level on wave heights over the reef for a range of current and future sea levels.

--- SENTENCE 6 ---

--- SENTENCE 7 ---
Model results provide insight into how and at what reef depths rising sea levels reduce reef capacity to dissipate wave energy, compounding shoreline threats.

--- SENTENCE 8 ---
This study aims to bring increased attention to the immediate threats to American Samoa's way of life, and to demonstrate the utility of SWASH for extrapolating wave transformation to future sea level.

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
Becker et al. (2014)[16](#page-10-15) considered observations from three fringing reefs in the Pacifc and found that water level over the reef fats controls the amount of shoreline SS wave energy.

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
Te dependence of SS shoreline wave heights on water level varies from site to site in part because the amount of dissipation depends on reef width and bottom roughness in addition to water depth over the reef fat.

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
While fringing reefs are efective at damping SS waves, longer period infragravity waves (25 to 200 s[\)17,](#page-11-0) far infragravity waves (200 to 1000 s[\)18,](#page-11-1) and wave setup[16](#page-10-15) can be energetic components of wave-driven shoreline water levels.

--- SENTENCE 39 ---
Observations at Ipan reef, Guam, demonstrated that higher water levels increased the normal mode frequency of the fringing reef fat, and allowed for modal excitation of water levels in the far infragravity frequency band by wave-group forcing.

--- SENTENCE 40 ---
The reef-flat modes have anti-nodes at the shoreline, resulting in increased low-frequency shoreline water level variation and therefore potential for flooding due to elevated water levels<sup>18</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 41 ---
When the combination of reef geometry and water level does not lead to the excitation of reef modes, however, coral reefs have been demonstrated to reduce low-frequency motion on the reef flat and wave runup at the shoreline<sup>19</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
Reef-fringed island shorelines differ from continental sandy beach shorelines in that runup amplitudes on sloping sand beaches increase with steepening beach slope, but not necessarily with water level.

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
For example, the well-established statistical runup model of Stockdon et al.

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
(2006)<sup>20</sup> does not include a water level dependence, although beach slope may change with water level on non-uniform beach profiles.

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
Climate change related projections of wave runup and coastal flooding on sandy beach shorelines thus depend on expected changes in the incident wave climate, e.g., due to changing storm patterns, or changes in shoreline morphology.

--- SENTENCE 46 ---
The ability of island reefs to suppress wave energy is water-level dependent, and hence the influence of SLR may be two-fold, causing both higher still water levels as well as higher wave energy.

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
The inability of live reefs to accrete and keep pace with SLR is an additional potential concern.

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
Absent other external stressors, relative SLR alone may potentially encourage reef growth, since reef accretion rates on shallow reef flats often are constrained by low tide and the lack of accommodation space<sup>21</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
Conversely, continued ocean warming and acidification will likely lead to coral bleaching and mortality, inhibiting reef accretion<sup>22</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
For the purposes of this study, we assume the reef bathymetry is fixed, and that relative SLR will increase the water depth over the fringing reef.

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
Our projections are more closely tied to the water level over the fringing reef than to time, leaving room for the uncertainty in reef accretion and emissions pathway-dependent SLR.

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
Here we investigate how rising sea level over a fringing reef may affect nearshore sea-swell wave heights, lower frequency wave heights and breaking-wave setup, and highlight possible impacts of total water level changes on American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
A goal of this work is to support calls for immediate mitigation and adaptation planning and lay the groundwork for forecasting future flooding events that result from the combination of elevated water level and swell waves.

--- SENTENCE 54 ---
We use a one-dimensional, phase-resolving non-hydrostatic wave model to emulate wave transformation over a fringing reef study site for a range of present and future sea levels.

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
The work extends a similar coastal flooding analysis conducted on reef-fringed shorelines in the Marshall Islands<sup>23</sup>; however, in this study the influence of increasing sea level on runup, and specifically SS wave energy, is explicitly modeled.

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
As in Merrifield et al. (2014)<sup>23</sup>, we hindcast total water level to assess the influence of recent relative sea-level rise at American Samoa on total water levels.

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
We examine the effects of land subsidence associated with the September 2009 Samoa-Tonga earthquake doublet.

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
We focus on total water levels that are likely to reach the elevation of the existing coastal roadway that is the main transportation artery for Tutuila.

#### Methods Field survey

Our study site is the fringing reef at Faga'itua Bay, a southern-facing shoreline in eastern Tutuila and home to five villages (Fig. 1a).

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
The Faga'itua Bay morphology is representative of other embayed fringing reefs around the island.

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
The reef varies in width from approximately 260 to 600 m, and in fore reef slope for an average slope of approximately 1/6 at our site.

--- SENTENCE 61 ---
Spur and grooves on the fore reef extend up to the reef crest, which is still exposed at the lowest tides.

--- SENTENCE 62 ---
The reef flat is shallow (<2 m) and mainly reef rock.

--- SENTENCE 63 ---
The shoreline around the bay is comprised of narrow (0-10 m) sandy beaches backed by rocky, vegetated slopes up to the main coastal road, which has a nominal elevation varying from 1.5 to 5 m (3.4 m at our transect location) above MSL.

--- SENTENCE 64 ---
Wave breaking is typically confined to a narrow breaking zone at the outer edge of the reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
From April to October 2017, three bottom-mounted pressure sensors (Sea-Bird SBE26plus) were deployed across the reef at Fagaʿitua Bay.

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
The sensors were located offshore of the steeply sloping shoreline in deployment-averaged water depths of 0.57 m near the shore (A), 1.00 m at mid reef (B), and 8.55 m on the steep fore reef (C) (Fig. 1b).

--- SENTENCE 67 ---
Depths are referenced to MSL based on comparison with the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA)

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
Pago Pago tide gauge on Tutuila during the sampling period.

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
To maximize battery life, measurements were collected at 1 Hz for 1.5 h bursts every 3 h. Intermediate water depth wave data from 2014 to present day is available from the Coastal Data Information Program's (CDIP) buoy station 189 at Aunu'u.

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
At our study site, the sandy beach has been recently lost to rising relative sea level.

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
We convert pressure time series to surface elevation using first-order linear wave theory, from which we calculate wave spectra and bulk wave statistics.

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
The 1.5-h bursts are broken into 30-min records and detided by removing the dominant M2 tidal component.

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
Atmospheric pressure measurements collected every 6 min from NOAA's National Data Buoy Center (NDBC) station NSTP6—1770000 at Pago Pago are interpolated to match sensor samples and removed from total pressure measurements.

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
For this study, we define the low frequency (LF) band, encompassing infragravity and far infragravity waves bands, from 0.001 to 0.04 Hz (periods of 25–1000 s) and the sea-swell (SS) band from 0.04 to 0.3 Hz (3.33–25 s).

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
Records with water depth < 0.3 m are excluded from analysis.

--- SENTENCE 76 ---
At Faga'itua Bay, we have 2100 30-min records during which both the nearshore (A) and reef face (C) sensors were operational.

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
The reef flat (B) sensor had issues with timing and stability during deployment, so we use the observations for verification of the bathymetric depth only.

--- SENTENCE 78 ---

#### Bathymetry

We specify a 1-m resolution cross-shore bathymetry transect at Faga'itua using a preliminary digital elevation model (DEM) provided by NOAA.

--- SENTENCE 86 ---
We verify that the reef bathymetry at Faga'itua appears to closely match aerial photographs of alongshore variations including reef channels and smaller spur and groove features on the fore reef.

--- SENTENCE 87 ---
Additionally, the relative depths of the 3 reef sensors match estimates of the mean water depths from observations to within 0.3 m. For the numerical modeling described in subsequent sections, we use a 500-m long, 1-dimensional grid from 32-m water depth to 10 m above MSL.

--- SENTENCE 88 ---
We vary the cross-shore grid spacing from 2 m at the seaward boundary to 0.04 m at the shoreward boundary using linear interpolation.

--- SENTENCE 89 ---
The input bathymetry has 3 grid points in the alongshore direction with 1-m spacing, and each cross-shore transect is identical.

--- SENTENCE 90 ---
This single transect is derived from an average of parallel cross-shore transects spanning 100 m in the alongshore, spaced every 2 m (Fig. 1c).

--- SENTENCE 91 ---
Several transects include a rock protrusion but its impact on the final average transect are negligible.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
We adjust the simulated still water level for each model run to match the reef face (sensor C) location water depth with the 30-min average water level for that observation period.

#### 1-D Numerical modeling of nearshore waves using SWASH

Wave transformation across the Fagaʻitua reef is simulated by the phase-resolving non-hydrostatic wave model Simulating WAves till SHore (SWASH) version 10.01.

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
SWASH has been used to investigate waves over reefs with validation using laboratory experiments<sup>25–27</sup> and field observations<sup>28–31</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
SWASH is a nonhydrostatic waveflow model using the Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes Eqs.

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
We implement SWASH on a 1-dimensional transect but use the 2-dimensional, nonhydrostatic mode of SWASH to employ variable cross-shore grid spacing described above, following Fiedler et al.  $(2018)^{33}$ .

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
We use 10 vertical layers and the default breaking wave parameter<sup>25</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 97 ---
Turbulent mixing was activated using the vertical  $k-\epsilon$  model<sup>34</sup> with default parameters.

--- SENTENCE 98 ---
A background viscosity of  $1*10^{-4}$  is assumed.

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
Following Risandi et al.  $(2020)^{28}$ , who studied a fringing reef in southwestern Australia with visually similar coral cover, we account for friction between the flow and rough bed using a constant friction factor of 0.05.

--- SENTENCE 100 ---
SWASH is run with 15 min of spin-up time to allow breaking-wave setup to stabilize followed by 30 min of model time.

--- SENTENCE 101 ---
We analyze the simulated sea surface elevation to compute wave spectra and significant wave heights in both the LF and SS frequency bands following the same methods used for the observations.

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
The default SWASH method is used for computing wave setup.

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
At the offshore boundary we initiate simulations with 30-min average wave spectra computed from the reef face (C) sensor observations, back-refracted from observation depth ( $\sim$ 8 m) to the model boundary depth (32 m) using linear wave theory.

--- SENTENCE 104 ---
We neglect SS wave energy associated with reflections at the fore reef based on the findings of Péquignet et al.

--- SENTENCE 105 ---
(2011)<sup>35</sup> for a similar steeply sloping reef face.

--- SENTENCE 106 ---
Time series of SS waves at the boundary are obtained from the back-refracted wave spectra assuming random wave phase.

--- SENTENCE 107 ---
Of the 2100 observational records, a sample of 200 was chosen for numerical simulation and comparison.

--- SENTENCE 108 ---
Of the 200 records chosen for the sample, 49 are the largest observed nearshore (A) water level records with  $\bar{h}_A > 0.9$  m to ensure that we compare model behavior at the highest observed water levels.

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
The remaining 151 are randomly chosen to span the range of observed water levels and incident wave energy.

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
Each of the 200 30-min incident wave spectra are simulated using SWASH with observed water levels for model validation.

--- SENTENCE 111 ---
The validated model is extended to higher water levels by maintaining the wave and tide conditions used in the historical hindcasts and increasing the still water level (SWL) (e.g., due to tides, non-tidal residual sea level, SLR, and vertical land motion) to 0.6–2.0 m above current MSL.

--- SENTENCE 112 ---
The range of SLR is chosen to extend observed water levels to end of century projections for American Samoa made by Baizeau et al.

--- SENTENCE 113 ---
(2023)<sup>9</sup>, based on a combination of sea level rise resulting from the ITF intermediate emissions scenario and vertical land motion estimates from Han et al. (2019)<sup>6</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 114 ---
(2023)<sup>9</sup> project 0.8249 m of relative SLR by 2070 and 1.4112 m by 2100, relative to 2005 MSL.

--- SENTENCE 115 ---
We track the modeled runup line with a threshold depth of at least 0.01 m.

#### Nearshore setup and sea-swell wave hindcasts

We perform a wave hindcast (January 1979 through May 2023) at Fagaʿitua Bay using observed water levels at the Pago Pago tide gauge, a deep water wave hindcast, and a refraction model over local bathymetry to estimate reef face (sensor C) significant wave height.

--- SENTENCE 116 ---
Empirical relationships then are used to estimate breaking-wave setup and nearshore SS wave height (sensor A).

--- SENTENCE 117 ---
We obtain a wave hindcast dating back to 1979 for the reef face location (sensor C) using an open-source WWIII-generated wave hindcast <sup>36</sup> and a linear spectral refraction model<sup>37</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 118 ---

directional spectra around American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 119 ---
We obtain spectra from the output model grid point located at  $-14.53^{\circ}$ N,  $189.53^{\circ}$ E, just to the south of the CDIP Aunu'u buoy, against which we validate the wave hindcast for periods of overlap.

--- SENTENCE 120 ---
We use a non-stationary, linear spectral refraction wave model<sup>37</sup> to generate a transfer matrix that is both a function of frequency and wave direction.

--- SENTENCE 121 ---
The matrix is constrained with a cutoff of 130 degrees based on the geometry of Faga'itua Bay to prevent errant excess energy making it to our reef face location.

--- SENTENCE 122 ---
We bias-correct the model using our observations from the 2017 field deployment.

#### Results

The wave field at Faga'itua reef exhibits strong SS energy dissipation and LF energy propagation across the reef flat (Fig. 2).

--- SENTENCE 132 ---
Reef face (sensor C) SS significant wave heights ( $H_{SS}$ ) range between 0.3 and 1.8 m, while nearshore (sensor A)  $H_{SS}$  never exceed 0.3 m (Fig. 2b).

--- SENTENCE 133 ---
Nearshore LF significant wave heights often equal or exceed the SS counterpart, and nearshore LF energy exceeds the LF energy observed at the reef face (Fig.

--- SENTENCE 134 ---
The nearshore  $H_{SS}$  scales with the mean water depth over the reef flat (Fig. 3),

--- SENTENCE 135 ---
similar to the reefs studied for example in Becker et al.  $(2014)^{16}$ .

--- SENTENCE 136 ---
Nearshore  $H_{SS}$  are depth limited for the considered range of offshore wave conditions at Faga'itua.

--- SENTENCE 137 ---
The depth limitation is set as waves break at the outer reef, i.e., waves break when their heights exceed a certain fraction of the water depth.

--- SENTENCE 138 ---
Additional dissipation occurs as waves travel shoreward of the break zone due to bottom friction over the rough reef substrate and turbulent energy loss in the broken wave rollers, which both depend to some extent on water depth.

--- SENTENCE 139 ---
The water level variations on the reef in turn are set by the tide, non-tidal residual sea level effects, and wave setup.

--- SENTENCE 140 ---
For a given reef flat water level, the nearshore  $H_{SS}$  exhibits a dependence on the offshore wave height; however, this effect is weak compared to the water-level dependence.

--- SENTENCE 141 ---
As in Merrifield et al.  $(2014)^{23}$ , observed nearshore  $H_{SS}$  may be estimated using a quadratic fit to mean water level, but to what extent may this be extrapolated to higher, unobserved water levels?

#### Discussion

Wave and water level observations at a fringing reef in Faga'itua Bay on Tutuila island in American Samoa reveal that under current sea level, SS significant wave height at the shoreline is controlled by water depth over the reef, and the extreme wave-driven water levels are dominated by breaking-wave setup and LF motions.

--- SENTENCE 184 ---
The greater the water level over the fringing reef, the greater the observed nearshore SS significant wave heights, consistent with depth-limited breaking.

--- SENTENCE 185 ---
Deep water offshore wave heights determine the amount of breaking-wave setup, which increases the nearshore water level and therefore increases the nearshore SS wave height.

--- SENTENCE 192 ---
To assess how changes in relative sea level affect shoreline SS wave heights, we use SWASH, a phase-resolving nonhydrostatic numerical model applied at a cross-reef transect in Faga'itua Bay.

--- SENTENCE 193 ---
Waves are transformed over the fringing reef with past, present, and future water levels.

--- SENTENCE 194 ---
We find that the model predicts that the maximum nearshore SS significant wave height for a given water depth increases quadratically with water depth.

--- SENTENCE 195 ---
As water levels exceed ~1.2 m mean water depth, the dependence on offshore wave height increases as smaller waves are able to propagate across the reef without breaking.

--- SENTENCE 196 ---
Over the past 50 years, rising sea levels have contributed significantly to amplified shoreline wave energy at Faga'itua Bay.

--- SENTENCE 197 ---
From 1979 through September 2009, we estimate that the daily maximum nearshore SS significant wave height increased at a rate of 0.01 m/decade for an SLR of 0.04 m/decade; from October 2009 through May 2023, that rate jumped to 0.06 m/decade for an SLR of 0.16 m/decade.

--- SENTENCE 198 ---
The effects of land subsidence associated with the earthquakes, most recently measured at 6 to 9 mm/yr, are expected to continue for several decades.

--- SENTENCE 199 ---
Modeling results suggest that the destructive potential of swells incident on fringing reef shorelines of American Samoa will increase as relative sea level increases.

--- SENTENCE 200 ---
The extreme water level becomes dominated by SS wave heights as some SS waves cease to break at the reef crest.

--- SENTENCE 201 ---
In addition to the flood risk associated with relative sea level increases projected for American Samoa, our results raise concerns about the flood risk associated with the increase in the 2% exceedance shoreline runup, and a growing dominance of SS energy.

--- SENTENCE 202 ---
Given the proximity of built infrastructure to the shoreline at American Samoa, the combined impacts of increased waves and water levels pose a considerable challenge for coastal defense.

--- SENTENCE 203 ---
For example, we use the SWASH results to illustrate how rising waves and water levels will impact the maximum wave pressure on a vertical breakwater (Fig. 9).

--- SENTENCE 204 ---
We use Goda's (1974)<sup>40</sup> formulation for calculating the maximum wave pressure on a hypothetical vertical breakwater built nearshore (at sensor A's location) as a function of sea level, assuming normal incidence.

$$p_1 = 1.8\rho g H_S^A(\alpha_1 + \frac{2h}{1.8H_S^A}) \tag{7}$$

--- SENTENCE 220 ---

--- SENTENCE 221 ---
Reef accretion and growth relative to rising sea level will limit increases in shoreline wave energy.

--- SENTENCE 222 ---

--- SENTENCE 223 ---

--- SENTENCE 225 ---

--- SENTENCE 226 ---
American Samoa's relative sea level continues to rise, increasing the risk to coastal infrastructure from damaging wave events.

--- SENTENCE 227 ---
The majority of American Samoa's housing and infrastructure will face significant threats from the relative SLR and associated increases in nearshore SS wave energy.

--- SENTENCE 228 ---
Coastal erosion rates have increased dramatically due to rapid relative SLR<sup>42</sup>, causing a cascade of deleterious impacts: loss of most sandy beaches around the island, loss of coastal land, damage to main roads, and the failures of seawalls that have hardened much of the coastline of Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 229 ---
Current passive flood modeling estimates based on relative sea level projections alone predict that much of American Samoa's most critical infrastructure, including the only international airport and much of the main coastal road, will be threatened by 2100 under the intermediate emissions scenario resulting in 1.4 m relative SLR.

--- SENTENCE 230 ---
Our model results suggest that the impacts under this amount of relative SLR will be greater because these estimates have not taken into account the increase in potentially destructive SS wave energy at the shoreline.

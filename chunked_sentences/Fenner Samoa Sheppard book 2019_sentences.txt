--- SENTENCE 3 ---
Geography, Topography, Geological Description

The Samoan Archipelago consists of a nearly east—west chain of islands plus one small atoll to the north.

--- SENTENCE 4 ---
It is located in the central South Pacific, about 14 degrees south of the equator.

--- SENTENCE 5 ---
The western half of the chain is the Independent State of Samoa (previously called Western Samoa), and the eastern half is the US Territory of American Samoa (Fig. 28.1).

--- SENTENCE 6 ---
Independent Samoa includes the western most island which is also the largest, Savai'i, and Upolu, the second largest island.

--- SENTENCE 7 ---
American Samoa's largest island is Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 8 ---
Just south of that is Aunu'u, east of which is a group of three small islands called "Manu'a," two of which are connected by a bridge and surrounded by a single continuous reef.

--- SENTENCE 9 ---
To the east of that lies a small atoll, Rose (Muliava), and to the north lies Swains Island which is a slightly raised atoll with a closed, brackish lagoon.

--- SENTENCE 10 ---
The latter are all parts of American Samoa (see Fig. 28.1).

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
The archipelago rests on a hotspot on the Pacific Plate, which moves westward about 7 cm per year.

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
The hotspot is presently located under an active submarine volcano named Vailulu'u located east of Ta'u.

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
Ta'u is 70,000-years old, and the progression of islands westwards increase in age to 5 million years old at Savai'i (Koppers et al., 2008), consistent with the hotspot and plate motion hypothesis.

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
Younger larva eruptions occur on some of the older islands.

--- SENTENCE 15 ---
Savai'i is a broad island with gentle slopes, but the rest of the volcanic high islands are mostly quite steep, with relatively frequent small landslides and stream deltas composed of small basalt rocks on top of the carbonate reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
The south side of Ta'u is crescent shaped and was produced by a gigantic slide of rock that has been dated to 22,000 years ago (Williams et al., 2014).

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
Ofu and Olosega are the remains of a single volcano from which the north and south sides slid off into deep water (Hart & Jackson, 2014).

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
Tutuila and Ofu-Olosega have submerged shelves around them, about 30–100 m deep, and up to 1–4km wide.

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
At the outer edge the shelf drops off steeply, in some areas as a vertical cliff extending from 100 to about 450 m deep.

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
The shelf has an interrupted ring of banks inward from the edge which appears to be a drowned barrier reef.

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
On southwestern Tutuila, there are lava flows on top of the shelf that are younger than the shelf (Fig. 28.2).

--- SENTENCE 22 ---
Some of the island shorelines in the archipelago have fringing reefs (Figs. 28.3 and 28.4A–C.).

--- SENTENCE 23 ---
Swains Island also is surrounded by fringing reef, extending as reef flat from the shoreline.

--- SENTENCE 24 ---
On Tutuila, reef flats (Fig. 28.4B) extend along about half the coast and average about 116 m wide (Gelfenbaum, Apotsos, Stevens, & Jaffe, 2011).

--- SENTENCE 25 ---
Swains does not have coral on its reef flat, which appears mostly to be slightly higher than extreme low tide levels.

--- SENTENCE 26 ---
Typically, the reef crest has a mixture of corals and coralline algae although those of Rose Atoll and Swains Island are composed of only coralline algae.

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
In most areas, the upper forereef slope is gradual, with deep surge channels that run down the slope (Fig. 28.4C).

--- SENTENCE 28 ---
The reef slope often has the highest coral cover on the reef, and ends at about 10–30 m deep where there is a nearly flat shelf, but extends further downward on Ta'u, Swains, and Rose (Vroom, Page, Kenyon, & Brainard, 2006; Fig. 28.3).

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
For more information on the geology of American Samoa and additional references, see Birkeland et al. (2008), Fenner (2016), and Hart et al. (2004) Hart and Jackson (2014).

# 28.2 PHYSICAL OCEANOGRAPHY

During the austral winter, trade winds blow the east, and more wave energy strikes the south coast than the north.

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
American Samoa lies in the South Equatorial Current, which has warm water flowing westward, and a deep thermocline at about 120–200 m deep.

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
Sea surface temperatures in the Samoan archipelago average about 29–30°C in the austral summer and 27–28°C in the austral winter.

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
Conditions differ with latitude, so the sea surface temperature (SST) at Swains is warmest.

--- SENTENCE 60 ---

# MAJOR COASTAL AND SHALLOW HABITATS

#### 28.4.1 **Coral Reefs**

Most of the coral reefs of the Samoan archipelago are fringing, but Rose Atoll (Muliava) is an atoll with a lagoon, Tutuila is surrounded by a drowned barrier reef in addition to the fringing reef, and Upolu and Savai'i have wider barrier reefs with very shallow lagoons.

--- SENTENCE 64 ---
Ofu has natural back-reef pools with a high diversity of corals, dominated by massive Porites (Craig, Birkeland, & Belliveau, 2001).

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
The coral communities of dredged back-reef pools on Tutuila are typically dominated by staghorn and finger corals (Fig. 28.5A), have low diversity, and sandy areas.

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
The forereef slopes in some areas are more gentle and encrusting *Montipora* is the most common coral (Fig. 28.5B).

--- SENTENCE 67 ---
Slopes often have a diversity of corals and can reach high coral cover (Fig.

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
The reef slopes of American Samoa have few vertical "walls."

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
The first transect ever taken on coral reefs in the Pacific (and second in the world) was taken by Alfred Mayor on reef flats in Pago Pago Harbor in 1917.

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
At that time, it had abundant coral.

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
It had less coral in 1974, which continued to decline until the 1990s, by which time there was a borrow pit near shore, most of the reef flat was covered with dead coral rubble, and only the crest area had live coral.

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
It has remained that way since then (Birkeland, Green, Fenner, Squair, & Dahl, 2013).

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
Another transect taken at the same time in the harbor by Cary originally had a high soft coral cover, but by the time that transect was repeated in 2003, there was no soft coral cover left (see Craig, DiDonato, Fenner, & Hawkins, 2005).

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
All reefs in the inner half of the harbor have been dredged, built over, or are now degraded.

### 28.4.1.1 Diversity

A total of about 2700 marine species have been reported in American Samoa so far (listed by group, in Fenner et al., 2008), but that figure is heavily dependent on the amount of effort expended on each group.

--- SENTENCE 139 ---
About 1000 fish species have been reported (Wass, 1984; P. Brown personal communication) and with only few additional species being reported, the fish fauna is probably well known.

--- SENTENCE 140 ---
A field guidebook illustrates common invertebrates on the reef flats (Madrigal, 1999).

--- SENTENCE 141 ---
Fenner (2016) has verified only about 250 corals so far from skeletons, from the 400 names that have been claimed for the region.

--- SENTENCE 142 ---
Brown (2011) reported 385 species of gastropods in 50 families; about 222 species of eukaryote algae and two species of seagrass are known and 360 species of eukaryote algae, while three species of seagrass are known from Independent Samoa (Skelton, 2003; Skelton & South, 2004, 2006, 2007; P. Skelton, personal communication).

--- SENTENCE 143 ---
If blue-green algae (cyanobacteria) are added, the total for the archipelago is probably to be about 500 species (P. Skelton, personal communication).

--- SENTENCE 144 ---
American Samoa has 45% of the coral species that the Coral Triangle has, 42% of the coral species on reef crests, and 23% of the coral species on reef flats (Karlson, Cornell, & Hughes, 2004).

--- SENTENCE 145 ---
Kendall et al. (2011) distinguished 30 distinct biogeographic regions within the archipelago, and 51 hotspots within it.

--- SENTENCE 146 ---
They reported that the sparsely populated islands of Manu'a (Ofu-Olosega and Ta'u) and Savai'i generally had higher values of several variables than the more densely populated islands of Tutuila and Upolu, and within Upolu and Tutuila the densely populated areas around Apia (the only city in the archipelago) and Pago Pago Harbor have many of the sites with the lowest values of the measured variables.

--- SENTENCE 147 ---
Fish showed generally smaller biogeographic differences among sites than corals.

--- SENTENCE 148 ---
The relatively isolated Swains Island had very different fish and coral communities.

--- SENTENCE 149 ---
Rose Atoll, a small island isolated from upstream larval sources had relatively low coral and fish richness.

--- SENTENCE 150 ---
There were general trends in the archipelago of increasing coral cover, richness, and fish biomass toward the west, consistent with islands to the west being downstream from those to the east (Kendall, Poti, Wynne, Kinian, & Bauer, 2011), and being larger.

--- SENTENCE 151 ---
Giant clams are uncommon to rare on outer reef slopes of all of the islands of American Samoa, but are common on pinnacles in the lagoon at Rose Atoll (Muliava) (Green & Craig, 1999).

--- SENTENCE 152 ---
Giant clams continue to be rare outside Rose Atoll lagoon (Brainard et al., 2008).

--- SENTENCE 153 ---
They are least common on the most densely populated islands of Upolu and Tutuila, which supports the view that they are overfished there (Green & Craig, 1999).

--- SENTENCE 154 ---
Fishermen have reported a decline in giant clam stocks, and fishing statistics show a decline in catch over two decades (references in Green & Craig, 1999).

--- SENTENCE 155 ---
The shelled cephalopod, Nautilus pompilius, is found in the Samoas.

--- SENTENCE 156 ---
Shells found are slightly different from those in other areas (Saunders, Bond, & Itano, 1989).

--- SENTENCE 157 ---
Surveys found that American Samoa had 1.48 Nautilus per km, which was not significantly different from Fiji and Australia (Barord et al., 2014).

--- SENTENCE 158 ---
A green colonial ascidian (sea squirt), *Diplosoma similis*, was recorded to have covered large areas of the reef at Swains in 2006 and 2008, growing over living coral (Vargas-Ángel, Godwin, Asher, & Brainard, 2009).

--- SENTENCE 159 ---
The average ascidian cover increased from near zero in 2004 to over 15% in 2006, then over 20% in 2008, and returned to near zero in 2010.

--- SENTENCE 160 ---
However, live coral cover remained steady from 2004 to 2010 (Pacific Islands Fisheries Science Center, 2011a).

--- SENTENCE 161 ---
Filter feeders are uncommon and/or small in American Samoa, outside of the harbor.

--- SENTENCE 162 ---
Filter feeding bioeroders such as boring sponges and burrowing clams are also uncommon, as are large urchins.

--- SENTENCE 163 ---
There appears to be low levels of bioerosion.

--- SENTENCE 164 ---
In the harbor, there are filter feeding oysters along the water line and a sponge community in deep water, feeding on water green with plankton.

--- SENTENCE 165 ---
Green sea turtles nest on Rose Atoll, with a very small number nesting on the other islands of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 166 ---
Most of those adults spend most of the year feeding in Fiji, where seagrass beds are much larger and denser than in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 167 ---
Immature hawksbill turtles can be seen in shallow water, but adults are rarely seen.

--- SENTENCE 168 ---
Eleven species of marine mammals have been found in American Samoa, such as resident porpoises.

--- SENTENCE 169 ---
Humpback whales which feed off Antarctica in the austral summer—winter in the Samoan Archipelago where they calve and mate (citations in Fenner et al., 2008; Craig, 2009).

### 28.4.1.2 Disease

Coral diseases are widespread in American Samoa and present at most sites (Work & Rameyer, 2005), but only a very small proportion of colonies are affected (0.14%).

--- SENTENCE 170 ---
So far, a total of 15 coral diseases have been reported.

--- SENTENCE 171 ---
White syndrome (which is often fatal to the coral) and growth anomalies are the most common diseases on Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 172 ---
Disease prevalence (percentage of colonies infected) varies among coral genera, with *Acropora* having the highest prevalence, and among islands, with growth anomalies most common on Ta'u and Tutuila and least common at Rose (Aeby, Work, Fenner, & DiDonato, 2009; Fenner et al., 2008).

--- SENTENCE 173 ---
On all islands of American Samoa disease was found at an average of 63% of all sites, with Rose, Ta'u, and Swains having 81%, 88%, and 100% of sites affected, respectively.

--- SENTENCE 174 ---
Only 55% of sites on Tutuila, Ofu, and Olosega had disease.

--- SENTENCE 175 ---
The mean prevalence was 0.34% which is at least an order of magnitude lower than in the Caribbean and Great Barrier Reef.

--- SENTENCE 176 ---
Four groups of disease state were found: bleaching, growth anomalies, band disease, and "other lesions."

--- SENTENCE 177 ---
Growth anomalies were most common around Ofu-Olosega and affected *Acropora abrotanoides* and *Acropora cytherea* the most.

--- SENTENCE 178 ---
Bleaching was most common around Ofu-Olosega and all lesions were small and focal.

--- SENTENCE 179 ---
Only six instances of tissue loss were recorded, and one of black band.

--- SENTENCE 180 ---
Of the 45 coral genera recorded in the study, 15 showed disease and/or predation.

--- SENTENCE 181 ---
Acropora*, *Astreopora*, and *Montastrea* were the most commonly affected genera.

--- SENTENCE 182 ---
*Acanthaster* (starfish) and *Drupella* (snail) predation was most common on Rose followed by Swains, with lower frequencies around Tutuila and Ofu-Olosega (Brainard et al., 2008).

--- SENTENCE 183 ---
The most recent survey of diseases, in 2015, also found a low percentage of colonies with disease (NOAA PIFSC, 2015).

--- SENTENCE 184 ---
Acropora white syndrome was less prevalent in Tutuila, where coral diversity is high, than in the Northwestern Hawaiian Islands, where coral diversity is low.

--- SENTENCE 185 ---
The severity of this disease and mortality rates were higher in Hawaii than American Samoa (Aeby, Bourne, Wilson, & Work, 2011), which is consistent with the more general finding that disease prevalence is reduced in more diverse ecosystems.

--- SENTENCE 186 ---
One disease in American Samoa, a dark color patch on some species of *Pavona*, was found to be associated with fungal filaments growing up from the skeleton into the coral tissue (Work, Aeby, & Coles, 2008; Work, Aeby, Stanton, & Fenner, 2008).

--- SENTENCE 187 ---
The distribution, morphological variety, and prevalence of growth anomalies on *Acropora* colonies in American Samoa are greater than in Hawaii and Johnston Atoll (Work, Aeby, & Coles, 2008; Work, Aeby, Stanton, & Fenner, 2008).

--- SENTENCE 188 ---
During coral bleaching events in 2002 and 2003, formerly rare coral diseases such as white syndrome were observed to be even more common (Craig et al., 2005).

--- SENTENCE 189 ---
A few small outbreaks of disease have been recorded on Tutuila in recent years.

--- SENTENCE 190 ---
An outbreak of a disease that produced white and yellow areas on a group of about 100 *Porites rus* colonies in the back-reef pool in front of Vaoto Lodge on Ofu was observed in November 2011.

--- SENTENCE 191 ---
A few years later, there were only a few little patches of living tissue remaining on a few colonies.

--- SENTENCE 192 ---
Disease is common on *Pocillopora* colonies on reef slopes, as are dead colonies.

--- SENTENCE 193 ---
It appears that this disease is endemic and continuous (Fenner, 2011).

--- SENTENCE 194 ---
Coralline algae are also subject to diseases, with five such diseases known in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 195 ---
Coralline lethal orange disease (CLOD) is by far (97%) the most common disease recorded.

--- SENTENCE 196 ---
It is by far the most abundant on Ofu-Olosega, followed by Tutuila, with much less at Ta'u, less still at Rose, and none at Swains.

--- SENTENCE 197 ---
CLOD cases were found in clusters (Brainard et al., 2008).

--- SENTENCE 198 ---
American Samoa has the most coralline algae disease of any of the US Pacific Island areas, and more than 70% of all lesions observed in a study of the 42 US Pacific Islands were found in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 199 ---
However, coralline algae cover in American Samoa is so high that the amount of disease per unit of coralline algae cover is not as high as in the southern Marianas.

--- SENTENCE 200 ---
Within American Samoa, the highest amount of disease per unit of coralline algae was on

Tutuila, Ofu-Olosega, and Ta'u, and the lowest at Rose Atoll.

--- SENTENCE 201 ---
Coralline algae disease increased on Rose Atoll from 2006 to 2008, but changes were not statistically significant on other islands.

--- SENTENCE 202 ---
Thus, American Samoa has both a large number of coralline algae disease cases and a relatively high diversity of these diseases, yet it does not affect the abundance of the coralline algae (Vargas-Ángel, 2010).

--- SENTENCE 203 ---
# 28.4.2 Crown-of-Thorns Starfish

Fishermen remember an outbreak of crown-of-thorns starfish on Tutuila around 1938.

--- SENTENCE 204 ---
There was another on the south side of Upolu in 1969.

--- SENTENCE 205 ---
Another outbreak on Tutuila in 1977-81 was particularly severe, and at one point, a population estimated at 83,000 was found on Taema Banks, where coral mortality was estimated at about 95%.

--- SENTENCE 206 ---
Over 384,477 animals were estimated to be in just the Aoa to Oneonoa area of northeastern Tutuila in May 1978.

--- SENTENCE 207 ---
A bounty was paid for starfish removal, and 486,933 were removed before funding ran out, but they were still abundant in April 1979, and did not decline until the following year.

--- SENTENCE 208 ---
Most of western Tutuila was not affected, except in Fagatele Bay, where mortality was estimated to be 95%.

--- SENTENCE 209 ---
Manu'a, Swains, and Rose were not affected (Green, 2002), but the outbreak occurred in Upolu, with a few reports in Savai'i as well.

--- SENTENCE 210 ---
Outbreaks on Upolu continued for most of the 1980s, and appeared to increase in the mid-1980s on Savai'i (Zann & Bell, 1991).

--- SENTENCE 211 ---
In 2002, there was a chronic population on the slopes of Ofu (Fisk & Birkeland, 2002; Green, 2002).

--- SENTENCE 212 ---
In November 2011, about 80 starfish were found on the south side of Tutuila near the airport, and populations slowly but steadily increased after that.

--- SENTENCE 213 ---
Several agencies, led by the National Park, killed them with an auto-filling injection gun, removing 15,537 between March 2013 and October 2014.

--- SENTENCE 214 ---
By about mid-2015, the park area on the north side of Tutuila was largely clear of them.

--- SENTENCE 215 ---
The south side has not been surveyed (Clark, 2014).

--- SENTENCE 216 ---
The culling campaign may have averted a major outbreak and coral damage.

--- SENTENCE 217 ---
In Independent Samoa, an outbreak of crown-of-thorns was reported from several villages after the 2009 tsunami.

--- SENTENCE 218 ---
In 2005, the Marine Conservation Division of the Division of Environment and Conservation of the Ministry of Natural Resources and the Environment conducted surveys around selected areas of Upolu, Savai'i, and Manono Islands, and found crown of thorns at about 39 sites around Upolu, seven sites around Manono, and seven sites around Savai'i.

--- SENTENCE 219 ---
A total of 1530 starfish were injected, 1174 being on the eastern side of Nu'utele Island (Marine Conservation Division of the Division of Environment and Conservation, 2015).

#### 28.4.3 **Physical Disturbances**

The Samoan archipelago is periodically hit by hurricanes.

--- SENTENCE 221 ---
Hurricane Tusi struck Swains Island in February 1987 and badly damaged the reefs, reducing coral cover to 0%-12% coral cover, except on the eastern side where a small area escaped damage.

--- SENTENCE 222 ---
Nine years later (March 1996) coral had recovered to 45%–65% cover (Green, 1996).

--- SENTENCE 223 ---
Houk, Musberger, and Wiles (2010) recorded a 5%-45% drop in coral cover from 2003 to 2005 on Tutuila, which they attributed to hurricane Heta which struck in early January 2004.

--- SENTENCE 224 ---
The eye of Heta passed about 220 km west of Tutuila (Fenner et al., 2008) but was a category 5 storm and did significant damage on land.

--- SENTENCE 225 ---
At Swains Island, wave heights during Heta were reported to reach 8 m. Damage to coral was greatest at the NW and SW points of the island, with live coral cover decreased as much as 52%, while damage on the northeast corner was lowest, as low as 0%.

--- SENTENCE 226 ---
Average coral cover for the island decreased from 60% to 30%, and then 2 years later had increased to 45% (Brainard et al., 2008; Hoeke et al., 2009).

--- SENTENCE 227 ---
Coral cover recorded by visual estimate from towboards decreased at all of the islands from 2002 to 2004 (after Heta), but towboard estimates in 2004 were substantially less than transect visual estimates for Tutuila, Ta'u, and Swains, raising questions about accuracy.

--- SENTENCE 228 ---
The path of Heta, from northwest to southeast, took it closer to Tutuila than any other islands, and it was twice as far from Swains and Rose as from Tutuila (Fenner et al., 2008) suggesting that the data be viewed with some skepticism.

--- SENTENCE 229 ---
When the author first viewed the reefs of Tutuila in 2005, no damage was apparent at monitoring sites.

--- SENTENCE 230 ---
Damage to reefs following a hurricane was also observed In American Samoa after Hurricane Wilma struck Tutuila in 2011.

--- SENTENCE 231 ---
Wilma was category 1 strength and the only places damage was found were at Vatia Bay and Pola Island, on the north shore.

--- SENTENCE 232 ---
The outer part of the bay sustained moderate damage (Fenner, unpublished observations), while the east side of Pola Island, just outside the bay, sustained heavy damage.

--- SENTENCE 233 ---
Recovery in coral cover was recorded in subsequent years (Clark, Halperin, & Fuiava, 2015).

--- SENTENCE 234 ---
There are records of 59 tsunamis striking American Samoa before 2009, with the first in 1837 and the greatest runup height just 2.4 m (Dunbar & Weaver, 2015).

--- SENTENCE 235 ---
On September 29, 2009 in American Samoa, an 8.1 magnitude earthquake occurred east of the Tonga Trench, about 200 km south of the Samoan archipelago.

--- SENTENCE 236 ---
About 20 min later, a large tsunami struck Tutuila and Upolu.

--- SENTENCE 237 ---
On land, there was massive damage in some restricted locations.

--- SENTENCE 238 ---
On Tutuila, Pago Pago Harbor, Leone, Tula, Poloa, and Fagasa were among the villages hardest hit, while on Upolu the east coast and the central south coast of the island were hardest hit.

--- SENTENCE 239 ---
The runup of the tsunami was over 12 m on Tutuila at Fagasa and Paloa and 7 m at Tula and the head of Pago Pago Harbor, while it was only about 2-3 m along much of the coast.

--- SENTENCE 240 ---
Water went as far as 600 m inland (Jaffe et al., 2010).

--- SENTENCE 241 ---
A different source reports the maximum runup was 18 m at Paloa, with 5.9 m on Olosega, and 12 m on western Ta'u (Dunbar & Weaver, 2015).

--- SENTENCE 242 ---
A significant amount of debris from on land was deposited in some areas on the reef.

--- SENTENCE 243 ---
Damage to reefs was concentrated to some restricted locations but mostly was not damaged.

--- SENTENCE 244 ---
The tsunami had no detectable effect in the National Park Tutuila unit, which is on the north shore (Clark et al., 2015).

--- SENTENCE 245 ---
In Fagatele Bay, there were a few large swaths of old coral rubble which were mobilized by the tsunami, with no corals on them after the tsunami.

--- SENTENCE 246 ---
Within 6 months, the flows of rubble had 100% coralline algae cover (Fenner, 2011), presumably the first stage of recovery.

--- SENTENCE 247 ---
Fagasa and Vatia could not be surveyed on the north owing of weather and boat ramp damage.

--- SENTENCE 248 ---
The most debris was found in the southwest section of Tutuila, from Poloa to Leone.

--- SENTENCE 249 ---
A study of the effects of the tsunami on the eastern end and south-central coast of Upolu reported that damage ranged from severe where piles of dead corals were left to areas of little impact.

--- SENTENCE 250 ---
At Vailoa on the east coast, the area covered by algae increased over time, but the live coral cover, which was high, did not change in the transects.

--- SENTENCE 251 ---
Estimated fish catch did not change after the tsunami, but fish sales did show a brief reduction and then rapid recovery (McAdoo et al., 2011).

#### 28.5 INTRODUCED AND ENDANGERED SPECIES

--- SENTENCE 252 ---
A study of introduced marine species found 28 nonindigenous or cryptogenic marine species in American Samoa, mostly in Pago Pago Harbor (Coles et al., 2003).

--- SENTENCE 253 ---
None have been reported to be invasive, although the snowflake soft coral (Carijoa riisei) that is reported to be invasive in Hawaii is common on the docks in Apia Harbor, Upolu (P. Skelton, personal communication) and one colony has been found near the canneries in Pago Pago Harbor (Fenner, 2013b).

#### 28.5.1 **Species Extinction Risks**

A study of the extinction risk of reef corals worldwide (Carpenter et al., 2008) reported that 75 coral species in the US Pacific waters were threatened to some degree.

--- SENTENCE 255 ---
About 54 of the 75 species occur in American Samoa (Brainard et al., 2011).

--- SENTENCE 256 ---
Kenyon, Maragos, and Fenner (2010) compiled records of which species were found in federally protected waters in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 257 ---
Subsequently, NOAA listed 15 species as Threatened under the US Endangered Species Act, of which six have been found in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 258 ---
Zgliczynski et al. (2013) reported 21 species of reef fish on Tutuila which have been listed as endangered or vulnerable on the IUCN Red List.

--- SENTENCE 259 ---
American Samoa had the lowest abundance of large-bodied species listed as endangered or vulnerable of any US Pacific region, with 2 individuals km<sup>-2</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 260 ---
Among the small-bodied species listed, 5 of the 22 grouper species recorded in American Samoa were significantly more abundant on the uninhabited islands than the inhabited islands.

--- SENTENCE 261 ---
The coral hind and blacktip grouper were rare or not recorded on the inhabited islands.

# 28.5.2 Fish

About 1000 fish species are known from American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 263 ---
The total fish biomass on each is less than the median for all the 39 islands and reefs in the US Pacific surveyed, and less than one-sixth that at the highest biomass reefs (NOAA Pacific Islands Fisheries Science Center, 2011a, Fig. 28.6).

--- SENTENCE 264 ---
This contrasts with coral cover (see earlier).

--- SENTENCE 265 ---
The total reef-fish biomass at mid-depths on the reef slope decreased markedly at Rose Atoll and Ofu-Olosega from 2002 to 2010, and was relatively steady throughout the monitoring period for the other islands (Fig. 28.10A).

--- SENTENCE 266 ---
By biomass, over half were primary consumers on the high islands of Tutuila, Ofu-Olosega, and Ta'u, mainly composed of parrotfish and surgeonfish, with primarily small- and medium-size species in those families.

--- SENTENCE 267 ---
The detritus-eating striped bristletooth (surgeonfish, Ctenochaetus striatus) has the largest biomass of any species.

--- SENTENCE 268 ---
Primary consumers have a lower proportion of the biomass at Swains than at the other islands, with only 12%-25% of total biomass at Swains compared with 34%-67% at all other islands.

--- SENTENCE 269 ---
Parrotfish composed only 2% of the biomass at Swains, compared with 11%-21% of the biomass at other islands (NOAA PIFSC, 2011a).

--- SENTENCE 270 ---
Less than half of the total biomass of these fish communities was composed of secondary consumers (omnivores and invertivores), planktivores, and picivores, combined.

--- SENTENCE 271 ---
This contrasts with many remote, near-pristine reefs, where apex predators alone are the largest group by biomass, sometimes composing half or more of all the biomass.

--- SENTENCE 272 ---
The NOAA CRED program fish data have been divided into browsers, grazer/detritivores (bristletooth surgeonfish, Ctenochaetus), scrapers/small excavators (small bodied parrotfish), and large excavators/bioeroders (large-bodied parrotfish).

--- SENTENCE 273 ---
The biomass of browsers is low, and the biomass of grazers/detritivores is much larger.

--- SENTENCE 274 ---
Swains has low biomass of all herbivores.

--- SENTENCE 275 ---
Macroalgae and turf cover decreased with increasing biomass of grazer/detritivores.

--- SENTENCE 276 ---
Encrusting algae increased with increasing biomass of grazer/detritivores and browsers.

--- SENTENCE 295 ---
Maximum sustainable yield is at about 33% of the unfished biomass, thus piscivores at Tutuila are predicted to be overfished.

--- SENTENCE 298 ---

--- SENTENCE 299 ---
Abundances are higher on more exposed outer slopes (Chin et al., 2011; Sapatu & Hjorleifsson, 2008).

--- SENTENCE 300 ---
In a study of the reef fish of 17 Pacific Island nations and territories, Independent Samoa was reported to have abundances that were among the highest found in all 17 jurisdictions (American Samoa was not studied), and the fish biomass in Independent Samoa was mid-range for the 17 jurisdictions.

--- SENTENCE 301 ---
This pattern implies that Independent Samoa had a smaller proportion of large fish than the areas with high biomass.

--- SENTENCE 302 ---
Since fishing differentially removes larger fish, the moderate biomass suggests moderate fishing pressure in the range of fishing pressure found in this study (Pinca et al., 2012).

# 28.5.2.1 Larvae and Connectivity

Simulations based on current patterns have led to the conclusion that the overall westward currents in the archipelago mean that connectivity among islands consists mainly of islands providing larvae to islands to the west of the origin.

--- SENTENCE 304 ---
The archipelago is so distant from other archipelagoes that it is heavily dependent on internal sources of larvae to sustain coral reef populations.

--- SENTENCE 305 ---
Each island tends to be self-seeding, particularly for species with shorter larval lives.

--- SENTENCE 306 ---
The much larger islands of Independent Samoa contribute over twice as many larvae as the smaller islands of American Samoa (Kendall, Poti, Wynne, et al., 2011).

--- SENTENCE 307 ---
The larvae of a few species of coral reef fish are numerous enough that they can be observed to settle out of the plankton onto the reefs in pulses, usually around January-April.

--- SENTENCE 308 ---
Several species are relatively large when they settle, and can form noticeable schools in shallow water.

--- SENTENCE 309 ---
Striped bristletooth (surgeons) are about 3-4cm in length when they settle, as are rabbitfish, and goatfish are about 10 cm long.

--- SENTENCE 310 ---
In 2002, vast numbers of striped bristletooth (surgeons) settled and many appeared to be starving (Green, 2002).

--- SENTENCE 311 ---
Many reef fish lead relatively long lives.

--- SENTENCE 312 ---
Striped bristletooths and striped surgeons can live about 20-40 years.

--- SENTENCE 313 ---
They were found elsewhere to grow rapidly for their first 2 years, and then remain the same size for the rest of their lives.

--- SENTENCE 314 ---
This is true for striped bristletooths in American Samoa as well.

--- SENTENCE 315 ---
Size, age, and sexual maturity were used to distinguish recruits, juveniles, and adults in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 316 ---
Recruits had uniform densities around Tutuila, spatially and over time.

--- SENTENCE 317 ---
Densities of recruits were higher in rubble and the density of adults correlated with the presence of coralline algae.

--- SENTENCE 318 ---
Adults are more common outside bays than inside bays.

--- SENTENCE 319 ---
Recruit and adult local abundances were negatively correlated, indicating ontogenetic shifts in habitat use with increases in size and age (Ochavillo, Tofaeono, Sabater, & Trip, 2011).

--- SENTENCE 320 ---
A few new species of fish have been discovered in American Samoa, such as Leslie's Cardinalfish, Apogon leslei (named after Leslie Whaylen) discovered at Rose Atoll (Craig, 2009).

--- SENTENCE 321 ---
Whaylen and the author simultaneously discovered a new species of clownfish on Tutuila, also found on Fiji, Tonga, and Wallis Island, named the Pacific anemonefish, Amphiprion pacificus (Allen, Drew, & Fenner, 2010).

--- SENTENCE 322 ---
### 28.5.2.2 Summary of Reef Health

A summary of 17 different indicators of reef health found that most indicated that coral reef health in American Samoa is relatively good, although not perfect or pristine.

--- SENTENCE 323 ---
The greatest damage to the reefs was done by the 1978 crown-of-thorns starfish outbreak, by dredging of reef flats to obtain material to build airport runways or add to village land or to create small harbors, by filling in and building on top of reef flats, and by fishing (Fenner, 2013b).

--- SENTENCE 324 ---
The coral recovered well after the 1978 starfish outbreak, suggesting resilient reefs, although coral cover may not have returned to values seen before the outbreak, or to the same coral community.

--- SENTENCE 325 ---
An impact score based on the National Center for Ecological Analysis and Synthesis scoring system has been generated for the marine environment for each of the islands.

--- SENTENCE 326 ---
Tutuila scored 12.4, Ofu-Olosega 8.4, Ta'u and Swains 8.6, and Rose 8.2.

--- SENTENCE 327 ---
For comparison, Oahu scored 15.6 and Jarvis (Line Islands) scored 4.

--- SENTENCE 328 ---
On a microbilization score based on the metabolism of all microbes divided by the metabolism of microbes plus fish, which correlated well (r<sup>2</sup>=0.68) with the impact score, Tutuila scored 65%, Ta'u scored 57%, Swains score 47%, Ofu-Olosega 39%, and Rose 26%.

--- SENTENCE 329 ---
For comparison, Oahu scored 98% and Wake Atoll 8% (McDole et al., 2012).

#### 28.5.3 Soft Substrates

Pago Pago Harbor on Tutuila has a soft bottom, which in the shallow areas near the head of the harbor has some Halimeda algae on sandy mounds.

--- SENTENCE 331 ---
Much of the harbor is about 70 m deep.

--- SENTENCE 332 ---
Portions of the shelf around Tutuila which is about 30-100 m deep are indicated to be soft at the surface by multibeam backscatter (Brainard et al., 2008).

--- SENTENCE 333 ---
Between Taema Banks outside the harbor on the south side and the shelf edge at about 60-80 m depths, the sandy bottom was dominated by Halimeda algae.

--- SENTENCE 334 ---
Sandy areas at the deeper end of this range which do not have Halimeda had large populations of the world's largest discoid foraminiferan, *Cycloclypeus carpenteri*, which reaches a maximum of 10 cm diameter (Song, Black, & Lipps, 1994) although more commonly reaches about 3 cm diameter.

--- SENTENCE 335 ---
The Pala Lagoon on the south side of Tutuila is now nearly enclosed by the Pago Pago Airport runway.

--- SENTENCE 336 ---
The bottom is covered with soft terrestrial mud.

--- SENTENCE 337 ---
This appears to be a pupping area for reef sharks.

--- SENTENCE 338 ---
Mangrove crabs are taken from the fringing mangroves and some burrowing clams are taken from the lagoon.

--- SENTENCE 339 ---
28.5.3.1 Mangroves

American Samoa and Independent Samoa have the same three species of mangroves (Ellison, 2009).

--- SENTENCE 340 ---
All mangrove stands in American Samoa are in estuaries and all are small.

--- SENTENCE 341 ---
The largest is at the Pala Lagoon in Nu'uuli on the south coast of Tutuila, the next largest is at Leone on the southwest coast, there is also a very small grove at Alofau on the southeast coast, and there is a grove on Aunu'u Island.

--- SENTENCE 342 ---
American Samoa has 51 ha.

--- SENTENCE 343 ---
Independent Samoa has 700–725 ha of mangroves.

--- SENTENCE 344 ---
# 28.5.4 Seagrass Beds

American Samoa has only two species of seagrass, which are in the same genus (*Halophila*).

--- SENTENCE 345 ---
They produce very sparse beds from near the shoreline to at least 30 m deep (at Fatagele Bay, Tutuila).

--- SENTENCE 346 ---
They do not appear to be habitat for any organisms other than filamentous algae.

--- SENTENCE 347 ---
Independent Samoa has three to five species of seagrass (Ellison, 2009; Skelton & South, 2006) such as one species of much larger seagrass that grows at least 30 cm tall and forms dense beds (*Syringodium isoetifolium*), and thus is probably habitat and food for other organisms.

#### 28.6 OFFSHORE SYSTEMS

# 28.6.1 Pelagic and Benthic, Seamounts

The seamount about 65 km south of Tutuila known as "South Bank" is a guyote, with a nearly flat top and a slight raised rim, suggesting it was an atoll at one time.

--- SENTENCE 348 ---
The top is 25–30 m deep and almost entirely covered with coralline algae rhodoliths and sand on a very smooth, flat, solid, hard carbonate surface.

--- SENTENCE 349 ---
Coral cover and diversity is very low and very few fish were seen.

--- SENTENCE 350 ---
Both gray reef sharks and humphead wrasse were seen (Pacific Islands Fisheries Science Center, 2011b).

### 28.7 CLIMATE CHANGE IMPACTS

Sea surface temperatures have been rising in American Samoa (US EPA, 2007;

--- SENTENCE 351 ---
Pirhalla et al., 2011, Fig. 28.6) as they are elsewhere.

--- SENTENCE 352 ---
Mass coral bleaching events at Tutuila in American Samoa were documented in 1994, 2002, 2003, 2015, and 2017.

--- SENTENCE 353 ---
Significant but not catastrophic mortality was documented in 1994 (Goreau & Hayes, 1994) and observed in 2003, and to a lesser extent, in 2002 (P. Craig, personal communication).

--- SENTENCE 354 ---
In March 2002, 2.3% of colonies at 10 m depth were bleached on Tutuila, with an average of 11.8% on Manu'a and 18% on Ofu, 7% on Olosega, and 9% on Ta'u (Fisk & Birkeland, 2002).

--- SENTENCE 355 ---
Coral bleaching was also recorded from Rose Atoll and the Ofu back-reef pools in 1994 (Craig et al., 2001; Kenyon, Maragos, & Cooper, 2010; Kenyon, Maragos, & Fenner, 2010), indicating that the bleaching was not restricted to Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 356 ---
Corals again bleached in 2015, beginning early in the year and ending about June, and then again in 2017.

--- SENTENCE 357 ---
Bleaching on all islands was assessed in February 15–March 30, 2015 on reef slopes.

--- SENTENCE 358 ---
The mean percentage of colonies that were bleached ranged from 1.0% for Ta'u, to 10.6% for Swains (Table 1 in NOAA, 2015).

--- SENTENCE 359 ---
Bleaching on Tutuila in 2015 was most intense on some of the reef flats and was generally less on reef slopes, where it decreased with depth.

--- SENTENCE 360 ---
At two locations (airport pool and Lauli'i reef flat), up to 90% of staghorn corals were killed in 2015 and at another (Coconut Point pool) about 50% were killed.

--- SENTENCE 361 ---
In 2017, bleaching patterns appeared similar to 2015, although perhaps with less mortality.

--- SENTENCE 362 ---
Of the staghorn corals (*Acropora* sp.) and finger corals (*Porites cylindrica*) in back-reef pools on American Samoa, staghorn corals but not finger corals were observed bleached each summer starting in 2004 (Fig. 28.11A and B).

--- SENTENCE 363 ---
Few if any staghorn corals died by 2015.

--- SENTENCE 364 ---
The percentage of corals that were bleached correlated well with sea surface temperatures (Fenner & Heron, 2009).

--- SENTENCE 365 ---
The experience of 12 or more years of bleaching and recovery each year did not protect many of these staghorn corals from dying in the mass coral bleaching event in 2015.

The back-reef pools of Tutuila are artificial, having been dredged out to provide material for building land, perhaps around the 1940s.

--- SENTENCE 367 ---
They host a low diversity of corals.

#### **HUMAN POPULATIONS AFFECTING THE AREA** 28.8

The Independent State of Samoa has about 194,000 people, three quarters of which live on Upolu.

--- SENTENCE 383 ---
American Samoa had 55,519 people in the 2010 census, and has probably not changed much since then.

--- SENTENCE 384 ---
In 1900, American Samoa had only 5679 people, and the population grew exponentially until reaching 57,000 in the year 2000 (Craig et al., 2005).

--- SENTENCE 385 ---
The 2010 census results surprised many, and some still believe the 2010 figures were incorrect.

--- SENTENCE 386 ---
Swains Island, the smallest populated island in American Samoa, had 164 people in 1950 working on a coconut plantation that covered the whole island, then coconut prices dropped, and now no people live there.

--- SENTENCE 387 ---
The populations of Ofu, Olosega, and Ta'u have been decreasing in recent decades, they now have about half the population as previously (Brainard et al., 2008).

--- SENTENCE 388 ---
All of the chiefs of Ofu, Olosega, and Ta'u now live on Tutuila, which has 96% of the population (Craig, Green, & Tuilangi, 2008).

--- SENTENCE 389 ---
The population of Independent Samoa is also stable, with people leaving to go to New Zealand or Australia.

--- SENTENCE 390 ---
These patterns are consistent with a phenomenon called "the depopulating of the Pacific" where people leave small islands to move to larger population centers.

--- SENTENCE 391 ---
This is part of the broader, worldwide, movement of populations from rural to urban areas and poor countries to rich countries, driven by more jobs and services in urban areas and in rich countries.

--- SENTENCE 392 ---
Twice as many American Samoans live outside the territory as in the territory.

--- SENTENCE 393 ---
Completed family sizes have decreased from about six to four children in American Samoa.

#### 28.9 CITIES AND PEOPLE, DEVELOPMENT, ECONOMICS, AND CHANGE

The economy of American Samoa has been about 1/3 tuna canneries, 1/3 local government, and 1/3 local businesses selling to those who work in the other sectors.

--- SENTENCE 394 ---
Almost the entire economy is subsidized by the US government (mainly by programs all states receive), 70% of local government is financed by the United States.

--- SENTENCE 395 ---
In Independent Samoa, the industrial sector represents 58% of the economy, services 30%, and agriculture 11%.

--- SENTENCE 396 ---
Agriculture employs two-thirds of the labor force (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samoa).

--- SENTENCE 397 ---
Much of the eastern portion of Upolu is covered with coconut groves, but the coconut industry collapsed about the time of World War I. Independent Samoa receives less financial assistance from New Zealand and international agencies than American Samoa receives from the United States.

--- SENTENCE 398 ---
As a result, significant numbers of people from Samoa have gone to American Samoa, where most of the workers in the canneries are from Independent Samoa (and some are from Tonga), and nearly 40% of the population are from Independent Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 399 ---
Many people have relatives on the other side, everyone speaks the same language and has the same traditional culture.

--- SENTENCE 400 ---
The economies of both American Samoa and Independent Samoa do not appear to be growing rapidly.

--- SENTENCE 401 ---
Apia is the only (small) city in Independent Samoa, and American Samoa only has villages, but population is centered around the harbor and Tafuna Plains.

--- SENTENCE 402 ---
A study of the economic value of coral reefs and mangroves in American Samoa found that coral reefs had recreational benefits of \$73,000 per year, fisheries benefits of \$759,000, coastal protection benefits of \$447,000 a year, and a total economic value which includes resident and visitor nonuse values, of \$5.1 million a year.

--- SENTENCE 403 ---
Mangroves had \$46,000 annual fisheries benefits, \$135,000 annual coastal protection benefits, and a total economic value of \$750,000 per year.

--- SENTENCE 404 ---
When potential nonuse benefits accruing to the US general public are included, the overall benefits could be in the order of at least US\$10 million/year for coral reefs and US\$1.5 million/year for mangroves.

--- SENTENCE 405 ---
The study reported that continued business as usual practices would lead to a decline in use benefits, while an optimal sustainable management would see a considerable increase in both use and nonuse benefits (Spurgeon et al., 2004).

#### 28.9.1 Land-Based Pollution

High phosphate detergents sold in American Samoa produced high phosphates in stream water until 2012, when they were banned by the governor's executive order.

--- SENTENCE 406 ---
A study of the effects of nonpoint pollution at 16 reef slope sites around Tutuila found negative correlations among watershed variables and three biological measures (coral species richness, community evenness, and the percentage of benthic substrate favorable for coral growth) (Houk, Didonato, Iguel, & Van Woesik, 2005).

--- SENTENCE 407 ---
However, an attempt to find a correlation among the human population of districts and coral cover and coral richness on reef slopes did not find significant correlations (Fenner, 2008, 2009), but correlations among visibility in the water and coral cover and coral richness were significant (Fenner, 2013b).

--- SENTENCE 408 ---
Marine-Based Pollution

# 28.9.2.1 Coastal Erosion, Landfill, Mining

Some of the coastline of Tutuila shows signs of rapid erosion, and shoreline armoring with basalt rocks or concrete revetments continues.

--- SENTENCE 409 ---
There are several gravel quarries, one of which at Faga'alu (at the mouth of the harbor) released large amounts of silt in storm water (about three times natural), which killed many of the corals in a small area on the north side of Faga'alu Bay.

--- SENTENCE 410 ---
The quarry has been modified to reduce sediment discharge.

--- SENTENCE 411 ---
Bay sediments have relatively low, probably natural, levels of chemical contaminants (Holst Rice, Messina, Biggs, Vargas-Angel, & Whitall, 2016).

#### 28.9.2.2 Forest Clearance

In American Samoa, most of the native trees are too small and not straight enough to be commercially valuable, the slopes too steep to log, and islands too small and remote for logging to be profitable.

--- SENTENCE 412 ---
The slopes are also too steep for coconut groves, so most of the territory retains uncut forest, which holds water and soil from runoff.

--- SENTENCE 413 ---
Relatively small areas near the base of slopes have been cleared in a few places for taro patches.

--- SENTENCE 414 ---
The steep slopes of eastern Upolu are also forested, as is much of Savai'i.

--- SENTENCE 415 ---
The gentle slopes of western Upolu were cleared long ago for coconut groves, which remain.

--- SENTENCE 416 ---
# 28.9.2.3 Pollutants: Pesticides, Industrial, Nutrients, Radioactivity

--- SENTENCE 417 ---
There is probably chemical contamination of sediments below the shipyard in Pago Pago Harbor due to hull cleaning and other activities.

--- SENTENCE 418 ---
Heavy metal contamination has been found in fish and shellfish within the harbor and at some sites outside the harbor (Craig, Saucerman, & Wiegman, 2000).

--- SENTENCE 419 ---
The two canneries in the harbor used to release all their effluent into the harbor, which is not well flushed and so nutrient buildup was significant.

--- SENTENCE 420 ---
In 1991, a pipeline was built to 47 m depth at the mouth of Pago Pago Harbor to carry liquid effluent, and sludge was carried by ship to a location 8 km offshore (Craig et al., 2000, 2005).

--- SENTENCE 421 ---
Nutrient levels in the harbor dropped significantly within 2 years.

--- SENTENCE 422 ---
In April 2007, new turf on a soccer field at the head of Pago Pago Harbor was heavily fertilized to promote grass growth.

--- SENTENCE 423 ---
Soon red tide blooms appeared in the harbor.

--- SENTENCE 424 ---
The soccer field was not used in 2008.

--- SENTENCE 425 ---
Fertilizer application resumed in 2009, and severe red tide blooms in the harbor appeared periodically, caused by the dinoflagellate, *Ceratium furca*.

--- SENTENCE 426 ---
The blooms were not toxic.

--- SENTENCE 427 ---
There were no more blooms following a program of reduced soccer field fertilizer application (Morton, Shuler, Paternoster, Fanolua, & Vargo, 2011).

--- SENTENCE 428 ---
Black algae have been periodically reported in front of Olosega Village.

--- SENTENCE 429 ---
A filamentous algae bloom in Olosega back-reef pool was observed in January 2005, 9 days after Hurricane Olaf struck the island.

--- SENTENCE 430 ---
Eighteen months later a study of nitrogen isotopes algae in Olosega and Ofu lagoons did not find any blooms, the isotopes did not point to human nitrogen sources, and the lagoons were deemed healthy (Garrison, Kroeger, Fenner, & Craig, 2008).

--- SENTENCE 431 ---
Piggeries and septic systems are major contributors to nutrient runoff, particularly on Tutuila, where about 96% of the human population of American Samoa live.

--- SENTENCE 432 ---
Many isolated villages are not connected to the central sewage system.

--- SENTENCE 433 ---
Most have septic tanks, but some houses have cesspools which probably contribute nutrients to nearshore waters.

--- SENTENCE 434 ---
There are about 1000 piggeries in American Samoa with about 9000 pigs.

--- SENTENCE 435 ---
The American Samoa Environmental Protection Agency initiated a program to move piggeries away from streams and promote dry litter systems.

--- SENTENCE 436 ---
Nutrient inputs to streams from pigs have been reduced by 10s of thousands of pounds per year.

--- SENTENCE 437 ---
Waters near beaches frequently exceed national standards for bacterial contamination, about 40% of the time in 2006 (Craig et al., 2005).

# 28.9.2.4 Shipping, Accidents

A large Navy ship, the Chehalis, sank in Pago Pago Harbor in 1948 near the main docks, in about 60 m of water.

--- SENTENCE 439 ---
It remains there today, where it is not a navigation hazard owing of the depth, but some fuel remains on board.

--- SENTENCE 440 ---
Nine longliner tuna boats were driven onto the reef flat in Pago Pago Harbor by Hurricane Val in 1991.

--- SENTENCE 441 ---
All have been removed (Fenner et al., 2008).

--- SENTENCE 442 ---
Another longliner went aground at the shore near the village of Amouli and remains there.

--- SENTENCE 443 ---
A longliner went aground on Rose Atoll in 1993, and the surrounding reef became dominated by black cyanobacteria algae.

--- SENTENCE 444 ---
The ship was cut up and mostly removed over the years, and the black cyanobacteria has slowly declined since then (Craig et al., 2005; Kelly et al., 2012; Maragos & Burgett, 2003; Schroeder, Green, DeMartini, & Kenyon, 2008).

--- SENTENCE 445 ---
A longliner ran aground on the reef at Coconut Point on Tutuila in 2016 and remains there.

--- SENTENCE 446 ---
There are many other shipwrecks in American Samoa as well (Tilberg, 2007).

#### 28.10 RESOURCES

# 28.10.1 Fisheries, Artisanal, and Industrial

American Samoa has the world's largest and third largest tuna canneries, although the smaller cannery is not operational at this point.

--- SENTENCE 447 ---
The tuna comes from purse seiners and long liners from all over the South Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 448 ---
The purse seiners are mostly US flagged, and the long liners are mostly from the Republic of China (Taiwan).

--- SENTENCE 449 ---
The number of longliners has decreased greatly in recent years, they now go to Independent Samoa and transfer their catch to vessels that take the tuna to canneries in other countries.

--- SENTENCE 450 ---
About \$20 million per year worth of tuna has been caught in recent years by smaller ships (roughly 50 ft. long) in a locally based longliner fleet.

--- SENTENCE 451 ---
All fishing in the American Samoa exclusive economic zone (EEZ) is by US vessels.

--- SENTENCE 452 ---
The United States now imposes annual catch limits for each species of tuna.

--- SENTENCE 453 ---
American Samoa has four locally based fisheries: the shoreline subsistence (coral reef plus scad) fishery is the largest, followed by an artisanal pelagic fishery, an artisanal "bottomfish" fishery, and a very small recreational (pelagic) tournament fishery.

--- SENTENCE 454 ---
Between 1979 and 1991, when the human population increased by 46%, the coral reef fishery effort decreased by 8%, and the coral reef catch decreased by 54% (Craig, Bonwith, Aitaoto, & Hamm, 1993).

--- SENTENCE 455 ---
The decreased effort was caused by a shift toward store-bought food (Sabater & Carroll, 2009), while the decrease in catch more than effort indicates decreasing reef fish stocks.

--- SENTENCE 456 ---
The bottomfish fishery targets a handful of snapper and grouper species which live below the depths of coral reefs.

--- SENTENCE 457 ---
It uses small, double hull, aluminum boats constructed in Independent Samoa, and small outboard engines.

--- SENTENCE 458 ---
Several hooks are attached on the ends of nylon lines which are lowered vertically from the boat.

--- SENTENCE 459 ---
By 1991, the bottomfish catch had decreased to only 14% of its peak (Craig et al., 2005).

--- SENTENCE 460 ---
Since then, the price of fuel has risen while the

local price of fish has not risen, and as a result the artisinal bottomfish and pelagic fleets and catches have dwindled and nearly disappeared.

--- SENTENCE 461 ---
Craig et al. (1993) report 69 species in the subsistence fisheries of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 462 ---
The Western Pacific Regional Fisheries Management Council has a list of coral reef fisheries management unit species for American Samoa that consists of 102 species (and at least a half dozen important invertebrate fishery species), and lists a similar number of 'candidate species' which are caught less often (Fisheries Council, 2011).

--- SENTENCE 463 ---
The composition of organisms found in middens dated 1000-3000 years ago was similar to that currently harvested (Craig et al., 2008).

--- SENTENCE 464 ---
Traditional fishing during this period employed fish hooks carved from shells, lines woven from coconut husk fibers, wooden spears, woven basket traps for goatfish larvae, and wooden canoes made from hollowed out logs, powered by paddling or sails.

--- SENTENCE 465 ---
Coral reef fishery data taken in 1978 by Wass showed American Samoa to have the largest coral reef fish catch by weight per unit of reef area of any place known in the Pacific (Dalzell, Adams, & Polunin, 1996).

--- SENTENCE 466 ---
However, this was largely an artifact of Wass using a small reef area, to a depth of just 8 m, instead of the reef area to 30 m as is commonly done (Craig et al., 2008).

--- SENTENCE 467 ---
Reef fish catches have decreased substantially since then, and were declared "collapsed" by Newton, Côté, Pilling, Jennings, and Dulvy (2007) based on increasing human populations in American Samoa and very large catches followed by small catches.

--- SENTENCE 468 ---
However, increasing financial support from the United States has allowed people in American Samoa to purchase more packaged food in local grocery stores, which has reduced fishing (Craig et al., 2008; Sabater & Carroll, 2009).

--- SENTENCE 469 ---
Re-estimation of reef fish catches from 1950 to 2002 based on all available data shown that actual catches have been about 17 times larger than reported, and that catches have declined by 79% over that time period (Zeller, Booth, Davis, & Pauly, 2007).

--- SENTENCE 470 ---
Fishing on reefs does continue at low levels, using a variety of methods.

--- SENTENCE 471 ---
Traps are not used, except for small basket traps in the Manu'a Islands for newly settled goatfish.

--- SENTENCE 472 ---
There is no cyanide fishing, and blast fishing is rare if it occurs at all.

--- SENTENCE 473 ---
Even the lower levels of current fishing, however, produce substantial catches that probably have impacts (Craig et al., 2008).

--- SENTENCE 474 ---
Historically, Samoans free dove at night to spear fish (the method called Lama).

--- SENTENCE 475 ---
About 1994, nighttime scuba spearfishing using waterproof flashlights increased.

--- SENTENCE 476 ---
This greatly increased the power of fishing, particularly for catching parrotfish and surgeonfish.

--- SENTENCE 477 ---
Page (1998) found that night scuba spearfishing had a catch of 2.2kg per h per fisherman, while free divers caught 0.64kg per h per fisherman.

--- SENTENCE 478 ---
The total reef fish catch increased dramatically to over 10 times that previously.

--- SENTENCE 479 ---
Page (1998) found that populations of the two largest species of parrotfish in the study were declining.

--- SENTENCE 480 ---
The size of parrotfish caught declined over the period of the study (1996-98).

--- SENTENCE 481 ---
The total estimated catch of parrotfish was close to maximum sustainable yield for the entire parrotfish stock, and scuba spearfishing caught 89% of the total annual catch.

--- SENTENCE 482 ---
Scuba spearfishing was banned in 2001 by executive order (Green, 2003), but night snorkel spearfishing with flashlights remains legal.

--- SENTENCE 483 ---
Additional information supporting the view that reef fish are overfished in American Samoa was presented in Birkeland et al. (2008).

--- SENTENCE 484 ---
Of the 1000 species of coral reef fish in American Samoa, only about 10% are large enough and common enough to be regularly caught.

--- SENTENCE 485 ---
Large fish are much less common on populated islands but small fish are not (Williams et al., 2011), and this is a fingerprint of fishing.

--- SENTENCE 486 ---
With sharks especially, there are larger populations of reef sharks on the uninhabited islands than on all the islands that are inhabited (Fenner, 2014).

--- SENTENCE 487 ---
A study of shark populations found that the populated islands of American Samoa have about 4%-8% of the number of sharks they would have if people were not present (Nadon et al., 2012).

--- SENTENCE 488 ---
As anything below about 33% is overfished for most species, reef sharks are overfished in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 489 ---
Blacktip reef sharks and whitetip reef sharks are the most common, with all other shark species rare near shore.

--- SENTENCE 490 ---
Gray reef sharks are probably the most overfished, since they are the most abundant on remote unfished reefs, and less common on Tutuila than blacktips and whitetips.

--- SENTENCE 491 ---
Bumphead parrotfish are the largest species of parrotfish, and sleep in schools on the bottom, and thus are particularly vulnerable to nighttime spearfishing.

--- SENTENCE 492 ---
In American Samoa, interviews with fishermen and scientists indicated that there were schools of bumphead parrotfish in the past, sleeping in schools usually of 2-6 individuals but one being 30-50 individuals (Fenner, 2014).

--- SENTENCE 493 ---
Now, only seven individuals of the species have been seen in the last 17 years, the last being seen about 2012.

--- SENTENCE 494 ---
This species is commercially and ecologically extinct, and if not biologically extinct it is certainly critically endangered.

--- SENTENCE 495 ---
Yet in 2012, the Western Regional Fisheries Management Council estimated the biomass of this species in American Samoa as 4699 pounds and set an annual catch limit of 235 pounds for it.

--- SENTENCE 496 ---
A study of the potential of coral reef fish communities to recover from fishing found that the time for a fish community to recover to its unfished biomass was estimated at about 40 years for areas open to fishing in Ofu-Olosega, 42 years for Rose (which is closed to fishing), 42 years for restricted areas of Tutuila, 43 years for restricted areas of Swains, 50 years for open areas of Ta'u, and 54 years for open areas of Tutuila (MacNeil et al., 2015).

--- SENTENCE 497 ---
This is based on the biomass of all the species together, and individual species can be in far worse condition.

--- SENTENCE 498 ---
On Ofu, a small, remote island with a declining human population, elder fishers felt that fishing is as good as it was when they were young, unlike elder fishers on Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 499 ---
In Ofu, the modern fish catches are broadly similar in composition to those seen in middens deposited from 3000 to 1000 years ago.

--- SENTENCE 500 ---
The fish catch on Ofu is estimated to be only about 1%-3% of the standing stock of reef fish there.

--- SENTENCE 501 ---
However, the standing stock of reef fish is still lower than on remote, lightly fished reefs and perhaps is even below that of fished mid-Pacific reefs, the total amount of fish taken from the reefs per kilometer of shore is substantial, and large fish are rare (an indicator of fishing pressure).

--- SENTENCE 502 ---
Groupers, triggerfish, puffers, and butterflyfish were caught in large excess to their proportion in the fish community, while parrotfish, surgeonfish, emperors, and damselfish are caught much less (Craig et al., 2008).

--- SENTENCE 503 ---
Fish in the first group are more probably to be overfished than in the second group.

--- SENTENCE 504 ---
There are large traditional fisheries for Akule (big eye scad), Palolo (polychaete worms), and octopus.

--- SENTENCE 505 ---
Palolo famously spawn on one or two nights a year and are a traditional delicacy (Craig, 2009).

--- SENTENCE 506 ---
Octopus, giant clams, lobsters, some sea cucumbers, sea urchins, and snails are taken all year round.

--- SENTENCE 507 ---
Octopuses are very fast growing and so can resist heavy fishing, but giant clams and sea cucumbers are quite vulnerable.

--- SENTENCE 508 ---
Additional species of invertebrates taken in fisheries have been reported from Independent Samoa (Andrews & Holthus, 1989).

--- SENTENCE 509 ---
Independent Samoa is more dependent on local fisheries than American Samoa because of less outside financial support.

--- SENTENCE 510 ---
In four communities studied, the average consumption of fresh fish was 61 kg per person per year, and invertebrate consumption 9.6 kg per person per year.

--- SENTENCE 511 ---
No differences were detected in fish stocks in MPAs and outside MPAs probably due to poor policing of the MPAs (Vunisea et al., 2008).

--- SENTENCE 512 ---
Independent Samoa exports small amounts of aquarium fish and live rock, but American Samoa does not.

--- SENTENCE 513 ---
Aquaculture

Tilapia are grown in some very small freshwater operations on Tutuila, for local consumption.

--- SENTENCE 514 ---
Oil and Gas, Minerals

There are no oil, gas, or minerals in the archipelago.

### 28.10.4 Tourism

Tourists that visit Independent Samoa are primarily from New Zealand and Australia.

--- SENTENCE 515 ---
It had 100,000 tourist arrivals in 2005, and tourism has been growing.

--- SENTENCE 516 ---
Independent Samoa has the capacity and space for more tourists and welcomes them.

--- SENTENCE 517 ---
By the year 1997, tourism brought in SAT102 million per year (Skelton, Bell, Mulipola, & Trevor, 2000).

--- SENTENCE 518 ---
There is little tourism in American Samoa, with only 7027 tourists in 2005.

--- SENTENCE 519 ---
Cruise ships make several stops per year.

--- SENTENCE 520 ---
There are few other tourists; most visitors are family members, government workers, or business people.

American Samoa fishing regulations prohibit taking coral above 60 ft. depth, set minimum size limits for giant clams and lobsters, ban spear fishing on scuba, and ban the take and possession of all humphead wrasse, bumphead parrots, and giant groupers; shark fins without carcass are prohibited.

--- SENTENCE 563 ---
They also set a minimum mesh size for nets.

--- SENTENCE 564 ---
Commercial fishing and export of sea cucumbers were banned in fishing regulations in 2014 and export of "live rock" was banned in 2000.

--- SENTENCE 565 ---
The National Park agreement with landowners prohibits commercial fishing within its boundaries, but not subsistence fishing.

--- SENTENCE 566 ---
The National Marine Sanctuary prohibits fishing in parts of its (expanded) area, but not in other parts.

--- SENTENCE 567 ---
Commercial fishing is prohibited in the 50-mile square of the National Marine Monument and National Marine Sanctuary around Rose Atoll, but subsistence fishing is allowed except within 12 miles of Rose Atoll.

--- SENTENCE 568 ---
Rose Atoll from the high-water line inward is protected as a National Wildlife Refuge, this area is also a part of the National Monument but not a part of the National Marine Sanctuary.

--- SENTENCE 569 ---
The effectiveness of regulations in American Samoa is difficult to gauge.

--- SENTENCE 570 ---
The ban on scuba spearfishing appears to be successful, the businesses that filled scuba tanks for spearfishing have ceased that activity, and there are few if any reports of scuba spearfishing.

--- SENTENCE 571 ---
Female lobsters in berry and undersize lobsters are often found in markets, in spite of being illegal (Coutures, 2003).

--- SENTENCE 572 ---
About 35% of giant clams harvested on Ofu are below legal size (Craig et al., 2008).

--- SENTENCE 573 ---
Green sea turtles that nest on Rose Atoll mainly go to Fiji to feed, where they can be legally taken.

--- SENTENCE 574 ---
Independent Samoa has a large community-based MPA program with 35 reserves on Upolu, 15 on Savai'i, and 4 on Manono.

--- SENTENCE 575 ---
The American Samoa MPA program was modeled after the Samoa program.

--- SENTENCE 576 ---
Independent Samoa allows the take of turtles, with a minimum size limit.

--- SENTENCE 577 ---
As of 2005, there was a ban on the take of sea cucumbers, but they were still being taken (Vunisea et al., 2008).

--- SENTENCE 578 ---
Skelton et al. (2000) provide an extensive overview of coral reef management programs in Independent Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 579 ---
# 28.12.2 Deficiencies and Missing Measures

Independent Samoa needs to consider protecting all sea turtles and marine mammals, the large reef fish species, and enforcing protection of sea cucumbers from commercial export.

--- SENTENCE 580 ---
The international donor community needs to support monitoring, research, and management of coral reefs in Independent Samoa to a greater degree.

--- SENTENCE 581 ---
American Samoa requires more no-take MPAs and perhaps more management of giant clams, and needs to evaluate the effectiveness of MPAs at increasing fish stocks.

--- SENTENCE 582 ---
Major disturbances such as hurricanes need to be documented better with direct observations and measurements of damage.

--- SENTENCE 583 ---
The fish fauna of the archipelago is very well documented, but the coral fauna needs to be even more completely documented, such as taxonomic level study of skeletons.

--- SENTENCE 584 ---
Even more needs to be known of the population trends and overfishing status of rays and skates and of moderately large reef fishes.

#### 28.13 SUMMARY, PROGNOSES, OR NEEDS

The coral reefs of American Samoa are relatively healthy compared with many reefs around the world by a variety of criteria, but are not pristine.

--- SENTENCE 585 ---
Large reef fish species are overfished, reef flats have been dredged or built on, and crown-ofthorns starfish outbreaks have killed large amounts of coral.

--- SENTENCE 586 ---
Hurricanes, a tsunami, and the lowest tides each year have also damaged the reefs, but reefs are well adapted to recover from these natural, brief events.

--- SENTENCE 587 ---
Exactly why the reefs of the South Pacific and American Samoa have remained relatively healthy is not obvious.

--- SENTENCE 588 ---
Perhaps, the banning of scuba spearfishing and culling of crown-of-thorns starfish have been among the most helpful regulations and actions.

--- SENTENCE 589 ---
Possibly, the relatively low level of human impacts has allowed the reefs to remain relatively healthy and resilient.

--- SENTENCE 590 ---
Mass coral bleaching is predicted to end coral reef ecosystems as we know them unless vigorous action is taken around the world to reduce the emission of greenhouse gases.

Anyone who has been to the beach or lives close to the ocean has seen the difference between **high tide** and **low tide**

--- SENTENCE 23 ---
but what causes it and why is it important?

--- SENTENCE 24 ---
In Samoa, the difference in the height of the ocean between high tide and low tide is no more than about three feet but in some parts of the world, it is as much as fifty feet.

--- SENTENCE 25 ---
That's as high as six aiga buses piled on top of each other!!

#### **

--- SENTENCE 26 ---
What causes the tides?**

Gravity is the force which keeps the earth, moon and sun in their place, and stops you flying off the earth as it spins!

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
The gravitational interaction between the earth, moon and sun is also responsible for the tides.

--- SENTENCE 28 ---
Even though the moon is smaller than the sun, it is much closer to the earth and therefore has a more powerful effect on the ocean.

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
The side of earth that faces the moon experiences a strong pull causing the ocean to bulge out, and creating a **high tide**.

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
Because of centrifugal forces (the same force that pushes you to the outside of a car as it goes around a corner), the water on the opposite side of the earth also bulges out.

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
This means there are two bulges on opposite sides of the earth.

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
As the earth spins during its daily cycle, these bulges of water appear to travel around the earth's oceans.

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
As there are two bulges of water, most parts of the world (including Samoa) experience two **high tides** and two **low tides** each day.

--- SENTENCE 34 ---
Because the moon is slowly orbiting around the earth, these tides get later by about fifty minutes each day.

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
#### **Why are some tides bigger than others?

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
**

The sun also has an effect on the earth.

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
When the moon, sun and earth are in line; the forces add together and cause higher high and lower low tides, also known as **Spring Tides**.

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
These occur twice a month, during full and new moons.

!

--- SENTENCE 39 ---
Spring tides occur during the full (and new) moon.

--- SENTENCE 40 ---
This is when the sun, earth and moon are all in alignment

Very extreme low tides only happen twice a year, when the earth's tilt is also in line with the sun and moon (i.e. the equinox).

--- SENTENCE 41 ---
This is a very popular time for Octopus fishing and shellfish gathering, as they are easy to catch in the very shallow water

!

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
[](_page_4_Picture_1.jpeg)

Shellfish gathering during a very low spring tide.

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
When the moon is at right angles to the earth and the sun, **Neap Tides** occur.

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
This is when there is not such a big difference between the water level at high and low tides.

!

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
Neap tides occur during a half moon.

--- SENTENCE 46 ---
This is when the sun, earth and moon are at right angles.

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
As these bulges of water flow around the Pacific Ocean, the water is pushed past American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
We see this as the water level going up and down, and the water moving backwards and forwards.

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
This movement of water caused by the tides creates currents that are known as **Tidal Currents**.

#### **Ocean Currents**

Ocean Currents carrying water from one place to another occur throughout the world's oceans.

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
As the water moves around, so does everything in it, such as larvae (very young fish and invertebrates), sediment (dirt) and pollution.

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
#### Large Scale Currents in the Samoan Archipelago

While the tides slosh water backwards and forwards, the wind also plays an important role in moving ocean water.

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
The wind pushes the top layers of water, creating a surface current.

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
The depth of this surface current depends on how long the wind has been blowing in the same direction.

--- SENTENCE 54 ---
The **Coriolis Effect** causes the currents to be slightly to the right of the wind direction in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern hemisphere.

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
Over the whole Pacific Ocean, the winds and the Coriolis Effect create large scale currents (see map below).

!

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
Left: Surface currents around the Pacific Ocean.

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
Source: Tomczak and Godfrey.

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
From March to September, the winds in American Samoa generally blow from the south east (also known as the **trade winds**) and from October to February, they generally blow from the north east (during the Cyclone season).

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
The trade winds push the water from the East Pacific Ocean towards the west and cause the **South Equatorial Current** to usually flow past American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
However, if all the water in the Pacific Ocean always went west then the eastern Pacific would dry up!

--- SENTENCE 61 ---
The water returns back to the east through the north and south Pacific 'Gyres'.

--- SENTENCE 62 ---
A Gyre is a very large whirl pool, where the water flows slowly clockwise around the north Pacific, and anticlockwise around the south Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 63 ---
Some of this water occasionally flows back to the east closer to the equator, to the north of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 64 ---
This current is known as the South Equatorial Counter Current.

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
The strength and location of these currents change with the wind and the seasons.

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
Most of the time the currents around American Samoa flow towards the south west (towards Samoa), but not always.

#### What else causes localized currents?

!

--- SENTENCE 67 ---
[](_page_6_Picture_3.jpeg)

Waves caused be wind driven currents meeting coral reef.

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
Photo: A. Lawrence

These large scale *Oceanic Currents* 'squeeze' through and around the islands of American Samoa and combine with the *Tidal Currents* to control the movement of water.

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
However, the wind (from nearby or far away) can also affect the currents on a smaller scale.

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
When storms occur at other places in the Pacific, they generate very long (500ft – 2000ft) **swell-waves** that can travel across the whole Pacific Ocean in several days.

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
When these waves break on the coral reefs surrounding our islands, they push water over the edge of the reef into the lagoons (or reef flats).

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
This extra water in the lagoon then rushes out of the avas (channels in the reef) in a very fast current.

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
It can be very dangerous to swim near an ava as it can suck you out into the deep ocean very quickly!

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
As the waves break on the reef edge, some water is also pushed alongside the outside of the reef in what is called **long shore drift**.

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
The strength of these wave driven currents depends on both the height and length of the waves.

#### **Currents and Coral Reefs**

Not only do coral reefs provide a food source for people but they also provide protection for the islands against the strength of energetic ocean waves.

--- SENTENCE 76 ---
Coral reefs depend on the currents for a number of reasons such as: transport of coral and fish larvae; distribution of **plankton**; and movement of sediment and pollution towards or away from coral reefs.

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
Currents ultimately affect the formation of coral reefs and therefore play a role in determining their shape and size.

!

--- SENTENCE 78 ---
Coral reef in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 79 ---
#### How do currents assist with coral reproduction?

--- SENTENCE 80 ---
Corals reproduce in two ways: sexually and asexually.

--- SENTENCE 81 ---
Sexual reproduction occurs in different ways depending on the species but the end result is that coral **gametes** fertilize each other to form **planulae** which then drift in the currents before they settle to form new coral colonies.

--- SENTENCE 82 ---
Although some coral planulae do have the ability to swim, particularly up and down, their end destination also depends on the currents at that time.

--- SENTENCE 83 ---
In some cases, corals are known to spawn at a very specific time which could be determined by the temperature, moon phase and also the currents.

--- SENTENCE 84 ---
#### How do currents assist with transportation of other larvae?

!

--- SENTENCE 85 ---
Most coral reef fish and invertebrates also have a **planktonic larval phase**, such as this Giant Clam or Faisua (pictured left).

--- SENTENCE 86 ---
These larvae drift in the currents moving up and down, often in response to certain environmental cues, sometimes to take

advantage of certain currents that can vary with depth.

--- SENTENCE 87 ---
For example, it is known that some fish larvae can smell the coral reef and swim towards it.

--- SENTENCE 88 ---
Reef fish often choose certain times of the month or year to spawn which may coincide with specific moon phases or current patterns.

--- SENTENCE 89 ---
They may also take advantage of the outgoing tide.

--- SENTENCE 90 ---
Some reef fish such as Groupers congregate in certain areas such as channels in **Spawning Aggregations**.

--- SENTENCE 91 ---
This may be to take advantage of strong currents to assist with spreading their eggs out into the ocean.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
How do currents supply food for animals on the coral reef?

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
Many animals on the coral reef depend on a fresh supply of plankton each day in order to grow and reproduce.

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
The plankton consists of very small particles of Phytoplankton (tiny plants) and Zooplankton (tiny animals, such as crab larvae).

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
These are carried to and from coral reefs by the ocean currents.

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
Some reef flat and lagoon areas do not get 'flushed' with a fresh supply of water and plankton each day, which affects the amount of food available.

#### Sediment transport and pollution pathways.

!

--- SENTENCE 97 ---
[](_page_8_Picture_4.jpeg)

Ridges and reefs are closely connected in Tutuila.

Coral reefs survive best in certain conditions (specific light and temperature conditions).

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
They also require relatively clear **nutrient** free water.

--- SENTENCE 100 ---
Excessive **nutrients** can cause **algal growth** affecting the corals' ability to survive.

--- SENTENCE 101 ---
If the water becomes contaminated by **sediment** (fine mud), this can harm the inhabitants of the reef.

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
Tutuila has steep mountains and lots of streams that carry sediment directly to the reef during heavy rain.

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
Information on localized current patterns is therefore very important for coral reef managers, especially around possible sources of sediment and pollution (such as stream mouths).

## **Current Surveys in American Samoa**

In 2010/11 current surveys were carried out in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 105 ---
The funding was provided by the Western Pacific Regional Fisheries Management Council as part of their Marine Conservation Plan.

--- SENTENCE 106 ---
An instrument called an **Acoustic Doppler Current Profiler** (ADCP) was used to measure coastal currents at selected sites around Tutuila Island.

--- SENTENCE 107 ---
How does an ADCP work?

--- SENTENCE 108 ---
**

The ADCP works by sending high pitched 'ping sounds' down through the water that bounce back after hitting particles in the water.

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
The ADCP uses the **Doppler Shift** to calculate the speed of the water underneath the boat.

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
When an ambulance drives past you on the road, the pitch of the siren goes from high (as it comes towards you) to low (as it passes you and is travelling away) because of the '**Doppler Shift**'.

--- SENTENCE 111 ---
The same effect takes place when the pings bounce off plankton and sediment in the water column.

--- SENTENCE 112 ---
The ADCP listens to the pitch of the sound that comes back and calculates the speed and direction of the water.

--- SENTENCE 113 ---

--- SENTENCE 114 ---
**

The surveys were carried out at four locations.

--- SENTENCE 115 ---
Each survey was repeated at least three times throughout the year.

#### **How were ADCP surveys carried out?*

--- SENTENCE 119 ---
*

The ADCP was attached to an aluminium frame that was securely fastened to the side of the boat.

--- SENTENCE 120 ---
The ADCP was connected to a laptop computer that recorded the data in real time.

--- SENTENCE 121 ---
A Global Positioning Sensor (GPS) was used to guide the driver around a pre-assigned track and record the boat's position.

--- SENTENCE 122 ---
Depending on the weather, each survey lasted 12.5 hours in order to capture a full tidal cycle (e.g. low – high – low).

--- SENTENCE 123 ---
The survey consisted of a set track around which the boat travelled at a steady speed.

--- SENTENCE 124 ---
The boat repeatedly travelled around the track, with each circuit taking approximately one hour.

--- SENTENCE 125 ---
The ADCP used the Doppler shift to continuously record the current.

!

--- SENTENCE 126 ---
[](_page_10_Picture_4.jpeg)

Alia boat used for ADCP surveys (left).

--- SENTENCE 127 ---
ADCP attached to aluminum frame under the water (above).

--- SENTENCE 128 ---
After each survey, the speed and direction of the current were analysed.

--- SENTENCE 129 ---
As described above, the tides push water around the island in one direction or the other.

--- SENTENCE 130 ---
The timing of these cycles are driven by the sun and moon, so are well known.

--- SENTENCE 131 ---
It is therefore possible to measure the effect of the tide from the data and identify the other factors affecting the currents at the survey sites.

--- SENTENCE 132 ---
In the following sections, examples of the currents measured at each site are presented.

--- SENTENCE 133 ---
The images show the currents when they were at their strongest in each direction.

--- SENTENCE 134 ---
Sometimes, the background large scale current is stronger than the tide.

--- SENTENCE 135 ---
In these cases, the currents usually do not change direction with the tide, and just change strength.

## **Site 1: Fagamalo**

Fagamalo is the last village on the road in the north west of Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 137 ---
Fagamalo was selected because it established the first No Take MPA (long term no fishing area) in American Samoa in May 2010.

--- SENTENCE 138 ---
Their no-take area is 1.12 square miles and adjoins the village based MPA that was created in 2005.

!

--- SENTENCE 139 ---
[](_page_11_Picture_2.jpeg)

There are a total of thirty nine people in this small village (US Census 2000) living in nine households.

--- SENTENCE 140 ---
The High Talking Chief of Fagamalo village (Faletogo Tuilagi) is a strong advocate of fishery management and effectively engages the community in conservation practices.

--- SENTENCE 141 ---
The No Take MPA of Fagamalo is to the east of the village.

--- SENTENCE 142 ---
The MPA extends approximately 1.2 miles out to sea from the shoreline.

--- SENTENCE 143 ---
The maximum depth inside the no-take area is 200 feet and there is a bank in the north western corner of the MPA that reaches up to 60 feet in depth.

--- SENTENCE 144 ---
The seaward boundary of the MPA is quarter of a mile from the shelf edge where depths extend to 12,000 feet.

--- SENTENCE 145 ---
Three surveys were carried out between December 2010 and May 2011 around the grid shown on page ten.

### **Site 2: Aunu'u lsland**

Aunu'u is a small volcanic island in the south east of Tutuila with a land area of 0.59 square miles and a population of 476 (U.S. Census 2000).

--- SENTENCE 157 ---
Aunu'u is only accessible by alia boat which transports passengers from Auasi on Tutuila Island to Aunu'u.

--- SENTENCE 158 ---
Aunu'u has a fringing coral reef that supplies food, protection and recreation for the local population.

!

--- SENTENCE 159 ---
[](_page_13_Picture_2.jpeg)

Left: Aunu'u Island from the ocean.

!

--- SENTENCE 160 ---
P. Wiles

The No Take MPA Program identified Aunu'u as a priority site for conservation in 2005 and carried out biological reconnaissance surveys in 2007.

--- SENTENCE 161 ---
As a result of high scores for fish and invertebrate variables, Aunu'u was proposed for the next stage in the process of MPA consideration.

--- SENTENCE 162 ---
A full household survey was carried out in 2009 to better understand the interactions between the people and the reef as well as its importance to them.

--- SENTENCE 163 ---
Current surveys were carried out in Aunu'u to predict the likely pathways of larvae originating there.

--- SENTENCE 164 ---
Local knowledge suggested that there are strong currents in the channel near Aunu'u that change direction with the tide.

--- SENTENCE 165 ---
This could result in the swashing of larvae backwards and forwards, thus spreading it along the nearby coast of Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 166 ---
If this theory is correct, then Aunu'u could be a great candidate for a No Take MPA.

--- SENTENCE 167 ---
Three surveys were carried out between September 2010 and June 2011 around the grid shown on page ten.

### **Site 3: Taema Bank**

Taema Bank is an underwater reef one mile off the south coast of Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 182 ---
It rises to about thirty feet below the surface, and has a variety of types of corals, fish and other marine life.

--- SENTENCE 183 ---
It was identified as a potential No Take MPA due to reports of large fish and Whaleshark visits.

--- SENTENCE 184 ---
The bank is unique in its habitat type and distance from shoreline impacts such as nutrients and sediment.

--- SENTENCE 185 ---
When developing a network of protected areas, representations of all habitat types should be included.

--- SENTENCE 186 ---
The protected areas should be more resilient to the effects of climate change and be able to help to sustain ecosystems in the face of disaster.

--- SENTENCE 187 ---
Taema Bank is representative of several other banks that can be found surrounding Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 188 ---
It was suggested by some scientists (Oram 2008) that Taema bank could be a good source of larvae and current surveys could help determine where that larvae goes.

!

--- SENTENCE 189 ---
Left: Taema Bank under the water.

!

--- SENTENCE 190 ---
The survey track (see page ten) covered part of Taema Bank and Nafanua Bank, to the east.

--- SENTENCE 191 ---
There is a deep channel in the middle of the two banks called the Narragansett Passage where incoming water could be colder and saltier (heavier) than the rest of the water Three surveys were carried out between November 2010 and August 2011 around the grid shown on page ten.

### **Site 4: Amanave/Poloa**

Amanave is the western most village on the south shore of Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 207 ---
It was selected for the current surveys because it had been identified as a potential location for a No Take MPA in 2005 (Oram 2008).

--- SENTENCE 208 ---
The data from the biological reconnaissance surveys verified that this area does have diverse and abundant coral reef resources (Jacob and Oram 2012).

--- SENTENCE 209 ---
The population of 287 (US Census 2000) are concentrated in the eastern portion of the village but the boundary of the village extends all the way to the western tip of Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 210 ---
It borders the village of Poloa to the north.

--- SENTENCE 211 ---
There are several small islands off the tip of Tutuila (see photo below).

However, fishing is still a popular activity at Tapu Tapu (the western point of Tutuila) by alia fishing boats that fish commerically.

The survey track traced the shape of the coastline around the western tip of the island in order to capture differences in current flow that were expected at this area.

This site is relatively close to the No Take MPA in Fagamalo, so the currents here will continue to carry larvae that originated from the No Take MPA area.

The tides play a large role in the currents at Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 235 ---
Towards the east (at Aunu'u and Taema Bank), the flow reverses direction with the tide.

--- SENTENCE 236 ---
In the west (at Fagamalo and Amanave), the background large-scale currents are almost as strong as the tide, and the tide only caused a small change in current direction.

!

--- SENTENCE 237 ---
[](_page_19_Figure_2.jpeg)

Left: Summary map showing currents from all eight ADCP surveys (two at each of four sites)

--- SENTENCE 238 ---
Overall, currents around most of Tutuila were relatively slow (around ten centimeters per second) with the exception of the Amanave and Aunu'u sites where much stronger speeds were recorded (up to one meter per second).

--- SENTENCE 239 ---
Strong currents would also be expected around the tip of Pola island (see next page), in the south (near Fogama'a and Fagatele) and in the east (Tula).

--- SENTENCE 240 ---
At the No Take MPA in Fagamalo, over half a tidal cycle, passive larvae could travel approximately one mile in six hours, at which time the reversal of the tide could carry the larvae back in the opposite direction.

--- SENTENCE 241 ---
The larval stage of many species can last weeks or months, meaning that they could be carried to other islands, such as Upolu and Savai'i.

--- SENTENCE 242 ---
However, the swashing of the tide backwards and forwards and the eddy off Amanave combined with the ability of larvae to swim and the structure of the coral reef could help to retain the larvae in Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 243 ---
Therefore larvae from the No Take MPA could end up around the west or south shores (e.g. Leone).

--- SENTENCE 244 ---
## **Next Steps**

Further current surveys are planned around Tutuila, both from a boat (as described here), and by leaving the ADCP in one location to see how the currents change over several months.

--- SENTENCE 245 ---
Data is needed from places like Pola, Tula and Fogama'a (see page 20), each of which have prominent headlands that probably cause eddies.

!

--- SENTENCE 246 ---
Left: Pola Island on the north of Tutuila close to the village of Vatia.

--- SENTENCE 247 ---
Photo: L. Jacob

In addition to ADCP surveys, drifter surveys are currently being carried out by DMWR's MPA Program using **GPS** units which drift in the ocean and simulate where larvae could go to.

--- SENTENCE 248 ---
The drifter data will be combined with the ADCP data (presented here) to develop a computer numerical model.

--- SENTENCE 249 ---
The numerical model uses physics (the equations that describe how water moves) to simulate what the currents around the whole of Tutuila are doing on a computer.

--- SENTENCE 250 ---
DMWR also plan to look at the genes of certain fish species from around the island.

--- SENTENCE 251 ---
Through the use of **genetic markers**, it should be possible to tell which fish are related to each other.

--- SENTENCE 252 ---
In this way, the information from current surveys can be validated.

--- SENTENCE 253 ---
The ADCP data and the other data that is being collected will all be used by the MPA managers in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 254 ---
They will identify the most important areas to protect and will also have information on which areas are connected through the movement of larvae.

--- SENTENCE 255 ---
This helps with **MPA network design** to protect coral reefs for future generations.

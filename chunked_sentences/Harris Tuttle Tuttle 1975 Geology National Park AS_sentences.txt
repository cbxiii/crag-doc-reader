## **Geographic Setting**

The Samoa islands are south of the equator and just east of the International Date Line.

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
The islands are about 2.600 miles southwest of Hawaii and 1.800 miles northeast of New Zealand (fig. 42.2).

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
The Polynesians of the eastern Samoa islands; that is, American Samoa, have had close ties with Americans since the 1870s.

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
United States officials negotiated treaties in 1872 and 1878 with Samoan chiefs for trading rights and the use of the landlocked, deep-water harbor at Pago Pago (PAHNG-oh PAHNG-oh), one of the best natural harbors in the Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
The U.S. Navy used the site for a coaling and repair station and, during World War II, as a staging area.

--- SENTENCE 34 ---
Western Samoa (the western islands of the archipelago), which had been under the protection of New Zealand, became independent in 1962.

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
America Samoa, an unincorporated territory of the United States, has its own government with an elected territorial governor, a bicameral legislature, and an independent judiciary.

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
In the National Park of American Samoa, land tenure and jurisdiction are different from those in other parks.

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
Within authorized park boundaries, all lands are village or communally owned and controlled.

--- SENTENCE 40 ---

--- SENTENCE 41 ---
The federal government has management authority and exercises it to protect natural and cultural resources, as well as provide for visitor use.

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
The villagers continue to carry on their traditional subsistence and gathering activities as long as they do not endanger natural or cultural resources that the government is required to protect.

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
The word Samoa is derived from the native Samoan words sa ia Moa (sacred to Moa/center of the earth).

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
This refers to the legend that "the rocks cried to the earth, and the earth became pregnant."

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
The god of the rocks, named Salevao, noticed movement in the center of the earth (moa).

--- SENTENCE 46 ---
When the child was born he was named sa ia Moa after the place from which he came.

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
Salevao promised that everything that grew would be sacred to Moa (sa ia Moa).

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
Therefore, the earth and rocks were called sa ia Moa or SAMOA.

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
Polynesians arrived in Samoa as early as 1000 BC and spread from there to other Pacific islands.

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
Samoans, the people of Polynesia's oldest culture, are well tuned to their island environment, managing it communally and holding it to be precious.

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
The national park ethic of preserving environmental and cultural values fits well and naturally with the traditional Samoan way of life, which is called "fa'asamoa.

American Samoa, as a political unit, consists of five islands, five islets, and two atolls (fig. 42.3).

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
The most populated island in American Samoa is Tutuila (too-too-EE-lah) with 95 percent of the total population.

--- SENTENCE 54 ---
The territory time is one hour earlier than Hawaii.

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
Temperatures range from a high of 18° C (90° F) in December, the hottest month, to a low of 10° C (75° F) in August.

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
Annual rainfall averages 190 inches, the rainy season being from December to March.

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
The humidity averages 80 percent but is a little higher in the summer months.

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
Because the islands trend in the same direction as the prevailing southeast trade winds, the difference between the leeward (north and west sides) and windward (south and east sides) is not so great as in Hawaii.

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
Pago harbor (south of the park) has the highest yearly precipitation of close to 195 inches because the high ridge of Rainmaker Mountain at 1,718 feet, which is also a National Natural Landmark, intercepts precipitation.

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
Hurricanes periodically strike these islands.

--- SENTENCE 61 ---
Three recent ones were quite destructive; Tusi (1987), Ofa (1990), and Val (1991).

--- SENTENCE 62 ---
Of the three, Val was the worst.

--- SENTENCE 63 ---
In 1998 El Nino produced a drought.

#### The National Park Units

The three islands that have parklands and protected offshore waters are Tutuila, Ta'u (tah-OO) and Ofu (OHfoo).

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
All three islands have drowned barrier reefs offshore.

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
Because of weathering and leaching, the soils are low in silica, and high in iron and aluminum, and therefore have low fertility.

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
They are classified as latosols.

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
Wildlife is abundant, with about 900 species of fish, 200 species of coral, 35 species of birds, and 3 species of mammals (all bats).

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
This is the largest park unit with 190 acres of land and 300 acres) offshore.

--- SENTENCE 76 ---
The entire island is 15 square miles and 3,051 feet in elevation.

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
The park's land is located on the south side of the island.

--- SENTENCE 78 ---
The park's flora is mostly undisturbed rain forest that includes coastal lowlands, montane, and cloud forest communities.

--- SENTENCE 79 ---
On a rare clear day, the view from the cloud forest—largest in Samoa—toward the ocean is spectacular.

--- SENTENCE 80 ---
Sea cliffs drop in two or three steps, from the top of Lata Mountain—which at 3,170 feet is American Samoa's highest peak—down to the rugged coastline (fig 42.4).

--- SENTENCE 81 ---
Laufuti stream, the only perennial stream on the island, has the spectacular Laufuti Falls, which pours down from the cliff more than 1,000 feet.

--- SENTENCE 82 ---
Thriving here are rare plants, tropical birds, two species of large flying fox (fruit bats) with wingspans up to three feet.

Tutuila Unit.

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
This park area is located north of the capital of American Samoa, Pago Pago.

--- SENTENCE 100 ---
The unit consists

of about 2,670 acres of land and 450 acres of ocean.

--- SENTENCE 101 ---
The size of Tutuila Island is approximately 48 square miles, with an elevation of 2132.5 feet.

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
Narrow irregular ridges form the backbone of the island of Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
These ridges are older beds that have been deeply eroded.

--- SENTENCE 104 ---
The park unit is typical of the rest of the island; i.e., closely spaced streams that are deeply eroding the volcanic slopes made up of basalt.

--- SENTENCE 105 ---
The amount of precipitation and warm temperatures have formed a *laterite* soil.

--- SENTENCE 106 ---
Most of the island has no craters because of substantial weathering, with the exception of the Leone Peninsula, formed about 70,000 years ago.

--- SENTENCE 107 ---
Also found on the island are *drowned valleys*, small coastal flats, and volcanic intrusions such as Rainmaker Mountain, which is a *trachyte plug*, 1,718 feet high and outside of the park boundaries.

--- SENTENCE 108 ---
It is a National Natural Landmark, one of seven in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
Tutuila has a cyclic history of erosion followed by subsidence.

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
Pago Pago harbor is a drowned volcanic caldera south of the park.

--- SENTENCE 111 ---
At Vaisa Point on the west side of Tafeu Cove, the small streams enter the sea in hanging valleys because the wave action has eroded the cliffs faster than the streams can downcut.

--- SENTENCE 112 ---
Maugaloa Ridge separates the park southward from the rest of the island.

--- SENTENCE 113 ---
This unit includes an undisturbed expanse of rainforest in the north central section of the island, as well as offshore waters and a fringing reef.

--- SENTENCE 114 ---
Coastal lowland, montane, and ridge communities exist in the rainforest.

--- SENTENCE 115 ---
Sea cliffs rimming the coast provide safe rookeries for thousands of sea birds.

--- SENTENCE 116 ---
In Amalau Cove (between Vatia Bay and Afona Bay) *pillow lavas* can be seen along the shore (fig. 42.6).

--- SENTENCE 117 ---
The pillow lavas indicate extrusion under water.

--- SENTENCE 118 ---
The beach at the head of the bay is a mixture of basaltic boulders and cobbles, with large chunks of coral that have been rounded by wave action.

--- SENTENCE 119 ---
Because of the shallow slope, vigorous wave action, and size and shape of the rocks, they can be heard rubbing against one another and making a noise as the waves move back and forth.

--- SENTENCE 120 ---
They are called the "singing rocks."

--- SENTENCE 121 ---
Vaiava Strait is another National Natural Landmark.

--- SENTENCE 122 ---
It separates Pola Tai Island (Cock's Comb), which is 420 feet high from Tutuila Island and includes Matalia Point, Cockscomb Point and Polauta Ridge (fig. 42.7).

#### **Geologic Features**

The volcanoes of Samoa developed on the Pacific plate over a *hot spot* similar to the hot spot beneath the Hawaiian Islands.

--- SENTENCE 136 ---
In 1985, J.H. Natland and D.L. Turner, using evidence from potassium/argon dating, field observations, and petrologic data, concluded that the Samoan shield volcanoes formed in age progression from west to east just as the rest of the Pacific Ocean

There are benches and sea cut caves at 20 feet and 10 feet above high tide.

--- SENTENCE 370 ---
The 20 foot bench is very noticeable because of the erosion of the lavas around the island, which form cliffs from 33 to 328 feet high.

--- SENTENCE 371 ---
Off Tutuila's coastline the barrier reefs and fringing reefs merge, forming a narrow structure that is only 35 feet wide or is sometimes missing, especially along the southern coast.

--- SENTENCE 372 ---
This structure is missing from around Ofu/Olosega and Ta'u.

--- SENTENCE 373 ---
On Tutuila a modern fringing reef in Vatia Bay is located at the end of the paved section of Route 006.

--- SENTENCE 374 ---
The rest of the shoreline of the park at this location is a drowned barrier reef at about 50 feet deep.

--- SENTENCE 375 ---
There is a broad reef shelf at about 98 feet and the shelf edge then breaks at 131 to 164 feet and plunges to the submarine portion of the volcano.

--- SENTENCE 376 ---
The islands Ofu/Olosega and Ta'u have merged benches that are presently being eroded.

--- SENTENCE 377 ---
They are about 3 feet to almost 7 feet above high tide.

--- SENTENCE 378 ---
On Ofu Island, 295 foot high sea cliffs have formed around the island with talus slopes at their base.

--- SENTENCE 379 ---
Wave action has modified the slopes.

--- SENTENCE 380 ---
Within the park boundaries on Ofu is Sunu'itao Peak, which is 765 feet high.

--- SENTENCE 381 ---
The rise in sea level produced a series of drowned valleys.

--- SENTENCE 382 ---
Examples on Tutuila are Afone, Fagaituo, Fagasa, Nua Seetga, Pago Pago, and Vatia Bays.

--- SENTENCE 383 ---
The formation of Aunuu Island during the Holocene time at or about the same time as the Leone Basalts.

--- SENTENCE 384 ---
The island, a National Natural Landmark located off the southeast coast of Tutuila, was formed by submarine volcanic activity.

--- SENTENCE 385 ---
The tuffs accumulated to the thickness of 656 feet.

--- SENTENCE 386 ---
The eastern half of the small island is a tuff cone.

--- SENTENCE 387 ---
The western half was swampy because of an impermeable layer of weathered tuff, but it has been drained and taro is presently raised on it.

--- SENTENCE 388 ---
Formation of the Leone Volcanics from several locations (0.07 mya) in the Holocene time during the post-erosional volcanism stage.

--- SENTENCE 389 ---
The Leone eruptions built an almost flat plain from a fissure that ran between Fagamaa Crater to Ototele Peak about 70,000 years ago.

--- SENTENCE 390 ---
A cone chain marks the fissure.

--- SENTENCE 391 ---
Emitted were olivine basalts, in the form of pahoehoe flows, and pyroclastic tuffs.

--- SENTENCE 392 ---
The tuffs veneered much of the area and the flows covered the submerged reefs.

--- SENTENCE 393 ---
A cone about 246 feet high was formed at the source of the Tafuna flow (the Cinder Member).

--- SENTENCE 394 ---
The Stony Ash Member formed another cone 197 feet high.

--- SENTENCE 395 ---
Three craters near Steps Point ejected tuff that covers the volcanic ash.

--- SENTENCE 396 ---
An erosional unconformity separates the overlying Fagamaa Tuff from the Fagatele Tuff.

--- SENTENCE 397 ---
Because they are so young, geologically speaking, they are weathered the least.

--- SENTENCE 398 ---
The drainage is still in the process of developing; hence the drainage patterns are not well defined.

--- SENTENCE 399 ---
Soils are just starting to form.

--- SENTENCE 400 ---
Associated with the tuffs are cinder and ash cones.

--- SENTENCE 401 ---
Many of the flows are pahoehoe or ropy lava flows.

--- SENTENCE 402 ---
Stearns (1944) suggested that a fissure from Otolele Peak about 4 miles long opened across the coral reef with fire fountains developing a cone chain.

--- SENTENCE 403 ---
The trade winds blew much of the pyroclastic debris to the northwest on Tutuila Island and filled both the valley and the bay, producing the plain.

--- SENTENCE 404 ---
The thickness of the beds is about 197 feet.

#### 9.

--- SENTENCE 405 ---
Modern deposition of unconsolidated alluvium.

--- SENTENCE 406 ---
Alluvium is unconsolidated material that has been transported by running water and downward movement to the valley bottoms.

--- SENTENCE 407 ---
Talus consists of the rocks and boulders that accumulate at the bottom of the cliffs and steep slopes.

--- SENTENCE 408 ---
Talus slopes are found on the west coast of Ta'u and south coast of Ofu.

--- SENTENCE 409 ---
Because of the long duration of weathering, high humidity, and precipitation, the basaltic soils have been leached and have high concentrations of aluminum and iron, with low concen-

trations of silica.

--- SENTENCE 410 ---
Even though the soil supports dense vegetation, it has relatively low fertility.

--- SENTENCE 411 ---
Along the beaches are coral gravels mixed with calcareous sands and shells.

--- SENTENCE 412 ---
In a few places the rocks on the beach have been cemented by calcite.

--- SENTENCE 413 ---
The tropical climate is conducive to both physical and chemical weathering; hence soils are very deeply weathered.

--- SENTENCE 414 ---
Also affecting the soils are the fracturing and faulting of the original bedrock, and physical erosion, which make slopes susceptible to landsliding during tropical storms and hurricanes.

--- SENTENCE 415 ---
For example, in October of 1979 after several days of heavy rain, seventy landslides were triggered and four people were killed.

--- SENTENCE 416 ---
There is evidence of submarine landsliding in the geologic past, that may have caused tsunamis, which damaged nearby land areas.

#### 10.

--- SENTENCE 417 ---
Formation of the Vailulu'u seamount.

Vailulu'u seamount, located east of Ta'u, is a future Samoan Island.

--- SENTENCE 418 ---
Radiometric dating of the lava flows indicates that the flows range in age from 5 to 50 years.

--- SENTENCE 419 ---
Located on the easternmost end of the Samoan swell is the leading edge of the hot spot track.

--- SENTENCE 420 ---
The seamount has risen 14,764 feet from the sea floor.

--- SENTENCE 421 ---
Studying this seamount can give geologists insight as to how the Samoan Islands have formed.

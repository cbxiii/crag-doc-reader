This Response Plan's intended audience is CRAG member agencies such as DMWR, NMSAS, ASCC, AS-EPA, USFW, NPSA, and AS-DOC as well as any natural resource agencies dealing with threats to coral reefs such as: bleaching, disease, vessel grounding, marine debris, poor water quality, invasive and nuisance species, and natural disasters.

--- SENTENCE 151 ---
These topics are global and this report may act as a reference for coral management worldwide, but this report entails aspects that are specific to American Samoa.

PREFACE

The American Samoa (AS) Coral Reef Advisory Group (CRAG) coordinates the Territory's coral reef management efforts and activities.

--- SENTENCE 162 ---
The group is a formal collaboration of five local agencies: the Department of Marine and Wildlife Resources (DMWR), the Department of Commerce [DOC; including AS Coastal Zone Management Program (AS-DOC-CZM) & the National Marine Sanctuary of American Samoa (NMSAS)], the American Samoa Environmental Protection Agency (AS-EPA), the American Samoa Community College (ASCC), the U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service, and the National Park of American Samoa (NPSA).

--- SENTENCE 163 ---
CRAG member agencies and partners cooperate to manage coral reefs with the vision of healthy and resilient coral reef ecosystems in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 164 ---
The group works toward this vision by guiding Territorial leaders and implementing strategies to reach goals that address various coral reef ecosystem conservation areas.

--- SENTENCE 165 ---
Foci range from education, outreach, policy, and enforcement to scientific research and monitoring.

--- SENTENCE 166 ---
Coral reefs worldwide are experiencing a critical decline due to various factors.

--- SENTENCE 167 ---
These ecosystems face threats such as overfishing, pollution, diseases, invasive species, and hurricanes, all of which contribute to the weakening of coral resilience.

--- SENTENCE 168 ---
However, the most ominous threat looming over coral reefs is mass coral bleaching, projected to intensify throughout the 21st century as a consequence of climate change.

--- SENTENCE 169 ---
The significance of monitoring these events lies in the sudden onset and potentially devastating consequences.

--- SENTENCE 170 ---
Without a careful and adaptive plan in place, the recording of the impacts on coral reefs may be inadequate or, in some cases, entirely overlooked.

--- SENTENCE 171 ---
Therefore, a well-structured response plan is essential to comprehensively document and understand the condition of coral reefs, enabling effective conservation and management strategies in the face of escalating threats.

--- SENTENCE 172 ---
The American Samoa Rapid Reef Response Plan is a comprehensive framework designed to facilitate the coordinated and swift response of the American Samoa Coral Reef Advisory Group (CRAG) to events impacting coral health within the Territory of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 173 ---
This protocol establishes organized procedures for gathering information during the early stages of an event and serves as a foundation for informed long-term management decisions.

--- SENTENCE 174 ---
The primary purpose of this plan is to ensure a timely and effective response to emerging threats to coral health.

--- SENTENCE 175 ---
The plan aims to empower managers with the necessary insights to make informed decisions for the sustainable management of coral reefs in American Samoa by providing actionable steps for early information gathering and response.

--- SENTENCE 176 ---
This plan is a living document that recognizes the dynamic nature of environmental conditions and the evolving expertise within the community.

--- SENTENCE 177 ---
It is intended to be regularly updated as new information becomes available and local resources and expertise change.

--- SENTENCE 178 ---
This adaptability ensures the continued relevance and effectiveness of the response strategies outlined herein.

--- SENTENCE 179 ---
This document is intended for use by coral reef managers and scientists on American Samoa but may also be useful to individuals and groups in other locations impacted by coral threats, especially those who are interested in developing similar Response Plans.

--- SENTENCE 180 ---
A NOTE ON INTERAGENCY DIVE OPERATIONS

In the event of a reef emergency, all CRAG member agencies and partners are relied upon for the most effective response.

--- SENTENCE 181 ---
Pooling resources and collaborating on underwater response actions between ASCRMP, DMWR, AS-EPA, NMSAS, USFWS, and NPS is a must.

--- SENTENCE 182 ---
Federal agencies are required to have reciprocity with all diving partners.

CORAL REEFS IN AMERICAN SAMOA

American Samoa hosts over 200 coral and 890 fish species, representing a rich biodiversity and playing a vital role in American Samoa's reef ecosystem.

--- SENTENCE 227 ---
These ecosystems serve as a source of subsistence and recreation for the local population.

--- SENTENCE 228 ---
American Samoa is home to three types of coral reefs: fringing reefs close to shore, barrier reefs farther offshore with lagoons, and atolls formed far offshore, creating ring-shaped reefs around circular lagoons.

--- SENTENCE 229 ---
American Samoan reefs boast diverse species of Indo-Pacific corals, invertebrates, and fishes.

--- SENTENCE 230 ---
Sunlight is crucial for coral reef survival, as it powers microscopic algae, called zooxanthellae, living in their tissues.

--- SENTENCE 231 ---
This symbiotic relationship allows corals to thrive in nutrient-poor tropical seas by exchanging food and oxygen with the algae.

--- SENTENCE 232 ---
Despite the numerous benefits coral reefs provide to the island, they face increasing stress due to human activities and exacerbated natural stressors.

--- SENTENCE 233 ---
Natural stressors include bleaching events, COTS invasions, invasive species, and hurricanes.

--- SENTENCE 234 ---
While corals historically recover from these natural disturbances, human-induced stressors such as population growth, harmful fishing practices, sedimentation, and pollution pose new and significant threats.

--- SENTENCE 235 ---
American Samoa's coral reefs face challenges due to climate change and local anthropogenic-induced stressors.

--- SENTENCE 236 ---
Climate change has led to bleaching, Crown-of-Thorn starfish outbreaks, and coral death.

--- SENTENCE 237 ---
As identified in [American Samoa's Healthy Coral Reef Local Action](https://americansamoa-data.nocache.eightyoptions.com.au/system/files/AS%20Healthy%20Coral%20Reef%20Strategy_v2020-2025.pdf)

--- SENTENCE 238 ---
[Strategy \(2020-2025\),](https://americansamoa-data.nocache.eightyoptions.com.au/system/files/AS%20Healthy%20Coral%20Reef%20Strategy_v2020-2025.pdf) local stressors include pollution from coastal development, inadequate sewer-waste management, and unsustainable fishing practices.

--- SENTENCE 239 ---
Despite these challenges, coral reefs in American Samoa provide critical services, supporting commercial, recreational, and subsistence fishing.

--- SENTENCE 240 ---
These services' estimated annual economic benefit for residents and visitors amounts to \$5 millio[n](#page-19-3)<sup>1</sup>

CLIMATE CHANGE IN AMERICAN SAMOA

Climate change is driven by the escalation of greenhouse gases, particularly carbon dioxide, in the Earth's atmosphere.

--- SENTENCE 242 ---
These gases, stemming from the use of fuels like gasoline, oil, coal, natural gas, and even wood, create a natural greenhouse effect regulating heat.

--- SENTENCE 243 ---
However, since the Industrial Revolution, the concentration of these gases has surged, thickening the atmospheric layer and contributing to global warming.

--- SENTENCE 244 ---
In American Samoa, the community has already felt the impacts of climate variability, facing hurricanes, droughts, heavy rainfall, and coastal inundation.

--- SENTENCE 245 ---
These events directly and indirectly affect natural resources.

--- SENTENCE 246 ---
Drawing on their environmental knowledge, the people of American Samoa prepare for and build resilience against these occurrences.

--- SENTENCE 256 ---
CLIMATE CHANGE AND CORAL BLEACHING

Corals, essential components of marine ecosystems, harbor microscopic single-celled algae known as zooxanthellae or dinoflagellates.

--- SENTENCE 257 ---
These algae play a crucial role by conducting photosynthesis in the presence of sunlight, producing food that contributes to the nutrition of corals.

--- SENTENCE 258 ---
However, if sea temperatures rise significantly, these algae are expelled, resulting in the loss of color in corals.

--- SENTENCE 259 ---
This expulsion, termed "bleaching," leaves the coral tissue transparent, revealing the white skeleton beneath.

--- SENTENCE 260 ---
Despite the term "bleaching," no chemical bleach is involved in this process.

--- SENTENCE 261 ---
Bleaching occurs when sea temperatures surpass 1°C above the average summer high temperature.

--- SENTENCE 262 ---
Corals can recover if the temperature rise remains within this range.

--- SENTENCE 263 ---
However, if the increase exceeds 2°C above the average, some corals may begin to die.

--- SENTENCE 264 ---
Extended periods of high temperatures can lead to widespread coral mortality, causing severe damage to reefs.

--- SENTENCE 265 ---
While natural climate variability has historically contributed to sporadic bleaching events globally, projections indicate an alarming increase in these events due to climate changedriven rises in sea surface temperature.

--- SENTENCE 266 ---
Global warming is expected to make mass coral bleaching more prevalent, significantly impacting reefs' composition and overall health.

--- SENTENCE 267 ---
Documenting these events and the resulting changes becomes crucial in understanding and mitigating the profound effects on coral reefs.

--- SENTENCE 268 ---
OTHER IMPACTS OF CLIMATE CHANGE

Climate change will impact corals in direct and indirect ways.

--- SENTENCE 269 ---
Global climate change will result in future increases in diseases of marine organisms, storm activity, and severity of bleaching events.

--- SENTENCE 270 ---
Although the impacts remain relatively uncertain, this plan will assist in quantifying and mitigating changes to coral reefs.

OVERVIEW

The American Samoa Coral Bleaching Response Plan exists to maximize the effective coordination of activities conducted by CRAG and the CRAG Coordination.

--- SENTENCE 274 ---
This plan will ensure efficient use of resources and human capital by providing a standardized framework for responding to coral bleaching events.

--- SENTENCE 275 ---
Coral bleaching is primarily driven by ocean warming that results in the release of the colorful symbiotic algae (zooxanthellae) in coral tissues, displaying the white coral skeleton underneath (Figure 7).

--- SENTENCE 276 ---
Corals rely on zooxanthellae for a significant portion of their diet, so the loss of zooxanthellae can cause corals to slowly starve.

--- SENTENCE 277 ---
Unfortunately, coral bleaching cannot be directly influenced at a meaningful scale by local intervention.

--- SENTENCE 278 ---
Therefore, the management

response to coral bleaching is especially complex and challenging.

--- SENTENCE 280 ---
Though finalized in 2024, this document is intended to be a working draft that will be periodically updated and improved.

--- SENTENCE 281 ---
This plan includes an in-depth description of American Samoa's early warning system for coral bleaching events, standard operating procedures for response implementation, detailed assessment protocols, and recommendations for post-bleaching management, reef recovery, and restoration approaches.

BACKGROUND ON BLEACHING

Although coral reef ecosystems have existed for approximately 500 million years, their survival is threatened by local, regional, and global human impacts.

--- SENTENCE 289 ---
About one-fifth of the planet's coral reefs have already been lost; now, more than a quarter of the remaining reefs face imminent degradatio[n](#page-23-0)2.

--- SENTENCE 290 ---
Anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions have dire consequences for reefs, such as reduced calcification rates due to acidification (a result of carbon dioxide uptake by the ocean), outbreaks of emergent and existing coral diseases, and coral bleaching, which is caused by both warming seas and acidification[3](#page-23-1).

--- SENTENCE 291 ---
Fifteen Pacific coral species are listed under the U.S. Endangered Species Act; In American Samoa there are six ESA-listed coral species (*A. globiceps, A. jacquelineae, A. retusa, A. speciosa, Euphyllia paradivisa, Isopora crateriformis*), all listed as threatened4[.](#page-23-2)

--- SENTENCE 292 ---
Since the beginning of the 20th century, mean sea surface temperature (SST) has increased by an average of 0.07⁰C per decade (Figure 8).

--- SENTENCE 293 ---
Between 1971 and 2010, over 90% of the energy (heat) stored by the earth was taken up by the oceans[5](#page-23-3).

--- SENTENCE 294 ---
Most ocean warming is occurring near the surface, with an average increase of 0.11° C per decade in the shallowest 75 m of ocean waters over that period (IPCC 2014).

--- SENTENCE 295 ---
The majority of reef-building (hermatypic) coral species depend on mutualistic relationships with symbiotic unicellular dinoflagellates, algae known as zooxanthellae, which live in the tissue of each coral polyp (genus: *Symbiodinium*).

--- SENTENCE 296 ---
These coral species, known as the Scleractinia, obtain most of their nutrients from these photosynthetic algae and can accrete calcium carbonate to build their skeletons more rapidly than azooxanthellate corals.

--- SENTENCE 297 ---
Scleractinian corals are restricted to relatively shallow waters, as the symbionts must receive adequate solar irradiance to supply the coral polyps with energy and oxygen6[.](#page-23-4)

# 10.5.1.1.

--- SENTENCE 361 ---
NOAA CORAL REEF WATCH (CRW) PRODUCTS

The NOAA CRW program provides coral bleaching alerts based on satellite data (measurements of nightly SST) used to predict reefs at risk of thermal stress and bleaching.

--- SENTENCE 362 ---
CRW provides products at two resolutions: 5 km (daily updates) and 50 km (twice weekly updates).

--- SENTENCE 363 ---
The highresolution 5 km products, first released in February 2015, are expected to be more accurate than the 50 km products because of increased data density, quality, resolution, and understanding of climate patterns (CRW 2016).

--- SENTENCE 364 ---
CRW provides this high-resolution data on SST, SST anomaly, SST trend, HotSpot, and degree heating weeks (DHW) (see sidebar), accessible in data tables and on maps.

--- SENTENCE 365 ---
In addition to viewing current daily values and archived historical data and maps, CRW provides animations depicting variable changes over the most recent 30 and 90-day periods.

--- SENTENCE 366 ---
The data is organized into 212 regional virtual stations designed to present the information at a jurisdictional or subregional level.

--- SENTENCE 367 ---
This allows reef managers to assess conditions throughout their administrative areas and gain an understanding of regional impacts rather than evaluating each 5 km pixel separately (CRW 2016).

--- SENTENCE 368 ---
Interactive functions on the CRW website allow users to view multi-year graphs and see changes in SST and DHW over two years for each virtual station.

--- SENTENCE 369 ---
The 5-km Bleaching Alert Area product (updated daily at 13:30 EST) shows locations where thermal stress is currently at defined bleaching threshold levels (Figure 10).

--- SENTENCE 370 ---
This tool shows the highest stress thermal level that each pixel has been exposed to over the previous one to seven days.

--- SENTENCE 371 ---
The maps depict areas with elevated stress levels in warm colors (Figure 11).

--- SENTENCE 372 ---
CRW maintains a free email service that alerts users to changes in thermal stress levels at selected virtual stations (to sign up: [http://coralreefwatch-satops.noaa.gov\)](http://coralreefwatch-satops.noaa.gov/).

--- SENTENCE 373 ---
Users can receive alerts for all stations or only those within their region of interest.

--- SENTENCE 374 ---
Coral Reef Response Team members should regularly monitor these email alerts, with one or more persons designated to receive these alerts via email.

CRW also provides a 5 km three-month coral bleaching thermal stress outlook based on the SST forecast from the National Weather Service (NWS) and calculated with DHW and HotSpot levels projections (Figure 10).

--- SENTENCE 377 ---
The outlook (updated weekly on Tuesday mornings, EST) predicts the chance of coral bleaching thermal stress occurring in the upcoming three months, the usual length of one bleaching cycle.

--- SENTENCE 378 ---
CRW provides bleaching outlooks calculated using two methodologies available in weekly or four-month ranges.

--- SENTENCE 379 ---
The first method, which produces the 60% and 90% chance graphs, uses 28 weekly measurements of SST (4 per day) to determine a probabilistic outlook for up to 270 days in the future (Eakin et al. 2012; CRW 2016).

--- SENTENCE 380 ---
The most accessible element of the CRW site is the bleaching thermal stress gauges (Figure 11) and time series product, which provides summary data specific to a selected regional virtual station, including data for each 5 km pixel within the station.

--- SENTENCE 381 ---
The bleaching outlooks for each virtual station are based on the 60% likelihood calculations.

CLIMATE/ENSO PROJECTIONS

The CRAG Response Teams should review seasonal climate predictions by April to evaluate bleaching risk in the upcoming summer months.

--- SENTENCE 385 ---
ENSO cycles should be closely monitored.

--- SENTENCE 386 ---
The Multivariate ENSO Index (MEI), generated by [NOAA's Earth System Research Laboratory](http://www.esrl.noaa.gov/psd/enso/mei) (ESRL), measures air pressure and temperature, winds, SST, and cloud cover to evaluate ENSO conditions.

--- SENTENCE 387 ---
The MEI, updated once per month, is intended for research purposes, not real-time climate monitoring, and should be used cautiously and in conjunction with other components of the early warning system when predicting bleaching (ESRL 2016).

--- SENTENCE 388 ---
The MEI is a global index and does not describe ENSO conditions by region.

--- SENTENCE 389 ---
In the National Weather Service, NOAA's Climate Prediction Center (CPC) provides weekly ENSO updates published online every Monday.

--- SENTENCE 390 ---
A climate diagnostics bulletin is uploaded on the second Thursday of each month.

--- SENTENCE 391 ---
These reports describe the current strength and status of ENSO conditions, shifts in SST, wind, radiation, and precipitation anomalies, and an outlook for future ENSO conditions.

--- SENTENCE 392 ---
Weekly and monthly ENSO advisories are available [here.](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/analysis_monitoring/enso_advisory/)

TIDAL FORECASTS

Tidal forecasts should also be monitored to predict conditions that may increase the risk of thermal stress that causes coral bleaching.

--- SENTENCE 399 ---
Coral bleaching may be more likely during periods of repeated daytime extreme low tide conditions, especially during summer months.

--- SENTENCE 400 ---
Near-shore water temperature, particularly on shallow reef flats, can become anomalously high, while exposure to air causes additional stress for coral communities.

--- SENTENCE 401 ---
The flow of heated water from the reef flat over the reef margin (particularly through channels) and reef front may also cause coral bleaching in these zones.

BLEACHING ASSESSMENTS

American Samoa's bleaching assessment methods involve surveys of varying scales and resource requirements to measure the extent and severity of bleaching events, and evaluate the ecological impacts of coral bleaching on reef communities.

--- SENTENCE 490 ---
The data collected during the assessments and in post-bleaching surveys will improve our understanding of the extent and severity of coral bleaching, the duration and ecological effects of bleaching events in American Samoa, the capacity of reefs to recover after bleaching, and the impact of local stressors on bleaching severity and subsequent recovery.

--- SENTENCE 491 ---
Since coral bleaching events are expected to occur with increasing frequency and severity, we hope these assessments will provide insight into what American Samoa's future reefs may look like.

--- SENTENCE 492 ---
We also aim to use this data to measure the relative resilience of American Samoa's reefs and produce data-driven management recommendations for American Samoa's coral reef resources.

--- SENTENCE 493 ---
CRAG Coordination conducts annual monitoring surveys to obtain a baseline of reef health prior to the warmer season and a potential bleaching event.

--- SENTENCE 494 ---
Coral surveys will be completed, and temperature loggers (e.g., HOBO loggers) will be installed at each of the priority reefs in October/November to ensure ecological data is available and to record water temperature within reef flats and reef slopes for selected areas.

--- SENTENCE 495 ---
This will allow for before-and-after comparisons of reef health, coral mortality and changes in fish and benthic assemblages if bleaching occurs.

--- SENTENCE 496 ---
If resources allow, drone footage may be collected for each priority site.

--- SENTENCE 497 ---
Additional reef monitoring surveys are conducted by DMWR, NPS, NMSAS, USFWS, and EPA

--- SENTENCE 584 ---
If the alert status for American Samoa is elevated to warning or above and/or there are verified reports of bleached *Acropora* at two separate reef sites, rapid reconnaissance surveys will be conducted at designated priority sites around the island.

--- SENTENCE 585 ---
The objective of these snorkel-based

surveys is to evaluate the spatial extent of coral bleaching, severity of the event using the classification levels in Table 2, and identify affected taxa.

--- SENTENCE 586 ---
If overall bleaching is mild, rapid reconnaissance surveys may be performed again several weeks later.

--- SENTENCE 587 ---
These sites were selected because they are easily accessible by snorkelers from the shore and are known to have populations of bleaching-susceptible corals.

--- SENTENCE 588 ---
Before a bleaching event, the CTC will review CRAG monitoring sites.

--- SENTENCE 589 ---
Additional sites may be selected based on resource availability and reports of localized bleaching.

--- SENTENCE 590 ---
Close-up inspection of a coral colony is needed to determine whether a coral is bleached or recently dead, as newly dead corals are also white.

--- SENTENCE 591 ---
If a colony appears to be very clean and free of sediment and tentacles, which are visible when viewing the coral in profile, the colony is bleached but still alive.

--- SENTENCE 592 ---
Dead colonies no longer secrete the mucus, allowing them to remove debris that has settled on their outer tissue layer, so any sediment built up or biofilm on a coral indicates mortality[14](#page-52-0).

--- SENTENCE 593 ---
Snorkelers should record colony-level data for tagged and/or affected colonies if time allows.

--- SENTENCE 594 ---
Data to be recorded includes tag number (if tagged), species (or genera if species is unknown), colony location (lat/long), colony depth (at base), colony diameter (longest axis), bleaching description, location, and severity.

--- SENTENCE 595 ---
All bleached corals and tagged colonies should be photographed; GPS should be enabled to best track where photos are taken.

--- SENTENCE 596 ---
Photographs should be labeled according to convention, uploaded to the shared folder, and made accessible to all response team members.

--- SENTENCE 597 ---
The tag number is only included if the photo shows a tagged colony.

--- SENTENCE 598 ---
Photo labeling convention: SITEID\_MMDDYY\_OBSERVERINITIALS\_PHOTONUMBER\_TAGNO (e.g. S02\_052516\_JS\_001 or S10\_052516\_JS\_001\_S10A05)

--- SENTENCE 614 ---
TIMED SWIMS - REEF FLAT, REEF SLOPES AND POOLS

Reef flat surveys are semi-quantitative snorkel-based surveys following the observation of moderate to severe bleaching at many shallow reef monitoring sites.

--- SENTENCE 615 ---
Ideally, these surveys would begin in October/November and be conducted at 2-week intervals throughout the bleaching event and for several months afterward.

--- SENTENCE 616 ---
If time and resources allow, photo-quadrats will also be taken.

--- SENTENCE 617 ---
These surveys can be conducted with two trained snorkelers or one trained snorkeler and a buddy who can take rapid recon photos while the trained snorkeler completes the surveys.

--- SENTENCE 618 ---
At each site, three parallel transects (non-permanent) are surveyed for 30 minutes.

--- SENTENCE 619 ---
Each time a site is surveyed, the coordinates of each transect's start and end points are recorded (written on a datasheet and marked as GPS waypoints).

--- SENTENCE 620 ---
Following any site resurveys, the starting points from the first survey are used as the starting points for all transects during subsequent surveys.

--- SENTENCE 621 ---
The ending points will vary as the survey is limited by time rather than distance or location, but the GPS points are always recorded so that the transect length can be approximated.

--- SENTENCE 622 ---

Transects through underwater visual census and photo-transects will be done to assess the impact of coral events on a smaller scale, aiming to record detailed information on the proportion and abundance of affected corals, considering factors like genus and growth forms.

--- SENTENCE 658 ---
The goal is to quantify variations in impact severity among coral taxa and functional groups and understand the event's broader spatial and temporal influence on the reef system.

--- SENTENCE 659 ---
These surveys are initiated once bleaching has been reported at specific reefs and reaches bleaching watch or warning levels.

--- SENTENCE 660 ---
Conducted in appropriate locations such as reef slope sites, reef flats, and pools, transects will be initiated in October to collect baseline data per year for each reef before SST rises.

--- SENTENCE 661 ---
If bleaching occurs, revisits are scheduled periodically (at predetermined intervals or as time allows) to monitor the progression of loss, survival, and mortality, with the monitoring period finalizing in April when weather conditions worsen.

--- SENTENCE 662 ---
The process involves setting the GPS on 'Tracking' mode, attaching it to a float, and recording the location every minute.

--- SENTENCE 663 ---
A float is attached to the reef at the beginning of the transect, where three 10-meter transects are laid out at the chosen depth on the reef flat or slope.

--- SENTENCE 664 ---
Observer one records fish species, abundance, and size, excluding cryptic species, across a 5-meter belt on each side.

--- SENTENCE 665 ---
Observer two counts every coral colony on a 1-meter-wide belt along the transect, recording bleaching severity.

--- SENTENCE 666 ---
Observer three takes photos using a monopod every 1 meter along the transect.

--- SENTENCE 667 ---

--- SENTENCE 668 ---
This ensures steady and slow movement for high-quality footage, aiming to record 3-5 meters of habitat within the frame.

# 10.8.2.2.3.

--- SENTENCE 670 ---
DETAILED TRANSECT METHODS AND TAGGING COLONY METHODS

--- SENTENCE 671 ---
The survey approach will prioritize reef flats, pools, and slope sites by implementing three 25 m transects per site.

--- SENTENCE 672 ---
These transects will be strategically laid parallel to the shore for reef flats and pools, while on the reef slope, they will follow the 10 m depth contour.

--- SENTENCE 673 ---
The surveys conducted at these sites will encompass benthic, fish, and bleaching assessments, providing a comprehensive understanding of reef health and ecology throughout the pre-, during, and post-bleaching phases.

--- SENTENCE 674 ---
The transects will be semi-permanent, marked at their starting and ending points with GPS coordinates and large cable ties.

--- SENTENCE 675 ---
This design allows for subsequent re-surveys of the same transects, facilitating comparisons of reef health in fish and benthic communities before, during, and after bleaching events.

--- SENTENCE 676 ---
Sites will be surveyed on a rotational basis, with one site assessed per week, focusing on benthic and fish assemblages and bleaching throughout the bleaching season.

--- SENTENCE 677 ---
The initiation of these surveys will coincide with the reporting or prediction of bleaching events.

--- SENTENCE 678 ---
This could involve a coordinated effort across agencies to survey all sites over two weeks, strategically targeting peak bleaching and the post-bleaching period to ensure a thorough understanding of the impacts across various locations.

--- SENTENCE 679 ---
Bleached colonies (10 per site) will be tagged to monitor mortality/recovery at the colony level over time.

--- SENTENCE 680 ---
Tagged colonies will be photographed during and after the bleaching event.

--- SENTENCE 681 ---
Colonies will be tagged using numbered cow tags and cable ties.

--- SENTENCE 682 ---
The software 'ImageJ' will calculate tissue mortality and/or recovery for each colony using the cow tag as a size reference.

--- SENTENCE 683 ---
To ensure enough replicate colonies are tagged, five bleaching colonies of two common coral genera (Acropora & Porites) with similar growth forms will be tagged per site.

--- SENTENCE 684 ---
Colonies will then be tracked to assess how they respond to bleaching stress throughout summer.

--- SENTENCE 685 ---
Comparisons of bleaching impact and recovery across sites and between colonies within one site can be made to determine whether the resilience of these genera differs across sites.

--- SENTENCE 686 ---
To secure the tag in place, cable ties or metal wire on dead reef/ rock next to the colony can be used, or alternatively, a nail and hammer can keep the tag flat without obscuring/ damaging the coral.

--- SENTENCE 687 ---
When possible, an image of the whole colony will be captured (i.e., table *Acropora*).

--- SENTENCE 688 ---
Otherwise, a monopod (~75 cm) will capture a standardized colony area (i.e., *Porites cylindrica* thicket).

--- SENTENCE 689 ---
Ensure cow tags are flat (not on an angle) when taking images of the whole colony - so they can be measured accurately in ImageJ.

# <span id="page-60-0"></span>10.9.

--- SENTENCE 690 ---
POST-BLEACHING AND RECOVERY SURVEYS

--- SENTENCE 691 ---
Coral bleaching does not always result in mortality; many corals can recover from bleaching events if anomalous temperatures soon return to within normal range, and there is limited stress from other threats, such as land-based sources of pollution.

--- SENTENCE 692 ---
When mortality does occur, the impacts on coral reef ecosystems are much more severe as the structural complexity of the habitat is degraded.

--- SENTENCE 693 ---
Dead corals are quickly colonized by algae, making them difficult to differentiate from corals that died from other causes, such as COTS[15](#page-60-2).

--- SENTENCE 694 ---
Branching corals such as *Acropora spp*.

--- SENTENCE 695 ---
do not maintain their structure for long after the colony is dead and are soon reduced to loose rubble.

--- SENTENCE 696 ---
During a lengthy bleaching event, mortality should be measured regularly to capture all deaths due to bleaching.

--- SENTENCE 697 ---
Ideally, corals will be surveyed every 2-3 weeks to capture mortality and reliably attribute it to bleaching.

--- SENTENCE 698 ---
The optimal sampling interval to monitor reef health following a bleaching event is after two months, six months, 12 months, and 24 months8.

--- SENTENCE 699 ---
Post-bleaching surveys should begin once all corals have recovered or died, and no bleached corals are visible.

--- SENTENCE 700 ---
Tagging individual colonies and surveying them before, during, and after bleaching events is a highly effective way to measure mortality and recovery.

--- SENTENCE 701 ---
This also allows genetic analysis of samples from individuals that have proved highly resilient (or highly vulnerable) to bleaching.

MANAGEMENT ACTIONS

There are currently no viable management actions at the disposal of local resource managers to mitigate the impacts of mass coral bleaching events directly.

--- SENTENCE 713 ---
However, perhaps the most important management actions related to climate change and coral bleaching are those that deal instead with local impacts by ensuring that coral reef ecosystems are as healthy as possible, which will speed recovery following the coral mass mortality associated with bleaching.

--- SENTENCE 714 ---
Coral reef managers can also implement temporary measures to minimize certain anthropogenic impacts during periods of known thermal stress, such as restricting entry to areas known to host susceptible coral communities.

--- SENTENCE 715 ---
The challenge of reducing greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions is a global-scale problem that needs to be addressed through international coordination among all levels of government.

--- SENTENCE 716 ---
Even if GHG emissions ceased immediately, SST is still expected to rise due to the lag between the change in atmospheric GHG concentrations and consequent changes in atmospheric and oceanic temperatures due to thermal expansion in the ocean.

--- SENTENCE 717 ---
Global average SST is expected to increase by 0.4-1.1° C by 2025[16](#page-61-1).

--- SENTENCE 718 ---
Within 40 years, 95% of all coral reefs are expected to experience severe bleaching on a near annual frequency[17](#page-61-2) which may lead to the loss of a significant portion of the world's coral reef resources.

The American Samoa Vessel Grounding Response Plan was developed to address vessels that have run aground and harmed the surrounding environment in American Samoan waters.

BACKGROUND

Vessel groundings are frequent in American Samoa due to the prevalence of fishing boats, cargo ships, passenger ferries, and adverse weather conditions that can sweep vessels aground.

--- SENTENCE 812 ---
Groundings in American Samoa are difficult to manage as funds are limited and vessel insurance is an uncommon practice.

# 11.4.1.1.

--- SENTENCE 814 ---
JIN SHIANG FA SHIPWRECK 1993

Historical groundings of vessels in American Samoa include the 1993 Jin Shiang Fa Shipwreck of a Taiwanese longliner at Rose Atoll NWR, which left 100,000 gallons of diesel spilt and 300 tons of iron debris[27](#page-68-2).

--- SENTENCE 815 ---
The excess iron leaching into the water has contributed to harmful algal blooms that are creating 'black reefs,' which refers to the reduced clarity and settlement of dark waste[28](#page-68-3).

--- SENTENCE 816 ---
Similarly, copper from the metal wreckage and toluene and benzene from the fuel oil had leached into the water column creating toxic zones for marine organisms9.

--- SENTENCE 817 ---
The wreck crushed the reef and killed many inhabiting invertebrates affecting 9,000 ㎡ of reef.

--- SENTENCE 818 ---
This caused a shift in the system's ecology by removing native organisms and inviting species that are attracted to macroalgae.

--- SENTENCE 819 ---
Prior to removal, impact assessments three years later showed meager recovery.

--- SENTENCE 820 ---
Removal efforts, coordinated by US F&W, began 11 Nov 1993 and are ongoing as of June 2024.

--- SENTENCE 821 ---
The vast and prolonged efforts and funds needed to investigate and remedy a vessel grounding should encourage proper regulations and subsequent enforcement as preventative measures.

--- SENTENCE 822 ---
The current removal strategy is to have a crew of surface supplied divers saw off metal debris underwater to then float up with lift bags.

§ 1951-1958: MARINE DEBRIS ACT

The Marine Debris Act was signed into law in 2006 and amended in 2012 and 2018.

--- SENTENCE 965 ---
The Act established the NOAA Marine Debris Program to address the adverse impacts of marine debris on the U.S. economy, the marine environment, and navigation safety through identification, determination of sources, assessment, prevention, reduction, and removal of marine debris.

--- SENTENCE 966 ---
The Act defines "marine debris" as "any persistent solid material that is manufactured or processed and directly or indirectly, intentionally or unintentionally, disposed of or abandoned into the marine environment or the Great Lakes."

--- SENTENCE 967 ---
The Act also established a program within USCG to reduce violations and improve implementation of the [International Convention for the](http://encyclopedia.thefreedictionary.com/International%2BConvention%2Bfor%2Bthe%2BPrevention%2Bof%2BPollution%2Bfrom%2BShips)

MARINE PROTECTION, RESEARCH, AND SANCTUARIES ACT (TITLE 1)

--- SENTENCE 975 ---
The MPRSA, also known as the Ocean Dumping Act, regulates the dumping of all types of materials into ocean waters and strictly limits the dumping of any material that would adversely affect human health, welfare or amenities, marine environment, ecological systems, or economic potentialities.[47](#page-76-0)

--- SENTENCE 976 ---
Title I of the Act generally prohibits (1) transportation of material from the U.S. for the purpose of ocean dumping; (2) transportation of material from anywhere for the purpose of ocean dumping by U.S. agencies or U.S. flagged vessels; and (3) dumping of material transported from outside the U.S. into the U.S. territorial sea.

--- SENTENCE 977 ---
A permit is required to deviate from these prohibitions.

--- SENTENCE 978 ---
Under MPRSA, the standard for permit issuance is whether the dumping will "unreasonably degrade or endanger" human health, welfare, or the marine environment.

--- SENTENCE 979 ---
Evaluation of whether degradation would be unreasonable or cause endangerment requires consideration of alternatives to ocean disposal, including land-based alternatives.[48](#page-76-1)

EPA is the MPRSA permitting agency for all materials except dredged material.

--- SENTENCE 980 ---
(In the case of dredged material, the decision to issue a permit is made by USACE, using EPA's environmental criteria and subject to EPA's concurrence.)

--- SENTENCE 981 ---
EPA has issued a general permit under the MPRSA for the transportation and disposal of vessels.

--- SENTENCE 982 ---
The MPRSA implements the requirements of the 1972 London Convention on the Prevention of Marine Pollution by Dumping of Wastes and Other Matter (London Convention), the international ocean dumping treaty.

--- SENTENCE 983 ---
The U.S. Government reports the ocean dumping of vessels to the International Maritime Organization pursuant to the London Convention.[49](#page-76-2)

§ 1431 ET SEQ: NATIONAL MARINE SANCTUARIES ACT

Under the NMSA, NOAA manages a system of 14 marine protected areas in U.S. waters.

--- SENTENCE 987 ---
The statute provides NOAA the authority to address any activities that are likely to destroy, cause the loss of, or injure sanctuary resources.

--- SENTENCE 988 ---
For some sites within the system, this authority includes activities that occur outside the boundaries but that could enter and injure sanctuary resources.

--- SENTENCE 989 ---
Within these sanctuaries, NOAA can seek damages from responsible parties to cover response, injury and damage assessment, restoration, and replacement of the injured habitat or acquisition of equivalent habitat, and compensation of the public for the value of the injured resources until full recovery.

--- SENTENCE 990 ---
Under the NMSA, NOAA may have financial resources available to assist with the response, particularly if there is no longer a nexus to OPA 90 or CERCLA.

--- SENTENCE 991 ---
The NMSA also requires consultation under Section 304(d)[50](#page-77-0) for any activities that are "likely to destroy, cause the loss of or injure a sanctuary resource."

--- SENTENCE 992 ---
During consultation, NOAA may recommend reasonable and prudent alternatives to protect sanctuary resources and to address activities that conflict with site- specific activities such as disturbance of the seabed, and discharge or deposit of materials that could occur during assessment or removal activities.

§409, 411, 412, 414, AND 415: WRECK ACT

The Rivers and Harbors Appropriation Act of 1899 (RHA) contains several sections known as the Wreck Act, which provides USACE the authority to remove sunken vessels that pose an obstruction to navigation.

--- SENTENCE 994 ---
This statute applies to navigable waters of the U.S. Under the Wreck Act, the owner, operator, or lessee of a submerged vessel is responsible for immediately marking the vessel with a buoy or beacon during the day and a lighted lantern at night.[51](#page-77-1)

--- SENTENCE 995 ---
The markings must remain until the vessel is removed.

--- SENTENCE 996 ---
The owner, operator, or lessee is also required to "diligently" commence "immediate" removal of the submerged vessel.

--- SENTENCE 997 ---
If the vessel is not removed within 30 days, it will be considered abandoned and USACE may take action to remove the vessel from the navigable waters.[52](#page-77-2)

--- SENTENCE 998 ---
The determination of whether a wreck poses an obstacle to navigation rests initially with USACE and a reviewing court will only overturn the determination if the decision is found to be arbitrary and capricious.

--- SENTENCE 999 ---
If USACE determines the existence of the submerged or wrecked vessel in the navigable waters of the U.S. is creating an emergency situation, the vessel owner, operator, or lessee will be given 24 hours to begin removal of the vessel using the most expeditious method available.[53](#page-77-3) USACE makes every effort to locate, contact, and work with the vessel owner/ operator / lessee.

--- SENTENCE 1000 ---
However, if the vessel is not removed or steps are not taken in an expeditious manner to secure the vessel's removal, USACE may intercede to remove, sink, or destroy the vessel to alleviate the situation.

--- SENTENCE 1001 ---
The vessel owner, operator, or lessee will then be liable to the U.S. for all costs associated with the government's action.

--- SENTENCE 1002 ---
If the owner fails or refuses to reimburse the government within 30 days after notification, the vessel may be sold with the proceeds going to the U.S. Treasury.[54](#page-77-4)

The Salvage Facilities Act authorizes contracting of U.S. Navy Supervisor of Salvage (SUPSALV).

--- SENTENCE 1025 ---
The Salvage Facilities Act, enacted after World War II, has several objectives, including:

- To provide salvage resources to protect the redeployment of government-owned war materiel on chartered ships (but not in excess of national defense needs).

--- SENTENCE 1026 ---
- To allow (but not require) the Navy to render salvage services to private vessels when commercial salvors are not available, charging for those services to support the Navy's Salvage facilities.

# MARPOL 73/78

*Adapted from [USCG Office of Commercial Vessel Compliance](https://www.dco.uscg.mil/Our-Organization/Assistant-Commandant-for-Prevention-Policy-CG-5P/Inspections-Compliance-CG-5PC-/Commercial-Vessel-Compliance/Domestic-Compliance-Division/MARPOL/#:~:text=MARPOL%2073%2F78%20consists%20of,%2C%20III%2C%20V%20and%20VI.)

--- SENTENCE 1056 ---
(CG-CVC):*

MARPOL 73/78 consists of six separate Annexes, each set out regulations covering the various sources of ship-generated pollution.

--- SENTENCE 1057 ---
Annex I and II are mandatory for all signatory nations to MARPOL while Annexes III, IV, V and VI are optional.

--- SENTENCE 1058 ---
Currently, the U.S. is signatory to Annexes I, II, III, V and VI.

--- SENTENCE 1059 ---
Annexes I, II, V and VI have been incorporated into U.S. law by the Act to Prevent Pollution from Ships (APPS) and implemented within 33 USC 1901 and 33 CFR 151.

--- SENTENCE 1060 ---
The U.S. incorporates Annex III by the Hazardous Materials Transportation Act (HMTA) implemented within 46 USC 2101 and 49 CFR 171 -174 and 176.

--- SENTENCE 1061 ---
Although the U.S. has not ratified Annex IV, the U.S. has equivalent regulations for the treatment and discharge standards of shipboard sewage – the Federal Water Pollution Control Act (FWPCA) as amended by the Clean Water Act and implemented by 33 USC 1251 and 33 CFR 159.

Act to Prevent Pollution from Ships (APPS) 1980

# *From the [National Whistleblower Center:](https://www.whistleblowers.org/what-is-the-act-to-prevent-pollution-from-ships/)*

In order to implement the provisions of MARPOL, the [U.S. passed](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/96/hr6665) the Act to Prevent Pollution from Ships (APPS) in 1980.

--- SENTENCE 1063 ---
APPS applies to U.S. commercial vessels, as well as non-U.S. commercial vessels operating in U.S. waters or ports of U.S. jurisdiction.

--- SENTENCE 1064 ---
APPS makes it a crime to knowingly violate certain provisions of MARPOL and other oil pollution laws.

--- SENTENCE 1065 ---
The United States Coast Guard and the U.S Environmental Protection Agency are the main enforcers of MARPOL and APPS within the U.S. Additionally, APPS includes whistleblower provisions to help combat illegal pollution and empower and incentivize workers to expose any known information about pollution from ships.

*Dry Dock Dismantling for Smaller Vessels*

To reduce the possibility of discharging contaminants to the environment, the optimal location for vessel dismantling is within a National Pollution Discharge Elimination System (NPDES) permitted dry dock or land-based industrial facility.

--- SENTENCE 1175 ---
If dismantling at an NPDES permitted facility is impracticable (e.g., the hull is unsound and unfit for lifting or transport without disintegrating) and dismantling is conducted at an unpermitted land-based facility, a dismantling pad must be constructed and the work conducted during dry weather.

--- SENTENCE 1176 ---
Constructing the dismantling pad on concrete or asphalt is preferred.

--- SENTENCE 1177 ---
The dismantling pad must consist of a high-density polyethylene (HDPE) liner (minimum of 20-millimeter thick), or equivalent, overlaid with geotextile fabric and 4 to 6 inches of clean class II road base to protect the liner.

--- SENTENCE 1178 ---
The pad must be constructed with a 6-inch-high perimeter berm beneath the HDPE liner to prevent fluid from leaving the contained area.

--- SENTENCE 1179 ---
All dismantling activities — including the draining of fluids — must take place on the dismantling pad.

--- SENTENCE 1180 ---
All nearby storm drain inlets must be sealed to prevent any fluid discharge to the storm drain system.

--- SENTENCE 1181 ---
The dismantling pad and all residual materials within the pad must be properly disposed of upon project completion.

--- SENTENCE 1182 ---
Stationary equipment located outside the dismantling pad, such as generators and waste liquid storage tanks, should have secondary containment or be placed on spill pads

Dust and airborne debris from demolition operations must be minimized.

--- SENTENCE 1183 ---
The activities must be discontinued during high wind events or contained so that dust and airborne debris are maintained within the vessel dismantling pad.

--- SENTENCE 1184 ---
In addition, appropriate abatement measures for contaminants, such as lead-based paint, polychlorinated biphenyl-containing electrical equipment, and asbestos, must be in place prior to dismantling.

OVERVIEW OF CORAL DISEASE

Coral disease is a natural phenomenon that has become exacerbated by climate change and anthropogenic activities, especially those resulting in low water quality.

--- SENTENCE 1277 ---
Diseases affecting corals can be bacterial, viral, or fungal, but pathogenesis is poorly understood and is likely due to multiple factors, which make it difficult to treat and prevent.

--- SENTENCE 1278 ---
Most diseases occur naturally within reefs at a background rate of 3-5%[67](#page-98-4).As of writing this, there are approximately 40 coral diseases

<span id="page-98-4"></span><sup>67</sup> [https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial\\_corals/coral10\\_disease.html](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_corals/coral10_disease.html)

affecting scleractinians, with higher prevalence in the Red Sea and Caribbean.

--- SENTENCE 1279 ---
However, the rate and range of coral disease in the Indo-Pacific has been expanding since 2000.

--- SENTENCE 1280 ---
Factors contributing to the expansion of disease include loss of herbivorous fishes, contamination from LBSP outfalls, warming, acidification, and loss of coral genetic diversity.

--- SENTENCE 1281 ---
This disease protocol is initiated any time a potential disease is reported or observed in the field.

--- SENTENCE 1282 ---
While information can be gathered at the local level, it is advised to seek guidance from disease experts.

--- SENTENCE 1283 ---
This should be done as soon as there is any concern regarding a possible coral disease outbreak.

# **Step 4 — Continuous Monitoring: Develop a Regular Monitoring Schedule**

Once it is determined there is an active disease outbreak a regular monitoring schedule should be developed.

--- SENTENCE 1415 ---
Monitoring can be conducted at the original outbreak site, priority areas, and/or at any disease hot spots identified during previous assessments.

--- SENTENCE 1416 ---
Frequency of the monitoring should be based on the rate of disease progression and mortality.

--- SENTENCE 1417 ---
Where there are high rates of mortality sites should be revisited weekly or biweekly if possible.

--- SENTENCE 1418 ---
These monitoring trips should (modified from the Coral Disease Handbook, 2008):

- Revisit tagged colonies to determine disease progression and note the health status of the colony (progressing, stasis, recovering, dead);
- Look for new lesions either on infected colonies or on previously uninfected colonies;
- Look for signs of recovery either through re-sheeting over the skeleton or through new coral recruits.
- Keep track of abiotic factors that may allow for the disease to overtake e.g. temperature, low tides, increased freshwater input, etc.

--- SENTENCE 1419 ---
When possible, Step 1 should be repeated to see if the disease has spread to new sites.

DISEASE PREVENTION

There are few known vectors of coral disease since pathogenesis for the majority of diseases remains unknown.

--- SENTENCE 1424 ---
The USCRTF Pacific Preparedness Working Group is a great resource of information for preventing disease transmission in the Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 1425 ---
This Response Plan heavily recommends the following preventative steps:

- The highest risk of disease transmission to the Pacific is via ships coming from the Caribbean.

--- SENTENCE 1426 ---
Ballast water discharge has been shown to transport disease along reefs and discharge in Pago Pago Harbor can be tracked via the [National Ballast Information](https://invasions.si.edu/nbicdb/tanks?state=AS&port=2875&year=2024)

--- SENTENCE 1427 ---
Any discharge should be monitored in the case of a disease outbreak.](https://invasions.si.edu/nbicdb/tanks?state=AS&port=2875&year=2024)

--- SENTENCE 1428 ---
The USCG has mandatory ballast water discharge plans and has recommended the adoption of voluntary ballast water management plans, as well.

--- SENTENCE 1429 ---
The use of alternative ballast water management systems (BWMS) has increased since 2015 in American Samoa, but Stony Coral Tissue Loss Disease (SCLTD) has been shown to resist UV treatments and remain viable within ballast tanks for long periods of time.
- Another confirmed disease vector is from ship biofouling.

--- SENTENCE 1430 ---
This plan recommends that any hull cleaning for sailing yachts coming from the Caribbean should be done on land.

--- SENTENCE 1431 ---
- Thoroughly disinfecting gear after visiting contaminated sites, please refer to Figure 23.

--- SENTENCE 1432 ---
- Restrict the translocation of corals.

--- SENTENCE 1433 ---
No corals that show any signs of disease may be translocated between sites.

--- SENTENCE 1434 ---
Restoration projects requiring translocation must thoroughly inspect any corals to be moved.

--- SENTENCE 1435 ---
- A study on the Great Barrier Reef found that a healthy reef environment with functionally diverse fish communities had lower disease prevalence.[77](#page-109-1) MPAs in American Samoa should continue to be managed and possibly extended to vulnerable reefs.
- Outreach flyers to discourage swimmers and snorkelers from touching corals due to the potential for disease spread.

span id="page-111-0"></span>INTRODUCTION

This Permitting Guidance for Grounded Vessel Removal in American Samoa was developed to provide the regulatory requirements of addressing vessels that have run aground and harmed the surrounding environment in American Samoan waters.

--- SENTENCE 1448 ---
This guidance provides information tailored to the environment and authorities of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1449 ---
Vessel groundings include any vessel that is situated on the seabed or waterside and has therefore lost its ability to navigate.

--- SENTENCE 1450 ---
Vessel groundings trample reefs and other benthic habitats, killing sea life in their wake.

--- SENTENCE 1451 ---
The longer a vessel is left unattended, the more damage is incurred from vessel drift.

--- SENTENCE 1452 ---
Exact impacts of grounded and abandoned vessels are wide and varying.

--- SENTENCE 1453 ---
Examples include: shading seagrass, crushing coral, stirring up sediment, and leaching toxic chemicals.

--- SENTENCE 1454 ---
In many places, including American Samoa, removing ADVs is challenging, as there are no governing laws that clearly identify responsible parties for clean up or assign sources of funding.

--- SENTENCE 1455 ---
The vessel grounding response plan will help to quickly and efficiently organize assessment and removal activities to avoid further damage and gain a full understanding of the impacts and repercussions to inform conservation goals as well as associated legal matters.

--- SENTENCE 1456 ---
This document will supplement the American Samoa Vessel Grounding Response Plan by helping to identify on and off-island entities that can assist in removal as well as necessary permitting actions.

--- SENTENCE 1457 ---
This guide details permit requirements for dealing with a vessel grounding in American Samoa including: ecological impact assessments, removal, and disposal.

# <span id="page-117-1"></span>ARMY CORPS OF ENGINEERS (USACE)

From the USCRTF "A Guide for Coral Restoration Permitting in the Seven U.S. Coral Reef Jurisdictions" (2023).

- The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers' (USACE) Regulatory Program involves the regulation of discharges of dredged or fill material into waters of the United States and structures or work in navigable waters of the United States, under section 404 of the Clean Water Act and section 10 of the Rivers and Harbors Act of 1899.

--- SENTENCE 1515 ---
A proposed project's impacts to these areas will determine what permit type is required.

--- SENTENCE 1516 ---
- Permit Types:

- o An individual, or standard permit, is issued when projects have more than minimal individual or cumulative impacts, are evaluated using additional environmental criteria, and involve a more comprehensive public interest review.

--- SENTENCE 1517 ---
- o A general permit is issued for structures, work, or discharges that will result in only minimal adverse effects.

--- SENTENCE 1518 ---
General permits are issued on a nationwide, regional, or state basis for particular categories of activities.

--- SENTENCE 1519 ---
General permits are usually valid for five years and may be re-authorized by USACE.

--- SENTENCE 1520 ---
There are three types of general permits:
  - Nationwide permits are issued by USACE on a national basis and are designed to streamline Department of the Army authorization of projects such as commercial developments, utility lines, or road improvements that produce minimal impact to the nation's aquatic environment.
  - A regional general permit is issued for a specific geographic area by an individual USACE District.

--- SENTENCE 1521 ---
Each regional general permit has specific terms and conditions, all of which must be met for project-specific actions to be verified.
  - Programmatic general permits are based on an existing state, local, or other federal program and designed to avoid duplication of that program.

--- SENTENCE 1522 ---
A State Programmatic General Permit (SPGP) is a type of permit that is issued by USACE and designed to eliminate duplication of effort between USACE districts and state regulatory programs that provide similar protection to aquatic resources.

--- SENTENCE 1523 ---
In some states, the SPGP replaces some or all of the USACE nationwide permits, which results in greater efficiency in the overall permitting process.

# <span id="page-118-0"></span>MAGNUSON-STEVENS ACT-ESSENTIAL FISH HABITAT (EFH) CONSULTATION

Adapted from the USCRTF "A Guide for Coral Restoration Permitting in the Seven U.S. Coral Reef Jurisdictions" (2023).

--- SENTENCE 1525 ---
- A consultation with NOAA Fisheries on Essential Fish Habitat is required if:
  - o A federal agency has authorized, funded, or undertaken part or all of a proposed activity.

--- SENTENCE 1526 ---
For example, if a project requires a federal permit, then the federal agency issuing the permit must consult with NOAA Fisheries.

- o

--- SENTENCE 1527 ---
The action will adversely affect EFH.

--- SENTENCE 1528 ---
To determine whether an action will occur within EFH, use this [tool](https://www.habitat.noaa.gov/apps/efhmapper/?page=page_5).

--- SENTENCE 1529 ---
All of American Samoa's islands are located within EFH (NMFS, 2023).

--- SENTENCE 1530 ---
An adverse effect includes direct or indirect physical, chemical, or biological alterations.

--- SENTENCE 1531 ---
It includes adverse changes to:
  - Waters or substrate.
  - Species and their habitat.

--- SENTENCE 1532 ---
- Other ecosystem components.

--- SENTENCE 1533 ---
- Quality and/or quantity of EFH.

--- SENTENCE 1534 ---
If a federal agency determines that an action will not adversely affect EFH, and NOAA Fisheries agrees, no consultation is required.
- o Private landowners and state agencies are not required to consult with NOAA Fisheries.

--- SENTENCE 1535 ---
However, NOAA Fisheries must provide conservation recommendations on any state action that would substantially adversely affect EFH.

--- SENTENCE 1536 ---
- o Consultations are conducted in concert with the NOAA Fisheries Regional Office overseeing your jurisdiction.

--- SENTENCE 1537 ---
- o Normally EFH Assessments are prepared independently from other assessments (NEPA, ESA) (NMFS, 2023).

--- SENTENCE 1538 ---
- o There are both abbreviated and expanded consultations, depending on the extent of proposed EFH impacts (NMFS, 2023).

--- SENTENCE 1539 ---
- o Full EFHA Consultation Process in the Pacific Islands can be found here: [https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/pacific-islands/consultations/essential-fish-habitat](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/pacific-islands/consultations/essential-fish-habitat-consultations-pacific-islands)[consultations-pacific-islands](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/pacific-islands/consultations/essential-fish-habitat-consultations-pacific-islands)

<span id="page-127-1"></span>ARMY CORPS OF ENGINEERS (USACE)

--- SENTENCE 1619 ---
From the USCRTF "A Guide for Coral Restoration Permitting in the Seven U.S. Coral Reef Jurisdictions" (2023).

•

--- SENTENCE 1620 ---
The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers' (USACE) Regulatory Program involves the regulation of discharges of dredged or fill material into waters of the United States and structures or work in navigable waters of the United States, under section 404 of the Clean Water Act and section 10 of the Rivers and Harbors Act of 1899.

--- SENTENCE 1621 ---
A proposed project's impacts to these areas will determine what permit type is required.

# • Permit Types:

- o An individual, or standard permit, is issued when projects have more than minimal individual or cumulative impacts, are evaluated using additional environmental criteria, and involve a more comprehensive public interest review.

--- SENTENCE 1622 ---
- o A general permit is issued for structures, work, or discharges that will result in only minimal adverse effects.

--- SENTENCE 1623 ---
General permits are issued on a nationwide, regional, or state basis for particular categories of activities.

--- SENTENCE 1624 ---
General permits are usually valid for five years and may be re-authorized by USACE.

--- SENTENCE 1625 ---
There are three types of general permits:
  - Nationwide permits are issued by USACE on a national basis and are designed to streamline Department of the Army authorization of projects such as commercial developments, utility lines, or road improvements that produce minimal impact to the nation's aquatic environment.
  - A regional general permit is issued for a specific geographic area by an individual USACE District.

--- SENTENCE 1626 ---
Each regional general permit has specific terms and conditions, all of which must be met for project-specific actions to be verified.
  - Programmatic general permits are based on an existing state, local, or other federal program and designed to avoid duplication of that program.

--- SENTENCE 1627 ---
A State Programmatic General Permit (SPGP) is a type of permit that is issued by USACE and designed to eliminate duplication of effort between USACE districts and state regulatory programs that provide similar protection to aquatic resources.

--- SENTENCE 1628 ---
In some states, the SPGP replaces some or all of the USACE nationwide permits, which results in greater efficiency in the overall permitting process.

# <span id="page-128-0"></span>MAGNUSON-STEVENS ACT-ESSENTIAL FISH HABITAT (EFH) CONSULTATION

Adapted from the USCRTF "A Guide for Coral Restoration Permitting in the Seven U.S. Coral Reef Jurisdictions" (2023).

--- SENTENCE 1630 ---
- A consultation with NOAA Fisheries on Essential Fish Habitat is required if:
  - o A federal agency has authorized, funded, or undertaken part or all of a proposed activity.

--- SENTENCE 1631 ---
For example, if a project requires a federal permit, then the federal agency issuing the permit must consult with NOAA Fisheries.

--- SENTENCE 1632 ---
The action will adversely affect EFH.

--- SENTENCE 1633 ---
To determine whether an action will occur within EFH, use this [tool](https://www.habitat.noaa.gov/apps/efhmapper/?page=page_5).

--- SENTENCE 1634 ---
All of American Samoa's islands are located within EFH (NMFS, 2023).

--- SENTENCE 1635 ---
An adverse effect includes direct or indirect physical, chemical, or biological alterations.

--- SENTENCE 1636 ---
It includes adverse changes to:

- Waters or substrate.

--- SENTENCE 1637 ---
- Species and their habitat.

--- SENTENCE 1638 ---
- Other ecosystem components.

--- SENTENCE 1639 ---
- Quality and/or quantity of EFH.

--- SENTENCE 1640 ---
If a federal agency determines that an action will not adversely affect EFH, and NOAA Fisheries agrees, no consultation is required.
- o Private landowners and state agencies are not required to consult with NOAA Fisheries.

--- SENTENCE 1641 ---
However, NOAA Fisheries must provide conservation recommendations on any state action that would substantially adversely affect EFH.

--- SENTENCE 1642 ---
- o Consultations are conducted in concert with the NOAA Fisheries Regional Office overseeing your jurisdiction.

--- SENTENCE 1643 ---
- o Normally EFH Assessments are prepared independently from other assessments (NEPA, ESA) (NMFS, 2023).

--- SENTENCE 1644 ---
- o There are both abbreviated and expanded consultations, depending on the extent of proposed EFH impacts (NMFS, 2023).

## <span id="page-3-0"></span>**Review of ASCRMP Objectives**

The primary intent of the ASCRMP is to collect data on a yearly basis by on-island staff in order to detect change and trends in the measured parameters over a period of years.

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
The ASCRMP was not designed primarily to compare sites to each other based on geographic distribution, reef type, exposure, or impacts.

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
However, when patterns appear in the data, such patterns will be explored, keeping in mind that the sites were not randomly chosen, nor chosen to represent all habitats, exposures, impacts, etc.

--- SENTENCE 22 ---
Comparison with the results of other studies can help determine whether the observed patterns are real or spurious.

--- SENTENCE 23 ---
Nor does the ASCRMP attempt to survey and describe all of the island's reefs.

--- SENTENCE 24 ---
Rather, the purpose of ASCRMP is to collect data from the monitoring sites in order to provide long-term trend information about marine resources.

--- SENTENCE 25 ---
Change over time is the most important objective and should be kept in mind when reading and reviewing the ASCRMP reports.

--- SENTENCE 26 ---
As 2005 is the first year in the data collection for this long-term monitoring plan, this represents one data point.

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
In following years of data collection for the ASCRMP, additional data points data will be added and can then be compared and analyzed within each site in order to look for trends in coral reef health.

--- SENTENCE 28 ---
## **Monitoring Sites**

Eleven sites were chosen by the CRAG Monitoring Working Group as a reasonable sample size in the ASCRMP's first year of implementation.

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
Monitoring sites in the ASCRMP represented an equitable distribution around the main island of Tutuila and the nearby small island of Aunu'u, providing a reasonable geographic distribution and some of the variety of reef types and exposures (i.e. windward/leeward).

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
The site plan for the ASCRMP incorporated federal, territory, and community-based MPAs.

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
Three of the 11 monitoring sites are community-based MPAs within DMWR's Community-based Fisheries Management Program (CFMP) (Fagamalo, Vatia, and Amaua) and 2 sites are federal MPAs (Fagatele and Tafeu).

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
Based on population density and subsequent impacts to the watershed, the ASCRMP incorporated villages in the complete range of the ASEPA watershed classification scale; pristine, minimal, intermediate, and extensive (DiDonato 2004).

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
Three of the 11 monitoring sites (Leone, Nu'uuli and Faga'alu) are categorized as Extensive, three sites are categorized as Intermediate (Amaua, Aoa, and Fagasa), one site is categorized as Medium (Vatia), and three sites are categorized as Pristine (Fagatele, Tafeu and Fagamalo).

## **Timeline**

The number of monitoring sites was chosen based on logistical constraints and the desire to complete the monitoring program within the same season.

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
Additionally, the ASCRMP manual instructs that data collection of the core and supplemental monitoring programs should be conducted during the same quarter each year in an effort to minimize seasonal differences such as water temperature and prevailing winds.

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
Monitoring for the 2005 ASCRMP for the core sampling regime commenced on March 10, 2005, and concluded on April 21, 2005.

--- SENTENCE 39 ---
All replicates for bottom cover, invertebrates, and fish were accomplished in two dives per day or one monitoring site per day.

--- SENTENCE 40 ---
In total, the 11 monitoring sites were accomplished in 11 field days.

--- SENTENCE 41 ---
However, these field days were spread out over 31 business days.

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
Monitoring for the 2005 ASCRMP for the supplemental sampling regime for biodiversity counts commenced on April 22, 2005, and concluded on June 1, 2005.

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
One dive at each site was conducted.

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
In total, the 11 monitoring sites were accomplished in 7 field days.

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
However, these field days were spread out over 29 business days.

--- SENTENCE 46 ---
Overall, monitoring for the 2005 ASCRMP were accomplished in 18 field days spread out over 60 business days or 3 calendar months.

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
Delays were due to sharing the DMWR boat with other DMWR programs and visiting researchers, a boater safety course held at DMWR, 5 government holidays, off-island workshops, and on-island meetings and workshops such as CRAG, Western Pacific Fishery Information Network (WPacFIN), Ocean Symposium, and the CFMP Monitoring Workshop.

Monitoring for the ASCRMP was conducted on the reef slopes between 8-10m depth isobath.

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
Within each area, sampling sites were located with GPS coordinates.

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
Four replicates for bottom cover and six replicates for fish were conducted.

--- SENTENCE 76 ---
Divers boated to the monitoring site using a GPS and other navigational tools.

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
Once at the site, a weighted marker was dropped to mark the location.

--- SENTENCE 78 ---
Divers then descended at this line to the 8-10m depth contour, took the bearing as described in the Monitoring Tools Handbook, and began the monitoring.

## <span id="page-6-0"></span>*Bottom Cover*

Benthic attributes were recorded by the Coral Diver in four replicates of 50m transects using a point-intercept method.

--- SENTENCE 80 ---
Every ½ meter (100 data points per transect), bottom cover was recorded in categories and corals were identified to life form and genus (species when possible).

--- SENTENCE 81 ---
To reduce observer bias when the transect tape was not stationary, the first item spotted when the diver was directly over the point was recorded.

--- SENTENCE 82 ---
Bottom categories included crustose coralline algae, branching coralline algae, living hard coral, soft coral, macro algae, filamentous algae, *Halimeda*, invertebrate, dead coral, bleached coral, rubble, sand, and rock.

--- SENTENCE 83 ---
There were some foliose coralline algae (thin flat grey-brown plates) that were included in the crustose coralline algae.

--- SENTENCE 84 ---
Filamentous algae included all rock surfaces that had a fine, barely visible fuzz of filamentous algae.

--- SENTENCE 85 ---
Because the 2005 Coral Diver had extensive coral taxonomy training, corals were recorded in lifeform, genus, and where possible species.

--- SENTENCE 86 ---
Coral lifeforms included branching, massive, encrusting, foliose, submassive, mushroom, blue, *Acorpora* branching, *Acropora* digitate, *Acropora* table, *Acropora* encrusting, and *Acropora* submassive.

--- SENTENCE 87 ---
However, all corals were recorded in lifeform as well as species.

--- SENTENCE 88 ---
Thus, data from 2005 and future years will be comparable in lifeform even if species is not available.

--- SENTENCE 89 ---
Only one benthic category was recorded per point, so the totals add up to 100% cover.

--- SENTENCE 90 ---
If there were multiple layers below (and/or above) the tape, only the cover category on the top layer was recorded.

--- SENTENCE 91 ---
Anthropogenic damage was also recorded, such as debris and anchor damage.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
Occurrence of coral bleaching, disease, or predation (such as by the Crown-of-Thorns (COTS) *

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
Acanthaster* or the snail *Drupella*) were recorded if present in the transect under the recording points.

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
On a wider area, bleaching, disease, and anthropogenic damage were recorded anecdotally.

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
Rugosity was also measured by the Coral and Invertebrate Divers together by laying a 20m metal chain under the transect tape, following the reef contour.

#### *Fish*

Census for fish was performed by the Fish Diver who conducted six replicates of a modified Bohnsack stationary point count (SPC) with a radius of 7.5m. Approximate time to complete one SPC ranged from 11-18 minutes.

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
The first transect tape for benthic cover was laid 5m beyond the radius of the first SPC.

--- SENTENCE 97 ---
The Fish Diver recorded the start time and proceeded to an area to begin a SPC.

--- SENTENCE 98 ---
The diver visually estimated a 7.5m radius and immediately began recording data.

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
The observer enumerated fish within this visually specified area.

--- SENTENCE 100 ---
After the SPC was conducted, the Fish Diver confirmed the radius of the SPC with a transect tape.

--- SENTENCE 101 ---
A pre-printed list served as a guide for fish species that included key reef species, indicator species, and functional groups (see ASCRMP manual).

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
Additional information on length assessments for selected key reef fish species were also recorded in order to derive biomass.

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
Key reef species are those species targeted and harvested by inshore fishermen in American Samoa and correspond to those in DMWR Creel and Market Survey Program.

--- SENTENCE 104 ---
After the first SPC was completed, the Fish Diver swam at least 7.5m beyond the end of the first transect tape, recorded the start time, and began the second SPC.

--- SENTENCE 105 ---
After the second SPC was completed, the Fish Diver swam at least 7.5m beyond the end of the second transect tape, recorded the start time, and began the third SPC.

--- SENTENCE 106 ---
After the third SPC was completed, the Fish Diver reeled in the second transect tape used by the Coral and Invertebrate Divers for bottom cover assessments.

--- SENTENCE 107 ---
For the key reef species, the larger, mobile species groups were counted first to ensure these types of fish were counted before they left the census area.

--- SENTENCE 108 ---
The Fish Diver hovered 1m above the substrate in the middle of the circular area and began counting the first species group on the list while slowly turning in a circle.

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
Only one group of species was recorded while the Fish Diver rotated 360 degrees during a period of 1 minute.

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
Then a different species group was recorded on the next rotation.

--- SENTENCE 111 ---
Once a species group had been counted, no additional data were recorded for that species group.

--- SENTENCE 112 ---
Individuals (juvenile, initial, and terminal phases) of large wrasse species whose adult length for the species is a minimum of 25cm were included in this assessment of key reef species.

--- SENTENCE 113 ---
Length assessment of key reef fish species were recorded to centimeter, but were simplified and also recorded in the database in 5cm size categories, according to the ASCRMP manual.

--- SENTENCE 114 ---
Thus, data from 2005 and future years will be comparable in those categories.

--- SENTENCE 115 ---
Fish biomass estimates were calculated using the length assessments recorded during the 6 SPCs at each of the 11 ASCRMP sites.

--- SENTENCE 116 ---
The biomass was calculated by using the formula W=A\*L^B where W=weight, L= length, and A&B= values generated from slopes of length and weight of each species.

--- SENTENCE 117 ---
Other functional groups where only abundances (not length assessments) were recorded during the SPC included butterflyfish, angelfish, surgeonfish, and triggerfish.

--- SENTENCE 118 ---
Selected indicator species were also recorded.

--- SENTENCE 119 ---
The species indicative of healthy coral reef ecosystems in American Samoa according to McCardle (2003) included in the SPC counts for the ASCRMP are the following: *Plectroglyphidodon dicki* (Blackbar damselfish), *Melichthys vidua* (Pinktail triggerfish), *Labroides* sp. (cleaner wrasse), *Chaetodon vagabundus*

--- SENTENCE 120 ---
(Vagabond butterflyfish), and *Labrichthys unilineatus* (Tubelip wrasse).

--- SENTENCE 121 ---
Additionally, some of these species included in these groupings are obligate corallivores.

--- SENTENCE 122 ---
Each of the functional groups and indicator species was allotted one minute during the SPC.

--- SENTENCE 123 ---
However, since many of the surgeonfish are locally abundant (i.e. Lined Bristletooth, *Ctenochaetus striatus*), several of these species were allotted one minute separately from the rest of the species in that grouping.

--- SENTENCE 124 ---
Two species of surgeonfish, *Ctenochaetus striatus* (Lined Surgeonfish) and *Acanthurus nigrofuscus*

--- SENTENCE 125 ---
(Brown Surgeonfish) were lumped together in the category of Pone, a Samoan name for the dark brown or black-colored surgeonfish harvested from the reefs.

--- SENTENCE 126 ---
Three species of cleaner wrasse *Labroides dimidatus*

--- SENTENCE 127 ---
(Bluestreak Cleaner Wrasse), *L. bicolor* (Bicolor Cleaner Wrasse) and *L. rubrolabiatus* (Redlip Cleaner Wrasse), were lumped together during the data collection.

--- SENTENCE 128 ---
All species recorded are diurnally active, and not cryptic.

--- SENTENCE 129 ---
Nocturnal and cryptic species are not well recorded by UVC (Brock, 1982)

--- SENTENCE 130 ---
This report includes only the values of mean number for the groupings of corallivores, Pone, all surgeonfish, *P. dickii*.

--- SENTENCE 131 ---
A*canthurus lineatus* (Striped Surgeonfish), locally called Alogo, is a heavily harvested species and is also included in this report.

--- SENTENCE 132 ---
Mean numbers of the other groupings (i.e. cleaner wrasse, etc.) are available in the database.

## **Parameters and Methods in Supplemental Monitoring for Biodiversity**

Biodiversity counts for coral, fish, and other invertebrates were included as supplemental monitoring.

--- SENTENCE 145 ---
Biodiversity counts were conducted on a separate field day from the core sampling regime and were recorded during approximately one hour roving dives at each dive site.

--- SENTENCE 146 ---
All corals, fish, and invertebrates were identified in situ where possible, selected specimens photographed with a still camera, and samples collected when necessary.

--- SENTENCE 147 ---
Counts of COTS and giant clams were taken to supplement the macroinvertebrate transect data.

--- SENTENCE 148 ---
Any sea turtles sighted were reported on an In-Water Turtle Sighting Form and given to DMWR's Wildlife Division.

--- SENTENCE 149 ---
Observations of coral disease, bleaching, coralline algae disease, algae blooms, and coral predation (such as by COTS or *Drupella*) were recorded, as well as any other unusual phenomena.

--- SENTENCE 150 ---
Abundances for coral were estimated using the DAFOR (Dominant, Abundant, Frequent, Occasional, Rare) logarithmic scale.

--- SENTENCE 151 ---
During fish biodiversity counts, species were recorded and given an abundance category according to the Roving Diver Technique (RDT) used by Reef Environmental Education Foundation (REEF).

--- SENTENCE 152 ---
The categories are order of magnitude estimates of the number of individuals signed during the survey: **S**ingle=1, **F**ew=2-10, **M**any=11-100, and **A**bundant=over 100.

--- SENTENCE 153 ---
Sighting frequencies and density indices were calculated for each species.

--- SENTENCE 154 ---
Sighting frequency (%SF) is a measure of how often the species was observed.

--- SENTENCE 155 ---
It indicates the percentage of times out of all surveys that the species was recorded.

--- SENTENCE 156 ---
The %SF parameter is calculated as:

 S + F + M + A (for each species)

--- SENTENCE 157 ---

--- SENTENCE 158 ---
(Number of surveys)

The density index (Den) is a measure of how many individuals of a species were observed based on a scale of 1-4.

--- SENTENCE 159 ---
It is representative of the abundance category (1-4) which was most frequently recorded for the species when it was observed.

--- SENTENCE 160 ---
Abundance category weights are **S**ingle=1, **F**ew=2, **M**any=3, and **A**bundant=4.

--- SENTENCE 161 ---
This weighted density average is calculated as:

--- SENTENCE 163 ---
This number indicates which abundance category the species was most often recorded in when it was recorded.

--- SENTENCE 164 ---
For example, Den=2.2 would be reflective of a species that was most often recorded in category 2 (Few) but because the density index is greater than 2, there were some abundances recorded for this species in the other, larger abundance categories (either category 3 or 4).

--- SENTENCE 165 ---
The density index should be used as an abundance guide because area is not rigorously controlled in the RDT method.

--- SENTENCE 166 ---
It should also be kept in mind that the density (Den) parameter is reflective of sighting distributions in the four different abundance categories (S, F, M, and A) and different distributions of sightings in each abundance category could potentially give similar values of Den.

--- SENTENCE 167 ---
In other words, it does not account for non-sightings.

--- SENTENCE 168 ---
By simultaneously examining the sighting frequency (%SF) and density index (Den), data summaries can be interpreted for fish species.

--- SENTENCE 169 ---
The Den and %SF scores were multiplied to provide a measure of species abundance which includes zero observations.

--- SENTENCE 170 ---
Fish biodiversity was also sampled during the core sampling regime, but within a shorter time frame at each dive site.

--- SENTENCE 171 ---
The total observation time for biodiversity during the supplemental monitoring was 60 minutes.

--- SENTENCE 172 ---
Two dives at the same site during the core sampling regime were conducted, but biodiversity data were recorded in between Stationary Point Counts (SPCs), resulting in a total observation time for biodiversity of approximately 40 minutes.

--- SENTENCE 173 ---
Additionally, the method of biodiversity counts between the core and supplemental monitoring regimes differed in that the diver was focused for the entire dive on identifying and enumerating all fish species during the supplemental monitoring regime as opposed to only recording biodiversity data in between the Stationary Point Counts during the core sampling regime.

--- SENTENCE 174 ---
The Roving Diver Technique has been used frequently in monitoring fish biodiversity (Schmidt et al 2002; Hill & Wilkinson, 2004).

--- SENTENCE 175 ---
It compliments transect data, since it covers a larger area and thus more species are found, plus depths which are not included in the transects surveyed, and zonation is often such that species not found in transects are found at other depths.

--- SENTENCE 176 ---
However, transect data produces more accurate quantitative data.

--- SENTENCE 177 ---
The Roving Diver Technique is commonly used in Rapid Assessment Programs for corals and selected invertebrate groups as well as fish (e.g., Allen et al 2003).

--- SENTENCE 178 ---
Although areas covered are not measured, if the duration of the dive is standardized, quantitative comparisons of diversity are possible (Fenner, 2005b).

#### *Benthic Cover*

The benthic substrate of reef slopes in American Samoa is typically dominated by crustose coralline algae and hard corals.

--- SENTENCE 194 ---
Thus, an average of 35.2% of the substrate at the 11 ASCRMP sites was covered with crustose coralline algae, and 28.2% by living hard corals.

--- SENTENCE 195 ---
The 28% live coral cover is very close to the 27% live coral cover found by Sabater and Tofaeono 2006, for 24 sites around Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 196 ---
Their 24 sites were completely different from the present sites.

--- SENTENCE 197 ---
Aeby and Work (2005) in their coral disease study, also recorded benthic cover at 7 sites, and found 35% live coral cover.

--- SENTENCE 198 ---
Five of their sites were in the same general bays as five of our sites, but not at the same exact location, and for those five sites we found 32% cover and they found 39% cover.

--- SENTENCE 199 ---
The 28% living hard coral cover found in this study is slightly higher than the average (25%) for reef slopes in six other countries in the South Pacific (SPC, 2005), and very close to the estimated global average of 32% reported by Wilkinson (2002).

--- SENTENCE 200 ---
Dead coral was rarely recorded, with an average live coral index of 96.7%.

--- SENTENCE 201 ---
(Live coral index was calculated from the ratio of live coral cover to total coral cover, including dead coral.)

--- SENTENCE 202 ---
Macroalgae also had very low cover on most reefs in the 2005 ASCRMP, averaging 2% cover.

--- SENTENCE 203 ---
Most of the macroalgae is *Halimeda*, a green calcarious algae, and virtually none of it is brown macroalgae such as *Sargassum*, *Turbinaria*, or *Lobophyllia*, which are species that often dominate dead surfaces in the absence of herbivores and/or presence of elevated nutrients, such as in Fiji.

--- SENTENCE 204 ---
The abundance of crustose coralline algae and living hard corals along with the rarity of macroalgae indicates a healthy benthic coral reef community for the ASCRMP sites.

--- SENTENCE 205 ---
The 28% live coral cover found in the present study is well above the levels reported in studies done in 1995 and 1996, reporting 4% at 6 m depth and 12% at 3 m depth in 1995, and 16% in 1996.

--- SENTENCE 206 ---
This is consistent with the view that there has been significant recovery from hurricanes

and bleaching since then.

--- SENTENCE 207 ---
However, the 28% is well below the levels of 46% reported for 2002 and 40% for 2004 (summarized in Craig et al 2005).

--- SENTENCE 208 ---
It appears unlikely that there has been a significant decrease since 2004, so those figures will need rechecking.

--- SENTENCE 209 ---
It is possible that since they were different sites the differences reflect site differences not changes over time, or it may be that different methods of counting were used.

--- SENTENCE 210 ---
Two other major components of the algal community in American Samoa in addition to macroalgae and crustose coralline algae are filamentous algae and branching coralline algae.

--- SENTENCE 211 ---
Cover for branching coralline algae averaged 4.2%, ranging from 0% cover to 16% cover.

--- SENTENCE 212 ---
The branching coralline algae probably have ecological dynamics similar to crustose coralline algae, and thus may be a good sign.

--- SENTENCE 213 ---
Filamentous algae averaged 16.4% cover, ranging from 0% cover to 45% cover.

--- SENTENCE 214 ---
The filamentous algae usually grow on relatively newly opened substrate.

--- SENTENCE 215 ---
Filamentous algae have a very small biomass, but very high growth rate.

--- SENTENCE 216 ---
It is typically cropped very rapidly, primarily by herbivorous fish, which can bite the substrate up to 10's of thousands of bites per square meter per day (Hixon and Brostoff, 1996).

--- SENTENCE 217 ---
Thus, the filamentous algae are a major primary producer, and base of a food chain which in American Samoa appears to primarily consist of herbivorous fish and the carnivorous fish that feed on them (some places sea urchins are a major herbivore).

--- SENTENCE 218 ---
The presence of a significant area of filamentous algae, with very little macroalgae, is consistent with a fish community that has large numbers of herbivorous fish, such as surgeonfish (Acanthurids) and parrotfish (Scaridae).

--- SENTENCE 219 ---
Herbivores are a critical component of the coral reef ecosystem, since they must be sufficiently abundant to keep filamentous and macroalgae in check, and allow coralline algae and hard corals to compete and dominate the substrate.

--- SENTENCE 220 ---
Soft corals averaged just 0.8% bottom cover, and are thus a minor component of the bottom cover on reef slopes.

--- SENTENCE 221 ---
The most common genus by far is *Cladiella*, but a total of 10 genera have been found by D. Fenner on Tutuila so far.

--- SENTENCE 222 ---
In addition, two genera of black coral, three genera of zoanthids, and three genera of coralimorphs have been found by D. Fenner.

--- SENTENCE 223 ---
*sociatus* is common in reef flat pools, and *Rhodactis* sp. is common at Nuuuli pool and Tafeu.

--- SENTENCE 224 ---
The other species are all uncommon to rare.

Rugosity values did not vary much from site to site.

--- SENTENCE 318 ---
Most of the reef relief appeared to consist of fairly large reef features, such as holes or ledges in the reef, instead of the growth of corals.

--- SENTENCE 319 ---

*Coral Bleaching*

American Samoa's coral reefs have been impacted by a variety of major events, including periodic severe hurricanes, the most recent of which was Hurricane Heta, which was closest on Jan 6, 2004.

--- SENTENCE 323 ---
Other events have included a Crown-of-Thorns starfish outbreak in 1974 and night scuba spearfishing from 1994-2002.

--- SENTENCE 324 ---
Mass coral bleaching occurred in 1994 (Goreau and Hayes, 1994), and then again in the summers of 2002 (Green, 2002) and 2003

(Craig, personal comm., Mielbrecht, personal comm.), and the bleaching was severe enough to kill some corals.

--- SENTENCE 325 ---
A striking feature of the lagoon pools of Tutuila is the dominance of the coral populations by two groups (finger corals, *Porites cylindrica*, and staghorns (*Acropora*), 3 species).

--- SENTENCE 326 ---
A further striking feature is that a large proportion of the staghorn corals are dead, but virtually nothing else is dead.

--- SENTENCE 327 ---
The dead staghorn coral thickets are mostly still standing, but support a heavy growth of algae, primarily filamentous algae and coralline algae.

--- SENTENCE 328 ---
This indicates that the staghorns did not die within the last few months, but probably not more than a few years ago (as they would have collapsed).

--- SENTENCE 329 ---
Thus it appeared possible that the staghorns might have been killed by mass bleaching in 2002 and/or 2003.

--- SENTENCE 330 ---
The fact that only staghorns were dead suggested that the cause of death was species-specific.

--- SENTENCE 331 ---
Previous reports from elsewhere in the world indicate that corals in the genus *Acropora* are often more sensitive to bleaching than other corals.

--- SENTENCE 332 ---
Craig et al (2001) have reported that lagoon pools in Ofu reach high temperatures for short periods of time on sunny days at low tide when waves cannot make it over the crest to pump water through the lagoon pools.

--- SENTENCE 333 ---
All of these things together suggested that the staghorns in Tutuila lagoon pools may have been killed by previous mass bleaching events, and may be more sensitive to high temperatures (the primary factor causing mass coral bleaching).

--- SENTENCE 334 ---
Monitoring of bleaching in backreef pools of Tutuila began in December, 2003, and now 2 years of data have been collected.

--- SENTENCE 335 ---
Staghorns in three pools, Airport, Alofau and Nuuuuli have bleached every summer for the past three summers.

--- SENTENCE 336 ---
Monitoring began first in the Airport pool, and subsequently added to the Alofau pool.

--- SENTENCE 337 ---
Alofau and Nuuuuli appear to bleach most easily, Airport next most easily, Faguitua next, and Fagaalu, Utelei, and Onesosopo least.

--- SENTENCE 338 ---
Alofau and Nuuuli have very little circulation through the pools.

--- SENTENCE 339 ---
Utelei and Onesosopo have a bit more circulation, Fagaalu more, and Fagaitua has the most.

--- SENTENCE 340 ---
Water temperatures were recorded on temperature loggers in some of the lagoons.

--- SENTENCE 341 ---
By far the highest temperatures were recorded in Fagaitua, with a peak of 34.9 C recorded in 2005, at a point at which the staghorns there were only mildly bleached.

--- SENTENCE 342 ---
The graph below was taken by a Hobo temperature logger placed in about 1.5 m water depth among *Acropora muricata* staghorn colonies.

--- SENTENCE 343 ---
Other backreef pools on Tutuila reach maximum temperatures around 32 C or less.

--- SENTENCE 344 ---
In the same time period, satellite SST (Sea Surface Temperature) was about 30 C.

!

--- SENTENCE 345 ---
Strong water motion has been reported to reduce bleaching, so perhaps the strong water currents there make it possible for them to survive high temperatures.

--- SENTENCE 346 ---
Depths are also less there, and the pool may be natural, while in the other pools the water is deeper and the pools were made several decades ago by using material for landfill.

(=*formosa*) seems to be the most sensitive of the three species, and the other two appear to be approximately equal in sensitivity.

--- SENTENCE 366 ---
Bleaching very likely slows or stops growth, and has been reported to block sexual reproduction for a year.

--- SENTENCE 367 ---
Thus, these corals are likely growing less and not reproducing at all (other than asexually by fragmentation).

--- SENTENCE 368 ---
Bleaching is very likely to having a chronic negative impact on these coral populations.

--- SENTENCE 369 ---
Mass coral bleaching has been predicted to become an annual summer event in coming decades, and these staghorns appear to be the first multi-species population in the world exhibiting annual summer bleaching (*Oculina patigonica* in Israel and *Favia fragum* in Florida exhibit annual summer and fall bleaching, respectively, but only of the one species at a location).

#### **Low Tide Event**

 Starting in December, 2004, and continuing through April, 2005, there were a series of unusually low tides.

--- SENTENCE 371 ---
The extra low tides were in one week each month.

--- SENTENCE 372 ---
These tides were so low as to expose living corals on reef flats to the air for several hours each day.

--- SENTENCE 373 ---
When exposure occurred in the middle of the day on a sunny day, exposed corals and coralline algae died.

--- SENTENCE 374 ---
Each month the tides went a little lower and killed coral a little farther down.

--- SENTENCE 375 ---
The principle coral killed was a staghorn, *Acropora aspera*, which lives in some large patches on some of the reef flats.

--- SENTENCE 376 ---
In some areas nearly all of this coral was killed, while in other areas the bases may still be alive.

--- SENTENCE 377 ---
It appears that a majority of this species was killed.

--- SENTENCE 378 ---
Quantitative measurements have not yet been made.

--- SENTENCE 379 ---
It is hoped that a monitoring program for the reef flats can be designed and tested in the coming year, and may even be able to measure the amount of dead coral from this event.

--- SENTENCE 380 ---
It is clear that factors affecting the reef flats may often be totally different from those affecting the reef slopes, so it will be necessary to monitor both since the state of the reef flats cannot be predicted based on data from reef slopes.

--- SENTENCE 381 ---
In any case, the recent low tide events appear to be a totally natural and unavoidable trimming of the tallest corals, much like mowing a lawn.

--- SENTENCE 382 ---
The last such event is anecdotally reported to have been in March, 1998 (N. Dashbach, personal comm.).

#### *Coral Disease*

The two most common types of disease identified in American Samoa are tumors or neoplasms and White Syndrome.

--- SENTENCE 384 ---
Tumors do not often die, and even when they do, they do not lead to colony death.

--- SENTENCE 385 ---
However they do use colony energy, and thus less energy is available for other colony functions such as growth and reproduction.

--- SENTENCE 386 ---
In White Syndrome, a white band of newly dead coral moves across a coral (often a table coral), killing the coral (AIMS website).

--- SENTENCE 387 ---
White Syndrome probably results in colony death in most cases, within a fairly short period of time.

--- SENTENCE 388 ---
This is a potentially serious threat to the coral reefs of the entire Indo-Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 389 ---
There are also a

<span id="page-27-0"></span>considerable number of dead or partially dead *Pocillopora* colonies, and some show a thin white band, which may represent another disease.

--- SENTENCE 390 ---
Although coral diseases are common enough for there to be cause for concern, at this point they are only killing a minority of corals.

--- SENTENCE 391 ---
American Samoa does not appear to be in a major outbreak.

--- SENTENCE 392 ---
Continued monitoring is important.

--- SENTENCE 393 ---
There are also two coralline algae diseases in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 394 ---
Coralline Lethal Orange Disease (CLOD) is present and easy to recognize, but is rare at most sites.

--- SENTENCE 395 ---
It is a bacterium.

--- SENTENCE 396 ---
Even at the sites such as Fagatele Bay where CLOD is most common, this disease causes mortality in only a very small fraction of the coralline algae, and the algae appear to be able to regrow as fast as the disease can kill it.

--- SENTENCE 397 ---
At this point it is not a threat.

--- SENTENCE 398 ---
There is a second, black disease that is harder to recognize, and appears to be less common than CLOD.

--- SENTENCE 399 ---
It is a fungus.

--- SENTENCE 400 ---
For more information on diseases, see the series of reports by G. Aeby and T. Work.

#### *Coral Species Richness*

A total of 69 species of corals were found in all transects put together.

--- SENTENCE 401 ---
The coral species richness on one-hour roving biodiversity search dives averaged 71 species, with a range of 57- 87 species.

--- SENTENCE 402 ---
A total of 152 species of corals were found in all biodiversity dives together.

--- SENTENCE 403 ---
D. Fenner has now recorded a total of over 220 species of corals in American Samoa, and continues to find more.

--- SENTENCE 404 ---
Veron (2000) is the most authoritative current source for coral biodiversity data, indicating that American Samoa has 100-200 species of coral.

--- SENTENCE 405 ---
However, the 2005 ASCRMP data as well as the total number of corals observed by D. Fenner outside of the ASCRMP, indicate that American Samoa has higher coral diversity than Veron (2000) indicates.

--- SENTENCE 406 ---
Three different methods of estimation of the total number of coral species in American Samoa all indicate that there are probably over 400 species total that will eventually be found.

--- SENTENCE 407 ---
The first method of estimating the total is to look at the species which have a range that includes American Samoa (Veron, 2000).

--- SENTENCE 408 ---
Of those species, about half have been found in American Samoa so far by D. Fenner.

--- SENTENCE 409 ---
This suggests that the total eventually to be found will be about double that so far found by D. Fenner, for a total of about 440 species.

--- SENTENCE 410 ---
The second estimation depends on the observation from Veron (2000) that the ranges for coral species can extend both west and east, or they can extend westward only, but they never extend eastward only.

--- SENTENCE 411 ---
As a result, corals found to the east of American Samoa are likely to also be in American Samoa, though American Samoa likely has additional species.

--- SENTENCE 412 ---
The corals of Hawaii are relatively well studied (Fenner, 2005a) and most species there are likely to have been found.

--- SENTENCE 413 ---
If endemic species are excluded, about half of the Hawaiian species have been found so far in American Samoa by D. Fenner.

--- SENTENCE 414 ---
Thus, this also indicates that the total to eventually be found may be around 440 species.

--- SENTENCE 415 ---
Lastly, the fish of American Samoa are well studied and few additional are likely to be found.

--- SENTENCE 416 ---
There are about 850 reef fish known from American Samoa from the roughly 900 total species known.

--- SENTENCE 417 ---
The average biodiversity dive produced 71 species of corals and 123 fish species.

--- SENTENCE 418 ---
Using that ratio, the total number of corals expected to be eventually found is about 490.

--- SENTENCE 419 ---
In all biodiversity dives together, 152 coral species were found, and 270 fish species.

--- SENTENCE 420 ---
Using that ratio produces a prediction of 478 coral species.

--- SENTENCE 421 ---
Species accumulate with dives in a log fashion, such that doubling the number of dives adds a fixed number of additional coral species.

--- SENTENCE 422 ---
Rough calculations indicate that it will likely take over 2000 dives to find a total of about 440 coral species.

#### *Water Quality*

Water clarity was measured with a secci disc and/or by sighting along the transect tape.

--- SENTENCE 557 ---
An average was taken of these measurements, using the sighting along the tape when available and the secci reading when not, because the secci reading often had to be out from the reef in deeper water.

--- SENTENCE 558 ---
The visibility readings averaged 27.4 m, which is consistent with observations that our water is clear, with oceanic oligotrophic conditions.

--- SENTENCE 559 ---
## **Recommendations for 2006 Monitoring**

EPA and Maloy Sabater have advised that in order to get meaningful water quality data, it is necessary to sample frequently, since water quality can change rapidly.

--- SENTENCE 560 ---
Since this is not a central part of the program and since others such as EPA and Sabater have water quality programs, it may not be cost-efficient to pursue water quality sampling in the monitoring program, but rather to concentrate on biological variables and concentrating to expanding monitoring to the Manu'a islands, reef flats, and a study of monthly variations in fish populations.

--- SENTENCE 561 ---
The program should encourage EPA and Sabater to continue with the efforts to develop a water quality sampling program for the coral reef slopes in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 562 ---
Sketch maps of the monitoring sites would be a helpful addition to the Monitoring Tools Handbook, especially since many of the transects begin at notable landmarks such as 'ava (channels in the reef) or large, notable coral heads.

#### *Bottom cover*

A small amount of underestimation of dead coral cover may have occurred and thus the live coral index may have been overestimated by a small amount.

--- SENTENCE 594 ---
The problem is that dead corals quickly grow a cover of algae, filamentous and/or crustose coralline algae.

--- SENTENCE 595 ---
It is possible that sometimes the algae cover was recorded instead of the dead coral under it.

--- SENTENCE 596 ---
This was definitely the case for the rubble bed in Faga'alu, where algae were recorded instead of rubble (the rubble was formed from the collapse of dead branching corals).

--- SENTENCE 597 ---
In addition, the tape is commonly secured by passing it between coral branches or around the base of corals, and a clear policy of <span id="page-40-0"></span>recording dead corals that were over top the tape was not formulated.

--- SENTENCE 598 ---
Corrections to improve accuracy for counting dead coral should be implemented next year.

--- SENTENCE 599 ---
The coral biodiversity data collected in 2005 ASCRMP was entirely dependent on observation *in situ*, supplemented by *in situ* photography.

--- SENTENCE 600 ---
When time allows, the coral identifications from the 2005 ASCRMP should be verified with the DMWR coral collection and the collections of American Samoa corals in museums such as the Bishop Museum in Honolulu, University of Guam Marine Lab (Richard Randall collection), Smithsonian (Hoffmeister collection?) and University of California, Berkeley (Austin Lamberts collection).

--- SENTENCE 601 ---
Furthermore, samples should be collected to verify species not found in those collections.

--- SENTENCE 602 ---
One future goal should be a technical article, detailing all of the coral species found in American Samoa, supported by skeletal samples.

--- SENTENCE 603 ---

## *Rugosity*

Rugosity does not appear to be providing much useful information, and will very likely not change unless a major destructive event occurs.

--- SENTENCE 608 ---
Taking rugosity data along with invertebrate data takes so much time for a 2-person team that it makes dives so long that they are not safe.

--- SENTENCE 609 ---
Rugosity should not be repeated annually if only a 2-person team is available, but may be repeated if a major destructive event changes the appearance of reef rugosity.

# **Management Recommendations**

During the 2005 data collection for the ASCRMP, the largest fish recorded was a 110cm *Cheilinus undulatus*

--- SENTENCE 611 ---
(Humphead Wrasse, Napoleon Wrasse, or Maori Wrasse).

--- SENTENCE 612 ---
The maximum size of this species is 229cm, clearly showing that the individuals seen in American Samoa during the monitoring are not full-size adults.

--- SENTENCE 613 ---
Therefore, a recommendation for managers, based upon 2005 ASCRMP, is to conduct further studies on the population of *C. undulatus*, and if it should prove to be overfished, ban all fishing for *C. undulatus* within the Territory's waters.

--- SENTENCE 614 ---
No *Bolbometapon muricatum* (Bumphead Parrotfish) or Grey Reef Sharks (*Carcharhinus amblyrhynchos*) at all were seen in the 2005 ASCRMP fieldwork, nor in dives outside of the fieldwork by any team members or anyone else in DMWR during the calendar year or the previous year.

--- SENTENCE 615 ---
These species are very rare in all studies yet conducted here, and no one even claims *B. muricatum* is anything but rare.

--- SENTENCE 616 ---
The likely closing of the canneries in the near future is likely to increase fishing pressure due to loss of income.

--- SENTENCE 617 ---
*B. muricatum* and *C. amblyrhynchos* appear to be rarer than *C. undulatus*, and *B. muricatum* should receive the highest priority for gathering further information if necessary and banning all fishing.

--- SENTENCE 804 ---
A relative of hard corals, corallimorphs have large fleshy polyps but no skeleton.

--- SENTENCE 805 ---
Corallimorphs form large clusters or patches, completely covering the patch area.

--- SENTENCE 806 ---
They may be a pioneering species which once started on open substrate, can quickly cover large areas of open substrate.

--- SENTENCE 807 ---
This would correspond with the large area covered by filamentous algae, which also rapidly colonizes newly opened substrate.

--- SENTENCE 808 ---
Therefore, Tafeu may have had an event in the past that opened substrate by removing living organisms.

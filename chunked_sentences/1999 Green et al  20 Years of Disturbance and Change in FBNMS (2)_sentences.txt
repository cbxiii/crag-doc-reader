# **Twenty Years of Disturbance and Change in Fagatele Bay National Marine Sanctuary, American Samoa <sup>1</sup>**

A. L. GREEN, <sup>2</sup> C. E. BIRKELAND, <sup>3</sup> AND R. H. RANDALL<sup>3</sup>

ABSTRACT:

--- SENTENCE 2 ---
Fagatele Bay National Marine Sanctuary contains a moderately diverse coral reef community (150 coral species, 259 fish species) that is protected from most human activities.

--- SENTENCE 3 ---
The coral community was devastated by a crown-of-thorns starfish invasion in 1979 and has recently been affected by two major hurricanes (1990 and 1991) and a period of unusually high water temperature (1994).

--- SENTENCE 4 ---
Long-term monitoring of the sanctuary allows for description of the effects of these disturbances in the absence of anthropogenic processes.

--- SENTENCE 5 ---
The crown-of-thorns damaged deeper portions of the coral communities most severely, whereas the hurricanes and warm water affected shallower portions to a greater degree.

--- SENTENCE 6 ---
Soon after these disturbances, corals started recruiting abundantly and the reefs began to recover.

--- SENTENCE 7 ---
This is in contrast to some other areas in American Samoa, where chronic anthropogenic effects seem to have inhibited coral recruitment and reefrecovery.

--- SENTENCE 8 ---
Fish communities were affected by the habitat degradation associated with the crown-of-thorns outbreak, but have remained relatively unchanged ever since.

--- SENTENCE 9 ---
CORAL REEFS ARE COMPLEX tropical ecosystems subject to perturbations from a variety of sources, including natural disasters (e.g., hurricanes) and events of undetermined cause (e.g., crown-of-thorns starfish outbreaks and coral bleaching).

--- SENTENCE 10 ---
The effects of these disturbances on coral reef communities can range from minor to severe, depending on a variety of factors such as the previous condition of the community, the timing and intensity of the disturbance, and the physiography of the site (see review in Brown 1997).

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
However, it is often difficult to examine the effects of these disturbances on reefs, because of the confounding influence of anthropogenic processes (e.g., sedimentation, eutrophication, and chemical pollution).

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
Long-term monitoring of marine reserves can provide a valuable insight into the dynamics of coral

reef ecosystems in the absence of anthropogenic affects (Buddemeier 1992).

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## *Study Area*

Fagatele Bay National Marine Sanctuary is located on the southwestern side of Tutuila Island (Figure 1).

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
The bay is a small pocket of coastline (area = 0.65 km2) formed by the crater of an extinct volcano with its southwestern side open to the sea.

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
The overall bottom topography resembles the shape of the original crater.

--- SENTENCE 34 ---
However, shallower regions have been extensively modified by reef deposits, especially along the northeastern sector of the bay (Figure 1).

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
Tidal range is approximately 1 m.

The reefs in the sanctuary can be divided into two principal geomorphic regions: a reef flat platform and a forereef slope (Figure 1).

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
On the inside portion of the bay, a shallow reef flat with a depth of <

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
I m extends outward from the shoreline to the reef margin.

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
At this point, the reef slope drops gradually down to >40 m in depth.

--- SENTENCE 39 ---
In contrast, there is no reef flat platform on the outside portion of the bay, where the forereef slope drops directly from the shoreline to a depth of 6-7 m and then drops steeply down to the bottom of the bay.

--- SENTENCE 40 ---
A detailed description of the geology and physiography of Fagatele Bay can be found in Birkeland et al. (1987).

## *Survey Design*

Permanent sites were established at each of six locations around the bay in 1985 (SI-6: Figure 1).

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
At each site, a permanent line was marked from the reef margin to a depth of 12 m (Figure 1) using two or three large metal spikes driven into the substrate.

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
The exact location and general description of the reef along each line is described in detail in Birkeland et al. (1987).

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
Corals and fishes were then surveyed quantitatively using a stratified random sampling program.

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
Replicate transects (length = 30 m) were situated along four isobaths on the reef slope (3, 6, 9, and 12 m) perpendicular to the permanent lines and rougWy parallel to shore.

--- SENTENCE 46 ---
Transects were also situated on the reef flat platform at three sites only (S2-4).

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
Surveys were not done on the shallow reef slope (3 m) at Sl and S6 because reefs are only present in deeper water (::::6 m) at those sites.

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
The coral and fish communities in Fagatele Bay were described based on surveys done on three occasions over a period of 10 yr: the baseline survey in 1985 and two subsequent surveys in 1988 and 1995.

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
The number of depth isobaths censused along each line varied for corals and fishes and among surveys, because of time and weather constraints (Table 1).

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
Corals were surveyed along approximately twice as many transects as fishes in the baseline survey.

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
With few exceptions, all transects censused in the baseline coral study were resurveyed in 1988 and 1995.

## Coral Survey Methods

Hard coral communities in Fagatele Bay were censused in all three surveys by the same two observers (C.E.B. and R.H.R.) using the point-quarter method described in detail by Birkeland et al. (1987).

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
Points were selected by haphazardly throwing a hammer at 16 locations along each transect.

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
Four corals were then sampled at each point, yielding a total of 64 corals sampled per transect.

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
Corals were selected by choosing the closest colony to the point in each of four imaginary quadrants radiating out from the head of the hammer.

--- SENTENCE 61 ---
Each coral was identified, its size measured (diameter in centimeters), and the distance from the sample point to the center of the colony recorded.

--- SENTENCE 62 ---
The dimensions of the colony were also measured (usually length and width) and were later converted to estimates of cover using the appropriate areal formulas for each colony shape.

--- SENTENCE 63 ---
These measurements were then used to calculate the following: coral density =  $(unit area)/(mean distance)^2$ and coral cover = (average areal cover of colonies) × (coral density).

--- SENTENCE 64 ---
Two indices of community structure were calculated also: species richness = total number of species per transect and Shannon-Wiener Diversity Index:  $H' = -\sum p_i \log_{10} p_i$ , where  $p_i$  is the proportion of corals in category i.

Coral species observed but not included in the quantitative assessment were also recorded to obtain a more complete species list for the sanctuary.

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
## Fish Survey Methods

Fishes were censused along the transects by an experienced observer in each survey: R. Wass in 1985, S. Amesbury in 1988, and A.L.G. in 1995.

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
Fish survey methods were the same in all three surveys with one exception: fishes were surveyed while other divers were in the water in 1988, but this was not the case in the other two surveys.

--- SENTENCE 67 ---
All fishes observed within 1 m on either side of the transect and 2 m above it were identified to species and counted.

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
Holes and cracks in the reef within the transect corridor were inspected for nocturnal and secretive fishes, and the substrate was examined closely for cryptic species.

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
The duration of the count for each transect was approximately 10 min. Species that were observed in the bay but not on the transects were also recorded to provide a more complete fish list for the sanctuary.

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
All fish observations were made between 0900 and 1500 hours.

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
Quantitative fish counts were used to calculate fish density (per 10 m<sup>2</sup>) and species richness (number of species) on each transect.

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
Fish diversity was also calculated using the Shannon-Wiener Diversity Index (see Coral Survey Methods).

Reef slopes have been consolidated with a lush growth of pink coralline algae (Birkeland et al. 1996) and coral recruitment is high, as evidenced by the high density and species richness of small coral colonies observed in 1995 (Table 2).

--- SENTENCE 176 ---
Coral communities provide important habitat for coral reef fishes, and previous studies suggest that there were some major changes in the fish communities of Fagatele Bay as a result of the habitat degradation caused by the starfish invasion in 1979 (Birkeland et al. 1987, 1994, 1996).

--- SENTENCE 177 ---
In particular, there was a dramatic decline in small, siteattached species that are closely associated with live coral colonies (such as the damselfish *Plectroglyphidodon dickii)* and an increase in species that prefer coral rubble (Birkeland et al. 1996).

--- SENTENCE 178 ---
In contrast, there does not appear to have been any major changes in the fish assemblages in the bay over the last 10 yr (this study).

--- SENTENCE 179 ---
Fish species richness was similar in 1985 and 1995, but was much lower in 1988 (Figure 8).

--- SENTENCE 180 ---
However, this was probably due to methodological differences between surveys (see Materials and Methods) and not to an actual decline in species richness that year.

--- SENTENCE 181 ---
In particular, the presence of other divers in the water may have caused a greater disturbance to the fish community in 1988 (especially mobile families such as scarids, labrids, and acanthurids), resulting in the lower number of species observed at that time.

--- SENTENCE 182 ---
In contrast to species richness, fish abundance appears to have decreased over time, with a much higher density recorded in 1985 than in the last two surveys (Figure 8).

--- SENTENCE 183 ---
The lowest abundances were consistently recorded in 1988, which was probably due to the methodological differences between surveys (see above).

--- SENTENCE 184 ---
However, the much higher abundances in 1985 relative to 1995 appear to be a temporary artifact caused by a recent, massive recruitment pulse of *Ctenochaetus striatus* at the time of the first survey (see Results).

--- SENTENCE 185 ---
Observations at the time indicate that many of these juveniles were in poor condition, with sunken sides and frayed fins, and it is likely that there was a wholesale dieoff shortly after the survey was completed (Birkeland et al. 1987).

--- SENTENCE 186 ---
In summary, Fagatele Bay National Marine Sanctuary encompasses a healthy coral reef community, which has undergone some substantial changes over the last two decades as the result of four major disturbances.

--- SENTENCE 187 ---
Coral reefs can show considerable robustness when exposed to acute disturbances such as crown-of-thorns outbreaks, hurricanes, and extraordinarily warm water (Wilkinson 1992), and it appears that the reefs in the sanctuary are now recovering from these perturbations.

--- SENTENCE 188 ---
In the absence of any major disturbances in the next few years, we anticipate that the coral reef communities in the bay will continue to make a rapid recovery.

--- SENTENCE 189 ---
This is in marked contrast to some other reefs on the island, such as in Pago Pago Harbor, that have been subjected to chronic inhibition of coral recruitment from anthropogenic influences, where recovery from disturbances is slow (Birkeland et al. 1994, 1996, Green et al. 1997).

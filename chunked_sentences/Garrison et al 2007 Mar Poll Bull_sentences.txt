## Identifying nutrient sources to three lagoons at Ofu and Olosega, American Samoa using d15N of benthic macroalgae

Virginia Garrison a,\*, Kevin Kroeger <sup>b</sup> , Douglas Fenner <sup>c</sup> , Peter Craig <sup>d</sup>

<sup>a</sup> US Geological Survey, 600 Fourth Street South, St. Petersburg, FL 33701, USA <sup>b</sup> US Geological Survey, Woods Hole Science Center, 384 Woods Hole Road, Woods Hole, MA 02543-1598, USA <sup>c</sup> Department of Marine and Wildlife Resources, Pago Pago 96799, American Samoa <sup>d</sup>

National Park of American Samoa, Pago Pago 96799, American Samoa

Degradation of nearshore habitats is a serious problem in some areas of American Samoa, such as in Pago Pago Harbor on Tutuila Island, and is a smaller but chronic problem in other areas.

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
Sedimentation, pollution, nutrient enrichment from surface runoff or groundwater, and trampling are the major factors causing the changes (Peshut and Brooks, 2005).

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
On the outer islands of Ofu and Olosega (Manu'a Islands; Fig. 1), there is an interesting contrast between relatively pristine lagoon habitats not far from comparatively degraded lagoon habitats.

--- SENTENCE 22 ---
To'aga lagoon on the southeast side of Ofu Island (Fig. 1) has clear waters, a high diversity of corals and fishes, no human habitations, and an undeveloped watershed with no streams.

--- SENTENCE 23 ---
To'aga lagoon is within the boundaries of the National Park of American Samoa and is the site of long-term research on coral reef resilience and global climate change.

--- SENTENCE 24 ---
Only 3 km to the east of To'aga is a degraded lagoon that fronts Olosega Village.

--- SENTENCE 25 ---
The Olosega lagoon is similar in size but has significantly less live coral than To'aga, and blooms of filamentous algae have been reported to cover the Olosega lagoon/reef flat bottom (unpublished data, PC; Fig. 2).

--- SENTENCE 26 ---
The islands are influenced by the same regional-scale and biogeochemical regimes, and both islands are remnants of a volcanic caldera (Craig, 2005).

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
Thus, local factors operating on the scale of a kilometer or less are thought to be driving the differences observed between lagoons.

--- SENTENCE 28 ---
Land disturbance is limited to a road linking the villages, the clearing of vegetation for buildings, and two village dump sites located on the narrow strip of land between the steep slopes of the islands and the shoreline; there is no industry or associated pollution on either island.

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
Cesspools are used for sewage disposal.

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
Nutrient enrichment (from cesspools) of groundwater and the lagoon, as well as trampling during gleaning of reef organisms, are possible factors affecting the spatial relief and benthic composition of the lagoons.

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
A pristine lagoon site (To'aga) and two that may be influenced by adjacent human populations (Ofu and Olosega Villages) were selected for study.

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
Analysis of lagoon waters for nutrient content can fail to detect episodic nutrient influx because of the rapid removal and recycling of nutrients from seawater by plankton and macroalgae (e.g., review by Atkinson, 1988).

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
Unlike some marine organisms and plants, macroalgae are known to incorporate nutrients directly from the water column and store excess nutrients in their tissues, providing an integrated record of nutrient influx (Fong et al., 1994).

--- SENTENCE 34 ---

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
Biological processes usually result in fractionation of the isotopes of elements (such as nitro-

gen and carbon) incorporated by organisms and plants (Minagawa and Wada, 1984).

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
The ratio of <sup>15</sup>N to <sup>14</sup>N {more specifically the  $\delta^{15}N$  (%) = [( $^{15}N$ / $^{14}N$  sample)/  $(^{15}\text{N}/^{14}\text{N} \text{ atmospheric nitrogen}) - 1] \times 1000$ } in tissue has been reported to be effective in distinguishing between sources of nitrogen (e.g., atmospheric deposition, fertilizer, nitrogen fixation, and animals or plants; Peterson and Fry, 1987) and trophic level (the higher the trophic level, the greater the  $\delta^{15}$ N; e.g., Minagawa and Wada, 1984).

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
Atmospheric deposition and fertilizers have relatively low  $\delta^{15}N$ values  $(-12\%_{oo}$  to  $+5\%_{oo}$  and  $-3\%_{oo}$  to  $+3\%_{oo}$ , respectively; Kreitler and Browning, 1983; Russell et al., 1998).

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
$\delta^{15}$ N values of +5 to +22% are reported to indicate that nitrogen from animals may be present, with highest values (+10\% to +22\%; Kreitler and Browning, 1983;

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
Aravena et al., 1993) associated with septic and wastewater systems.

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
Distinguishing between multiple animal and human sources is not as well defined.

--- SENTENCE 176 ---
The nitrogen content (percent total nitrogen, concentration of the heavier nitrogen isotope  $^{15}$ N, and  $\delta^{15}$ N values) of macroalgae varies by species (due to physiology and biochemical processes), light regime, temperature, and availability of nitrogen and <sup>15</sup>N content of the water (e.g., Heikoop et al., 1998; Cohen and Fong, 2006).

--- SENTENCE 177 ---
Values of  $\delta^{15}$ N in this study (Table 3) were close to or lower than those reported for oligotrophic suspended particulate matter (4-5%; Waser et al., 2000), pelagic and coastal marine seaweeds (5-8% in Minagawa and Wada, 1984), and the euphotic layer DIN (7-10%; Minagawa and Wada, 1986).

--- SENTENCE 178 ---
The low  $\delta^{15}$ N values of the macroalgae, the low nutrient concentrations of lagoon waters, the lack of correlation between  $\delta^{15}N$  values and distance from shore, and the low concentrations of nutrients in well-water samples indicate that the major sources of nutrients to the three Ofu-Olosega lagoons at the time of this study were most likely oceanic/atmospheric, and not animal/anthropogenic in origin.

--- SENTENCE 179 ---
In contrast to this study, Umezawa et al. (2002) found an inverse linear or curvilinear decrease in  $\delta^{15}$ N values of macroalgae with distance from shore in the Ryuku Islands, Japan.

--- SENTENCE 180 ---
This pattern was attributed to multiple sources of nitrogen, including terrestrial and human.

--- SENTENCE 181 ---
Cohen and Fong (2006) reported a decrease in  $\delta^{15}N$  in macroalgae with distance from the head of estuaries in California during the dry season, due to a greater  $\delta^{15}N$ content of DIN from the watershed than oceanic sources.

--- SENTENCE 182 ---
Similarly, the  $\delta^{15}$ N in rooted macroalgae (not free-floating) was found to increase significantly with increased influx of sewage-nitrogen to estuaries in the northeastern US (Cole et al., 2005).

--- SENTENCE 183 ---
In the lagoons of Ofu and Olosega Islands, high volumes of oceanic waters and strong currents flush the lagoons daily and would be expected to dilute nutrient influx from land rapidly.

--- SENTENCE 184 ---
Under high-flow regimes, oligotrophic waters are able to provide sufficient nutrients for algal growth, but storage of nutrients in algal tissues is limited due to both low residence time and low nutrient concentrations of the water (Larned and Atkinson, 1997).

--- SENTENCE 185 ---
When high concentrations of nutrients are available, from episodic pulses to sustained high concentrations, some macroalgae store

nutrients in their tissues (Wheeler and North, 1980; Lapointe and Duke, 1984; Fong et al., 1994).

--- SENTENCE 186 ---
However, Fong et al. (2003) found there is not a direct correlation between nutrient concentrations in water and macroalgae tissue, but that nutrient content of tropical marine algae affects the organism's response to N and P influx, be it growth or storage.

--- SENTENCE 187 ---
Thus, the history of available nutrients combined with the biochemistry of the macroalgae species drive the responses observed in the field (Fong et al., 2003).

--- SENTENCE 188 ---
Whereas some macroalgae incorporate nutrients only from the water column, others can access nutrients in sediments, from adjacent heterotrophs (such as the scleractinian corals to which D. versluysii attaches; Larned and Stimson, 1996), or, in the case of some cyanobacteria, from fixation of nitrogen (Howarth et al., 1988).

--- SENTENCE 189 ---
Halimeda and Dictvosphaeria (Division Chlorophyta) are rhizophytic algae and can access nutrients from the water as well as from sediment via holdfasts (Littler and Littler, 1990).

--- SENTENCE 190 ---
Larned and Stimson (1996) found that D. cavernosa, closely related to D. versluysii, has low N storage capacity and needs a continual supply of DIN.

--- SENTENCE 191 ---
The close attachment of D. cavernosa (and presumably D. versluysii) to the substrate creates space between the alga and live coral wherein ammonium concentrations are twofold, and nitrate + nitrite concentrations are 10-fold greater than that of the water column (Larned and Stimson, 1996).

--- SENTENCE 192 ---
The lack of stored nitrogen (%) in Dictyosphaeria was evident in the data from this study (Table 3).

--- SENTENCE 193 ---
The molar C:N ratio in most phytoplankton is typically 106:16 (or 6.6; Redfield et al., 1963) when nutrients are not limiting.

--- SENTENCE 194 ---
In tropical epilithic macroalgae in Atlantic oligotrophic waters that are N limited, the C:N ratio is reported to range from 14 to 28 and to vary by species (Lapointe et al., 1992).

--- SENTENCE 195 ---
The C:N and %N data in this study may indicate that *Dictyosphaeria* and *Halimeda* are more N limited than *Chlorodesmis* at all sites (Table 3).

--- SENTENCE 196 ---
Alternatively, the observed difference between species could be a due to: a higher structural C content in *Dictyosphaeria* and *Halimeda*; and/or, the enhanced ability of *Chlorodesmis* to assimilate nitrogen from the environment or to store nitrogen than *Dictyosphaeria* or *Halimeda*.

--- SENTENCE 197 ---
Ofu and Olosega well-water nutrient levels from this study (Table 5) were within the range of American Samoa EPA values for October 2001, January and December 2003

samples (<0.2–8.7 lM Ofu; <0.2–43 lM Olosega).

--- SENTENCE 198 ---
Other than relatively high phosphate levels in the well-water samples, well-water nutrient levels were within normal groundwater concentrations, at the time of this study.

--- SENTENCE 199 ---
Other possible sources of nutrients to the lagoons include episodic intense rainfall events that could flush nutrients from the upland watersheds and from reservoirs (such as cesspools) in the sandy soil into the lagoons.

--- SENTENCE 200 ---
Hurricanes produce high seas and battering waves that can inundate the coast for tens of meters inshore and wash nutrients from the sandy soil into the lagoons.

--- SENTENCE 201 ---
Nearshore marine communities adjacent to villages with cesspools, pig farms, and other domestic animals would be expected to receive concentrated pulses of nutrients that could be quickly utilized by filamentous algae that can rapidly take up nutrients from the water and grow quickly.

--- SENTENCE 202 ---
If lagoon substrate was exposed by removal of sand or turf algae, as often occurs during storms, an opportunistic filamentous alga could colonize, grow rapidly, and produce a ''bloom''.

--- SENTENCE 203 ---
Hurricanes and other strong storms produce oceanic upwelling, where deep, cooler, nutrient-rich waters are brought to the surface (reviewed in Babin et al., 2004).

--- SENTENCE 204 ---
Storm-induced upwelling in conjunction with tidal pumping and surface runoff from land could provide a considerable source of nutrients to the nearshore waters.

--- SENTENCE 205 ---
All of these mechanisms could fuel algal blooms nearshore.

--- SENTENCE 206 ---
During a hurricane damage survey conducted by the National Park of American Samoa nine days after Hurricane Olaf, a filamentous alga bloom was observed in Olosega lagoon (P. Craig.; Fig.

--- SENTENCE 207 ---
Yet no macroalgae blooms were observed off To'aga or on the north side of Ofu at that time.

--- SENTENCE 208 ---
It is likely that an influx of nutrients flushed from the adjacent village via intense rainfall, tidal pumping, and inundating storm waves stimulated the bloom.

--- SENTENCE 209 ---
However, data from this study, conducted 18 months later, do not support or refute this speculation.

--- SENTENCE 210 ---
To determine the specific drivers for this system, nutrient influx from land and from upwelling, as well as the role of space availability will need to be examined.

--- SENTENCE 211 ---
From this study, Ofu and Olosega lagoons seem to have good water quality, with nitrogen influx of predominantly oceanic/atmospheric origin.

--- SENTENCE 212 ---
At the time of this study, anthropogenic sources of nutrients were not detected in macroalgae in the lagoons.

--- SENTENCE 213 ---
The major factors driving the differences among the three lagoons (live coral cover, macroalgae cover, and number of coral species) remain unproven.

--- SENTENCE 214 ---
Data from this study provide the baseline needed to compare against data from future episodic events, to determine the nitrogen sources fueling algal blooms in the lagoons of Ofu and Olosega.

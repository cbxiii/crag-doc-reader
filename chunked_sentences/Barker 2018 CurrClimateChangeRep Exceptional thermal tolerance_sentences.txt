Abstract

As climate change poses an ever increasing threat to coral reefs globally, understanding why particular corals are resistant to bleaching is paramount to their continued survival.

--- SENTENCE 5 ---
The coral reefs of Ofu Island, American Samoa, provide a living laboratory to examine mechanisms of coral adaptation to extreme thermal conditions and serve as an analog for a future environment impacted by climate change.

--- SENTENCE 6 ---
Three backreef pools exhibit remarkably different temperature regimes, which consequently results in varying levels of coral thermal tolerance.

--- SENTENCE 7 ---
In pool 300, temperatures can reach 35 °C and fluctuate up to 6 °C throughout the day.

--- SENTENCE 8 ---
Pools 400 and 500 are less variable, with temperatures rarely exceeding 32 °C.

--- SENTENCE 9 ---
Yet, the pools contain a highly diverse community of corals, including an abundance of thermally sensitive species.

--- SENTENCE 10 ---
This review summarizes the results of nearly two decades of research into the mechanisms contributing to differential bleaching resistance among pools.

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
Factors examined include the effects of intermittent water flow, previous exposure to subbleaching temperatures, Symbiodinium genotype, modifications of genetic expression within the polyp, and the associated bacterial microbiome.

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
Corals within the highly variable pool 300 appear to be more adequately adapted to thermal extremes by retaining chlorophyll concentrations during frequent heat pulses, associating with thermally tolerant endosymbionts, upregulating gene expression associated with heat acclimatization, and potentially possessing an advantageous microbiome composition.

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
Though encompassing a small geographic area, the findings from Ofu's reefs have widespread implications for coral conservation as they serve to elucidate the impacts of these many confounding factors and their contributions to bleaching resistance.

## Introduction

Coral reefs are vital ecosystems that support immense biological diversity and provide ecological goods and services totaling nearly \$30 billion USD globally

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
They are also one of the ecosystems most heavily impacted by climate change and rising ocean temperatures.

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
Nearly 27% of the world's coral reefs have been effectively lost, with little or no chance at recovery

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
Widespread coral bleaching events due to thermal stress,

Bleaching occurs when the coral host expels its symbiotic zooxanthellae (Symbiodinium sp.)

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
due to adverse environmental conditions.

--- SENTENCE 22 ---

--- SENTENCE 24 ---

American Samoa is located in the south central Pacific Ocean, approximately 4700 km southwest of Hawaii.

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
The US territory is composed of five volcanic islands and two remote atolls: Tutuila, Aunu'u, Ta'ū, Ofu, Olosega, Rose Atoll, and Swains Island.

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
Combined total land area is small, comprising only 196 km² [14].

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
The remote island of Ofu, the site of these exceptional coral reefs, is only 7.3 km² in size [14].

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
A fringing reef surrounds the island of Ofu, extending 80-180 m wide

--- SENTENCE 39 ---
[15]; however, most research has been conducted in a shallow (< 2.5 m) lagoon on the southeastern side of the island, within the boundaries of the National Park of American Samoa (NPSA)

--- SENTENCE 40 ---
Within this lagoon are several backreef pools, which have been the sites of extensive study.

--- SENTENCE 41 ---
At low tide, the pools (referred to in the literature as pool 300/HV, 400/MV, and 500/LV) are cut off from oceanic currents by a high reef crest, and during daytime low-tide events, the water temperature rises dramatically (see Table 1 for physical characteristics).

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
While all of the pools are subjected to elevated temperatures, pool 300 is the most variable.

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
In this pool, water temperatures reach over 35 °C, well above the established regional coral bleaching threshold (approx.

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
32 °C), and temperatures have been recorded fluctuating over 6 °C throughout the day (see Figs. 1, 2, and 3 for long-term temperature datasets)

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
Over 85 species of corals have

been identified within the pools, with *Acropora*, *Pocillopora*, *Porites*, and *Millepora* species present in high numbers

--- SENTENCE 46 ---
Live coral coverage ranges from 25 to 30% and is dominated by massive *Porites* colonies forming microatolls surrounded by large sandy patches

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
Exceptional thermal tolerance has previously been displayed by corals living in extreme temperature environments such as the Persian Gulf

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
However, often only "hardy" coral genera such as Pavona and Cyphastrea are able to withstand these high temperatures [21, 22].

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
Meanwhile, thermally susceptible corals, such as Acropora and Pocillopora, are often disproportionately affected [23, 24].

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
Of u is exceptional in that a diverse assemblage of corals is thermally resistant to bleaching, with almost the entire coral community surviving these temperature extremes.

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
The factors behind the corals' thermotolerance has been the basis of extensive study for nearly two decades.

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
Research suggests that intermittent water flow, previous exposure to elevated, sub-bleaching temperatures, the genetics of endosymbiotic Symbiodinium, modified coral gene expression, and the associated bacterial microbiome may all contribute to bleaching resistance.

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
This review discusses these various factors in detail and critically evaluates their contribution to the thermal tolerance of corals in Ofu.

# Previous Short-term Exposure to Elevated Temperatures

Another line of research suggests that elevated, sub-bleaching temperatures may serve to protect corals from acute bleaching stress

--- SENTENCE 91 ---
This sub-lethal pulse of heat preconditions corals for subsequent bleaching events.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
Variable heat stress of this type occurs regularly in pool 300 over a strong tidal cycle, when ocean temperatures spike at low tide.

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
In one field study, a widespread transcriptional response that involved hundreds of genes was observed in colonies of Acropora hyacinthus at temperatures above 30.5 °C

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
These genes are vital to the unfolded protein response, an ancient eukaryotic cellular response to physiological stress.

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
During the course of the experiment, these genes were only expressed on days of strong tides, elevated temperatures, and large pH and salinity fluctuations.

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
After the temporary heat pulse, expression of these genes returned to baseline levels.

--- SENTENCE 97 ---
When this response was tested in a laboratory setting, the gene expression increased as corals paled and then bleached, suggesting that the unfolded protein response becomes more dramatic as physiological stress increases [[36](#page-9-0)].

--- SENTENCE 98 ---
Pool 300 corals also appear to be able to regulate their Symbiodinium more effectively due to previous thermal stress

!

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
[](_page_3_Picture_6.jpeg)

Coral mortality may also be affected by variable temperature regimes.

--- SENTENCE 100 ---
When nubbins of A. hyacinthus from both pools 300 and 400 were placed in elevated (31.5 °C) and ambient (28 °C) experimental tanks, mortality varied depending upon the source pool [[41\]](#page-9-0).

--- SENTENCE 101 ---
Corals taken from pool 400 experienced nearly 50% mortality, regardless of Symbiodinium type.

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
Corals from pool 300, which regularly experiences a wide range of thermal variability, experienced low mortality (16.6 ± 8.8%), statistically indistinguishable from the control group

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
It has been suggested that the harsher environment found in pool 300 acts as a Bselective sieve^ for poorly adapted individuals, which are quickly removed from the population [\[42\]](#page-9-0).

--- SENTENCE 104 ---
In one analysis, a large range in daily temperatures was found to be the most important variable in predicting future bleaching prevalence [[43](#page-9-0)].

--- SENTENCE 105 ---
The authors concluded that a 1 °C increase in diel temperature fluctuations reduced the risk of severe bleaching by a factor of 33.

--- SENTENCE 106 ---
In pool 300, temperatures can fluctuate over 6 °C during a strong tidal cycle [\[16](#page-8-0)], which may be a critical component to the corals' thermotolerance.

## Dinoflagellate Endosymbionts

The advent of new genetic sequencing technologies has allowed researchers to examine the unique genetic structure of endosymbiotic Symbiodinium and determine how various clades enhance coral health and bleaching resistance.

--- SENTENCE 108 ---
It is generally concluded that Symbiodinium clade D confers enhanced thermal tolerance to the coral host and can increase

!

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
[](_page_3_Picture_10.jpeg)

<span id="page-4-0"></span>post-stress survival [\[44](#page-9-0)–[47](#page-9-0)].

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
Clade D has been shown to associate with a wide-variety of scleractinian coral genera including branching Acropora, massive Montastrea, encrusting Montipora, as well as free living Fungia

--- SENTENCE 111 ---
Clade D is a relatively rare clade that represents less than 10% of total dinoflagellate endosymbionts

--- SENTENCE 112 ---
[[48](#page-9-0)] but is prevalent in American Samoa, particularly at the backreef pools

--- SENTENCE 113 ---
The Symbiodinium makeup of the Ofu corals is primarily composed of one genotype of clade D and four genotypes of clade C, which may exist in the same colony at various abundances [\[50](#page-9-0)].

--- SENTENCE 114 ---
In one study, 88% of corals tested hosted multiple genotypes (for a full list of clade differentiation by species, see Table 2)

--- SENTENCE 115 ---
In another, 9 out of 10 sampled coral species associated with multiple clades, with only Porites annae exclusively containing clade C in both pools 300 and 400 [[51\]](#page-9-0).

--- SENTENCE 116 ---
Hosting multiple clades may allow for rapid acclimatization to changing temperatures [\[54](#page-9-0)] but clade D's thermotolerance was clear: only clade D was detected within A. hyacinthus in pool 300, while the less variable pools 400 and 500 contained a mix of both clades C and D

--- SENTENCE 117 ---
In another study, clade D made up a higher proportion of Symbiodinium associated with pool 300 corals in 8 out of 9 species examined [\[50\]](#page-9-0).

--- SENTENCE 118 ---
In an extreme example, Acropora pulchra showed 100% of samples dominated by clade C in pool 400 and 100% of samples dominated by clade D in pool 300 (p = 0.005, Table 2).

--- SENTENCE 119 ---
It is poorly understood how different clades vary physiologically under thermal stress and how these variations may impact coral bleaching resistance.

--- SENTENCE 120 ---
While the coral hosts can modify their gene expression in response to heat stress ([[55\]](#page-9-0), see B[Host Genetics](#page-5-0)^), Symbiodinium do not appear to do the same [\[56\]](#page-9-0).

--- SENTENCE 121 ---
Neither clade C nor D Symbiodinium exhibited a transcriptome-wide response to short-term heat stress after 3 days of elevated temperatures [[56](#page-9-0)].

--- SENTENCE 122 ---
However, between clades, hundreds of genes naturally exhibit different expressions as the result of divergent genotypes.

--- SENTENCE 123 ---
This paradox has been termed the Btranscriptional conundrum^; different clades of Symbiodinium result in differing thermal tolerances yet display little to no short-term response to heat stress.

--- SENTENCE 124 ---
The answer may lie in environmental constraints, as it is likely that Symbiodinium are limited in their ability to respond to temperature changes by modifying their gene expression.

--- SENTENCE 125 ---
While the prevalence of clade D Symbiodinium is promising, there are still concerns about which corals it associates with, particularly regarding abundant Poritesspecies.

--- SENTENCE 126 ---
The Ofu pools contain at least six known species of Poritesincluding P. lobata, P. lichen, P. cylindrica, P. annae, and Porites mound sp., as well as an unknown species designated Porites sp. 2 (recently classified as P. randalli)

--- SENTENCE 127 ---
All samples of P. lobata, P. annae, and Porites mound sp. tested contained primarily clade C Symbiodinium

--- SENTENCE 128 ---
This trend has also been observed in a habitually warm marine lake in Palau, where 6 out of 7 Porites species tested harbored clade C

While clade D may confer thermal tolerance, it appears to be selectively associating with coral hosts, which could leave a large portion of the Ofu community vulnerable to thermal bleaching.

--- SENTENCE 180 ---
For corals that are not associated with a high abundance of clade D, some may partake in symbiont swapping to favor more thermally tolerant individuals and selectively expel more sensitive symbionts [\[59](#page-9-0)].

--- SENTENCE 182 ---
Yet, when examined postbleaching, clade D often becomes dominant.

--- SENTENCE 183 ---
In a study conducted on Keppels Island, Australia, 93.5% of A. millepora sampled before bleaching contained clade C2, a thermally sensitive clade of Symbiodinium.

--- SENTENCE 184 ---

--- SENTENCE 185 ---

--- SENTENCE 189 ---

--- SENTENCE 190 ---

--- SENTENCE 192 ---

--- SENTENCE 193 ---
[\[67](#page-9-0)] and A. millepora

--- SENTENCE 194 ---

--- SENTENCE 195 ---
However, the opposite effect has been found in colonies of P. eydeuxi on Ofu [\[17\]](#page-8-0).

--- SENTENCE 196 ---
Individuals in pool 300 actually had greater skeletal growth than their pool 400 conspecifics, despite the presence of clade D Symbiodinium.

--- SENTENCE 197 ---
These contrasting results may have to do with environmental factors.

--- SENTENCE 198 ---

--- SENTENCE 199 ---
However, it has also been suggested that symbiont type explains Ba negligible fraction^ of the variation in bleaching resistance [\[37\]](#page-9-0).

--- SENTENCE 200 ---
In transplantation studies, the introduction of coral fragments to a more extreme environment would suggest that the corals would partake in symbiont swapping, favoring a more thermotolerant clade.

--- SENTENCE 201 ---
However, only a minimal shift in clade composition (< 1 to around 2%) was observed when A. hyacinthusfragments were transplanted from pool 400 to pool 300

--- SENTENCE 202 ---
Another study conducted in pool 400 found that only one of seven colonies of A. hyacinthus modified its associated Symbiodinium composition to include a higher percentage of clade D during a natural bleaching event

--- SENTENCE 203 ---
Ten months later, the colony had returned to its pre-bleaching proportions.

--- SENTENCE 204 ---
There is also evidence of corals living in extreme thermal environments that associate with more generalist clades and still experience minimal visible bleaching [[19\]](#page-8-0).

--- SENTENCE 205 ---
In a comparison of the endosymbiotic makeup of 9 coral species in the Arabian/Persian Gulf, none were found to associate with clade D, despite summer temperatures reaching up to 36 °C

--- SENTENCE 206 ---
These results bring into question the importance of Symbiodinium clade and postulate that association with thermally tolerant endosymbionts is not the sole determining factor in coral thermotolerance.

--- SENTENCE 207 ---
Clearly, the effects of Clade D Symbiodinium on their coral hosts is more nuanced than the widely held belief that clade D is ideal for enhancing coral thermotolerance but poor for accelerated growth.

--- SENTENCE 208 ---
More research is needed to determine under which environmental conditions Clade D is advantageous and how this impacts other physiological aspects of the coral host.

--- SENTENCE 209 ---
## Host Genetics

The coral host is capable of modifying its gene expression under conditions of thermal stress

--- SENTENCE 210 ---
This is a transcriptome-wide response, influencing the expression of hundreds of genes related to heat acclimatization, including those regulating apoptosis, antioxidant enzymes, and heat shock proteins [[37](#page-9-0),

--- SENTENCE 211 ---
These changes occur widely throughout the coral tissue, with both epidermal and symbiontcontaining gastrodermal cells impacted [[71\]](#page-10-0).

--- SENTENCE 212 ---
When comparing gene expression between coral individuals in the different Ofu pools, A. hyacinthus in pool 300 appears to Bfrontload^ many of these protective genes, expressing them at higher levels under control conditions than pool 400 conspecifics [\[55](#page-9-0)].

--- SENTENCE 213 ---
Transcriptome-wide changes occur rapidly after the onset of heat stress.

--- SENTENCE 214 ---
For instance, 14% of the A. hyacinthus transcriptome was differentially expressed after 120 min at 34– 35 °C

--- SENTENCE 215 ---
In another study, twenty-seven percent of the A.

!

--- SENTENCE 216 ---
[](_page_5_Picture_11.jpeg)

hyacinthus transcriptome was significantly regulated after 1 h at 35 °C

--- SENTENCE 217 ---
However, after 15 h of heat exposure, only 12% of the transcriptome was significantly regulated, despite visible bleaching.

--- SENTENCE 218 ---
This return to normal levels of gene expression after acute stress has been deemed Btranscriptome resilience^ and it could play a key part in determining bleaching severity [\[69\]](#page-9-0).

--- SENTENCE 219 ---
When gene expression levels dropped off quickly, the coral generally experienced limited bleaching.

--- SENTENCE 220 ---
However, if these high levels of gene expression were sustained over a long period of time, bleaching was often severe

--- SENTENCE 221 ---
Therefore, it may not only matter which genes are impacted but the rate at which they return to baseline levels which determines bleaching severity.

--- SENTENCE 222 ---
It is important to note that early signs of cellular degeneration can occur several days before bleaching is observed [\[72\]](#page-10-0) and transcriptome-wide stress responses may persist for months after [\[53\]](#page-9-0).

--- SENTENCE 223 ---
Even when corals do not appear visibly bleached, they exhibit cellular effects with severe necrosis of both the coral and Symbiodinium cells

--- SENTENCE 224 ---
After only 5 h of elevated temperatures, Symbiodinium appeared misshapen, with shrunken and lysed nuclei

--- SENTENCE 225 ---
Further, genetic effects in the coral host are felt long after the bleaching event has subsided.

--- SENTENCE 226 ---
Nearly 20% of the transcriptome of A. hyacinthus colonies was impacted during the 2015 bleaching event and a large proportion remained perturbed for up to 6 months later, even after temperatures had fallen and Symbiodinium populations had rebounded [\[53](#page-9-0)].

--- SENTENCE 227 ---
Hundreds of genes remained upregulated for a full year after the bleaching event, including those associated with apoptotic and stress response proteins

--- SENTENCE 228 ---
Therefore, any sustained temperatures above the mean monthly maximum should be treated as potentially damaging, even when bleaching is not evident or the coral appears to be fully recovered.

--- SENTENCE 229 ---
Analysis of coral gene expression demonstrates that bleaching resistance is at least partially the result of many small genetic changes working in concert.

--- SENTENCE 230 ---
The Ofu studies have been conducted primarily on A. hyacinthus but A. millepora

--- SENTENCE 231 ---
[[75\]](#page-10-0), and Montastrea faveolata

--- SENTENCE 232 ---
[\[76\]](#page-10-0) have also been shown to differentially express genes associated with thermotolerance during heat stress events.

--- SENTENCE 233 ---
One study even suggests that dramatic variations in gene expression can differentially occur at the same time within the same coral colony

--- SENTENCE 234 ---
[\[73\]](#page-10-0), indicating that small scale variation in gene expression may be important to coral survival and post-bleaching recovery.

# Microbiome

The microbiome associated with Ofu corals varies between pools and may shift during periods of heat stress.

--- SENTENCE 236 ---
A. hyacinthus colonies within pools 300 and 400 were determined to have a significantly different assemblage of bacterial microbiomes (p < 0.001)

--- SENTENCE 237 ---
Pool 300 microbiomes were characterized by three main bacterial families: Alteromonadaceae (~ 15%), Rhodospirillaceae (~ 15%), and Hahellaceae (13%).

--- SENTENCE 238 ---
Pool 400 microbiomes were dominated by Hahellaceae (27%) and Alteromonadaceae (24%), but were lacking in Rhodospirillaceae (< 2%).

--- SENTENCE 239 ---
In a 17-month reciprocal transplant study, pool 400 natives altered their microbial composition when placed in the thermally variable pool 300, yet pool 300 natives retained their original microbiomes when transplanted.

--- SENTENCE 240 ---
In a follow-up laboratory study, the pool 400 microbiomes were significantly affected by stress within 20 h and the corals were visibly bleached

--- SENTENCE 241 ---
Very little experimentation has been conducted to determine if particular bacteria enhance coral thermotolerance; however, the dramatic difference in bacterial community composition between pools 300 and 400 suggests that the microbiome is similarly affected by heat stress.

--- SENTENCE 242 ---
Microbiome composition may be a novel component to examine in understanding coral bleaching physiology and resilience.

## Managing Ofu Coral Reefs in the Time of Climate Change

Ofu corals face a myriad of natural and anthropogenic challenges but climate change is the most concerning and widespread.

--- SENTENCE 243 ---
One third of corals are already at an elevated risk of extinction due to climate change-related events and as ocean temperatures continue to rise, bleaching events are expected to occur more frequently and with more devastating effects

--- SENTENCE 244 ---
During the 2015–2016 mass bleaching event, corals on the island of Tutuila were heavily impacted, with some sites showing nearly 100% bleaching, particularly of thermally sensitive genera such as Acropora and Pocillopora

--- SENTENCE 245 ---
Followup surveys in a backreef pool by the Pago Pago International Airport estimated Acropora sp. mortality at nearly 80%, likely due to the bleaching event.

--- SENTENCE 246 ---
Corals in the Ofu pools were impacted by the bleaching event as well, though to a lesser extent.

--- SENTENCE 247 ---
Surveys conducted in February 2015 indicated low to moderate bleaching with Acropora, Millepora, and some massive Porites species impacted.

--- SENTENCE 248 ---
Additional surveys were not conducted again until February 2017, when widespread bleaching was observed by the American Samoa Coral Reef Advisory Group (CRAG) scientists.

--- SENTENCE 249 ---
Bleaching prevalence appeared nondiscriminatory as affected corals included both branching and plating Acropora, massive Porites, Isopora, Montipora, Pocillopora, Favia, and Favites species [\[79](#page-10-0)].

--- SENTENCE 250 ---
Percent mortality from this bleaching event is unknown.

--- SENTENCE 251 ---
NPSA bleaching surveys conducted in September and November 2017 indicate continued bleaching with Acropora, Montipora, Goniastria, Pocillopora colonies affected [\[79](#page-10-0)].

!

--- SENTENCE 252 ---
[](_page_6_Picture_9.jpeg)

Whether or not corals will be able to survive the changing climate is unknown.

--- SENTENCE 253 ---
The number of reefs globally that are impacted by bleaching stress has tripled since 1985 and 97% of reef areas examined display an increasing trend in sea surface temperatures [[4\]](#page-8-0).

--- SENTENCE 254 ---
One evolutionary model based upon the prevalence of heat-adapted alleles in A. hyacinthus from Rarotonga, Cook Islands, suggests that those corals will be able to genetically adapt to tolerate temperatures predicted under IPCC Representative Concentration Pathways (RCPs) low CO2 emission plans RCP2.6 and RCP4.5

--- SENTENCE 255 ---
However, simulated populations were unable to adapt quickly enough to survive the dramatic temperature increases predicted in RCP6.0 or RCP8.5.

--- SENTENCE 256 ---
It is projected that the Pacific Ocean will continue to warm under RCP4.5 – RCP8.5 trajectories

--- SENTENCE 257 ---
[[81\]](#page-10-0), and therefore, under this evolutionary model, A. hyacinthus population collapse will occur when the average temperature reaches 29 °C, or approximately 2040 under RCP8.5

--- SENTENCE 258 ---
Therefore, it seems likely that without massive reductions in greenhouse gas emissions, many corals will potentially face extinction in the coming century.

--- SENTENCE 259 ---
The Ofu pools serve as an analog for a future environment impacted by climate change, simulating the thermal extremes projected to occur as the planet continues to warm.

--- SENTENCE 260 ---
They therefore serve as a valuable resource in determining how to mitigate future thermal bleaching.

--- SENTENCE 261 ---
However, climate change is unlikely to be solved with short-term, local scale management solutions and instead will rely on sweeping changes in national and international policy [\[82,](#page-10-0)

--- SENTENCE 262 ---
Therefore, resource managers at the National Park of American Samoa are working to improve reef resiliency by mitigating local stressors, such as outbreaks of crown of thorns seastars and terrestrial nutrient runoff, which are more responsive to local management.

--- SENTENCE 263 ---
They are also taking a passive monitoring approach by measuring environmental variables such as sea surface temperature, salinity, ocean acidity, etc. in order to quickly respond to any future algae outbreaks or sources of nutrient eutrophication.

--- SENTENCE 264 ---
Well-managed coral ecosystems can recover from bleaching events more quickly and more successfully than their degraded counterparts, which may reduce the time required for full reef recovery by years or even decades [[82\]](#page-10-0).

--- SENTENCE 265 ---
American Samoa's coral reefs and associated mangroves provide nearly \$6 million annually in fisheries, recreation, and shoreline protection, accounting for 1.2% of the territory's gross domestic product

--- SENTENCE 266 ---
This makes their management a top priority for marine resource managers and the local community.

--- SENTENCE 267 ---
In order to achieve longterm success, the local drivers of coral bleaching need to be carefully understood and monitored so that resource managers can predict future bleaching events and adopt an adaptive management style based on environmental fluctuations and trends.

--- SENTENCE 268 ---
One possible method of reducing physiological stress during bleaching events would be the introduction of shading.

--- SENTENCE 269 ---
To date, only one study has explored the potential of using shading techniques to protect Ofu corals from intense sunlight under elevated water temperatures

--- SENTENCE 270 ---
Fragments of A. muricata, P. damicornis, and P. cylindrica from pool 400 were placed in aquaria where the corals were subjected to various temperature and light regimes: a control (28.5 °C, approximately the mean seawater temperature of pool 400), and elevated thermal conditions (31.5 °C) with no shading, 50% shading, and 75% shading.

--- SENTENCE 271 ---
It was found that a higher the degree of shading resulted in a reduction in bleaching severity.

--- SENTENCE 272 ---
Statistically significant changes in coral coloration from the control were detected as early as DHW 1 for thermally sensitive A. muricata fragments under elevated temperatures with no shading.

--- SENTENCE 273 ---
All corals under elevated water temperatures grew slower than the controls, with non-shaded corals growing less than those in the 50% or 75% shade.

--- SENTENCE 274 ---
This study indicates that shading corals can reduce their risk of bleaching under elevated water temperatures, particularly for thermally sensitive species such as A. muricata and P. cylindrica

--- SENTENCE 275 ---
However, very little research has been done in this area and the efficacy of coral shading during a bleaching event has yet to be tested in the field.

--- SENTENCE 276 ---
Further, one important consideration regarding shading is whether or not it is practical at the scale of an entire reef.

--- SENTENCE 277 ---
It would likely be difficult, if not impossible, to adequately shade corals across the entirety of pool 400, or even a small subsection, due to logistical concerns.

--- SENTENCE 278 ---
Therefore, while shading has been shown to reduce light intensity and subsequent bleaching, far more work is needed to determine if this method is realistic to offset the effects of thermal stress.

--- SENTENCE 279 ---
One potential long-term goal of the National Park may be to spread the genetic basis of these Bsuper corals^ by enhancing outside populations through transplantation.

--- SENTENCE 280 ---
The hydrographic isolation of the Ofu pools and the amount of time spent cut off from the broader currents of the area limit the feasibility of coral recruitment into the wider region.

--- SENTENCE 281 ---
While no current studies have been conducted to determine the success of larval transport and dispersal to neighboring islands, Ofu corals are genetically distinct from corals found on the nearby island of Tutuila, despite being less than 100 km apart [[86\]](#page-10-0).

--- SENTENCE 282 ---
Therefore, while Ofu may not serve as a viable Bseed^ reef for other islands in the region, it serves as a stronghold of thermally tolerant genetic diversity and could function as a nursery site for future coral transplantation work.

--- SENTENCE 283 ---
Due to the financial and logistical complications involved in remote coral transplantation, robust species with high rates of survivorship post-transplantation should be selected for, such as slowgrowing massive Porites species

--- SENTENCE 284 ---
Corals thriving in adverse thermal conditions are found only in a few places around the world and their predisposition to rising ocean temperatures may prove vital in restoring heavily impacted, higher latitude reefs.

--- SENTENCE 285 ---
Coral larvae have been shown to experience a tenfold increase in survival during heat events (35.5 °C) when their parents came from warm, low latitude environments, which is

!

--- SENTENCE 286 ---
[](_page_7_Picture_6.jpeg)

<span id="page-8-0"></span>likely due to inherited differences in the expression of thermotolerant genes

--- SENTENCE 287 ---
Therefore, Ofu corals could serve as a reserve of thermotolerant diversity, to be transplanted on imperiled reefs into the future.

--- SENTENCE 288 ---
The success of Ofu's corals offers a beacon of hope for corals globally in the face of climate change and rising ocean temperatures.

--- SENTENCE 289 ---
The many interconnected factors associated with bleaching resilience make it difficult to determine the proportion that each factor contributes to overall reef resiliency.

--- SENTENCE 290 ---
While a small number of studies have focused on multiple factors such as the role of water flow and the symbiont genotype on bleaching prevalence [17], we are still far from a holistic understanding of how all of these factors interact.

--- SENTENCE 291 ---
However, the distinctive abiotic environment, previous exposure to elevated temperatures, thermotolerant endosymbionts, modification of coral gene expression, and the associated bacterial microbiome all appear to contribute to Ofu corals' thermal tolerance.

--- SENTENCE 292 ---
In one resiliency model, having an abundance of heat-tolerant coral species was determined to be the most important indicator for predicting longterm reef success and survival

--- SENTENCE 293 ---
The second most important indicator was previous exposure to temperature variability

--- SENTENCE 294 ---
Therefore, it is likely that Ofu corals are at a distinct advantage and may fare better than many reefs globally.

--- SENTENCE 295 ---
However, from 2015 to 2017, bleaching was observed within the Ofu backpool reefs

--- SENTENCE 296 ---
[\[79\]](#page-10-0), making the need for immediate global mitigation of climate change not only necessary but critical to coral survival.

--- SENTENCE 297 ---
The Ofu corals face a unique predicament; while they are highly adapted to hightemperature environments, when will they too reach their thermal limits?

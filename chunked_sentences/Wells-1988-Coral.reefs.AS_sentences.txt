#### INTRODUCTION

### General Description

American Samoa, an unincorporated territory of the U.S.A., comprises the six eastern islands of the Samoan Archipelago as well as Swains Atoll, which is geographically part of the Tokelau group.

--- SENTENCE 5 ---
The land area covers 76 sq. mi.

--- SENTENCE 6 ---
The geology of the Samoan Islands is described by Stearns (1944).

--- SENTENCE 7 ---
All the islands except Swains Atoll are aligned along the crest of a discontinuous submarine ridge which extends over 485 km and trends roughly north-west by south-east.

--- SENTENCE 8 ---
Swains and Rose Atolls are limestone, the others are composed principally of volcanic rock and are typically steep-sided, with little in the way of coastal plains, and with lush vegetation.

--- SENTENCE 9 ---
Terrestrial aspects of the ecology of the islands are described in some detail in Amerson et al. (1982) and Dahl (1970 and 1973) provides short ecological reports.

--- SENTENCE 10 ---
Tutuila is the largest island and is the top of a composite volcano rising approximately three miles (4.8 km) from the ocean floor, which results in deep water near shore.

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
There are many small but no major streams and the island is almost bisected on the south-east by a deep natural harbour, Pago Pago Harbor.

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
The coastline, except at the mouths of drowned alluvial valleys, is irregular, rocky and composed of steep cliffs of variable height.

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
American Samoa has a warm, humid tropical climate with average temperature of 70-90°F (21-32°C) and average humidity of 80%; mean rainfall is about 200 in.

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
(5080 mm), the heaviest rains occurring from December to March.

--- SENTENCE 15 ---
It is in the zone of the south-east trade winds which are moderate from May to November.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
During the remainder of the year winds are variable; the strongest occur during the winter months of June to August, and the weakest are from December to February.

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
Major hurricanes are experienced about once every five years; these normally approach from the north but occasionally come from other directions.

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
Tsunamis may also occur although only inner Pago Pago Harbor has experienced any sizeable run-up (USDC, 1984).

The islands are rich in fringing reefs although these are relatively narrow and lack good near-shore drop-offs.

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
They typically consist of a shallow lagoon or moat (about 2 m deep), a shallower fore-reef, a reef crest (usually emergent at low tide), a surge zone (with spur and groove formation on the south-west windward side) and a sharp reef front dropping 5-10 m to a reef terrace and gradually descending to deep water.

--- SENTENCE 34 ---
Most of the reefs have passes; the maximum width is 500 m and most are much narrower (Dahl, 1970 and 1973).

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
The reefs of American Samoa are among the best documented in the South Pacific region, those around Tutuila having been studied for nearly 70 years (Dahl, Lamberts (1983a) describes early scientific expeditions to the islands, the only extensive study of the reefs being the Carnegie Institution programme of 1917 to 1920.

--- SENTENCE 36 ---

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
Two thirds of the coastline of Tutuila (about 55 km), particularly to the south, is bordered by narrow fringing reefs which are partially exposed at low tide.

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
There are no barrier reefs and only a single well-developed lagoon (Pala Lagoon).

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
Beyond the breakers on the seaward margin of the reef flat, the bottom slopes rapidly to very deep water, except south-west of Aunu'u Island where depths are less because of the drowned barrier reef Nafanua Bank.

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
Because of rapid submergence during the last period of Pleistocene sea-level rise, the limited areas of fringing reefs are discontinuous and consist primarily of bedded calcareous sand and silt rather than coral reef framework (Stearns, 1944).

--- SENTENCE 54 ---
The most important reefs, identified in the course of compiling the coral reef inventory, are in Pago Pago Harbor, Utulei, Aua, Faga'alu, Tafananai, Alega, Faga'itua, Aoa, Masefau, Afono, Vatia, Fagasa, Massacre, Maloata, Poloa, Amanave, Nua-Se'etaga and Leone, Asili, Pala Lagoon, Matuu and around Aunu'u Island.

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
Structurally the most important reefs occur at Pala Lagoon, Faleapoi Pt (south of Leone), Coconut Pt, outer Pago Pago Harbor and Faga'itua Bay on the south coast and Aoa, Masefau, Fagasa and Poloa Bays on the north coast.

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
Much of the north coast lacks structural reefs altogether.

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
Important submerged structural reefs occur as the two offshore banks, Taema and Nafanua (Maragos, 1986; AF and AECOS, 1980).

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
The broadest fringing reef extends about 1000 m from the shore in the south mid portion of the island, at Nu'u'uli, near Pago Pago Airport.

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
It protects the shallow estuary, Pala Lagoon, separated from it in part by the sandy peninsula, Coconut Pt.

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
Prior to construction work for the airport, the deeper parts of this area had large thickets of staghorn acroporid corals some of which were over 30 m across and 2 m high (Helfrich et al., 1975).

--- SENTENCE 61 ---

Leatherback Turtles Dermochelys coriacea, Olive Ridleys Lepidochelys olivacea and Loggerheads Caretta caretta have been recorded in American Samoan waters, and both Green Turtles Chelonia mydas and Hawksbills Eretmochelys imbricata nest, mainly on Rose Atoll with apparently scattered nesting elsewhere, principally in the Manu'a group, although it is possible that Eretmochelys also nests sparsely on Tutuila (Amerson et al., 1982; Anon., 1979; Johannes, 1986).

#### Reef Resources

Tutuila is the most populated island with 90% of the total population of American Samoa (35 000 in 1980); one third lives around Pago Pago Bay and the remainder live mainly in small villages on the limited flat coastal areas.

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
Much of the inner part of the island is still relatively pristine as a result of the inaccessibility of the central ridge system.

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
The Manu'a group have a considerably lower population density and no industry and are therefore little changed, but the population is again concentrated on the coast in several small villages.

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
The Samoan people have historically relied on reef and lagoon organisms for a substantial part of their diet.

--- SENTENCE 97 ---
Fishing practices were surveyed by the Office of Marine Resources between 1977 and 1980 on Tutuila and the results are described by Wass (1983).

--- SENTENCE 98 ---
Additional information is provided by Hill (1978).

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
Shoreline recreational fishing is traditionally important and about 300 tonnes of fish a year are taken in this way.

--- SENTENCE 100 ---
Office of Marine and Wildlife Resources is conducting a bottom fish stock assessment in conjuction with the U.S. National Marine Fisheries Service (Itano, 1987).

--- SENTENCE 101 ---
Both eggs and meat of turtles (mainly Eretmochelys imbricata) are eaten, and tortoiseshell is used locally for jewellery and decoration.

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
Johannes (1986) estimated that ca 50 turtles a year were taken on Tutuila and Olosega, although in general there was little interest in catching turtles.

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
In the early 1980s the 50 or so inhabitants of Swains Atoll (see separate account) apparently relied to some extent on turtle eggs and meat for subsistence (Balazs, 1982).

--- SENTENCE 104 ---
Tourism and reef-related recreational activities are popular in some areas, particularly at Goat Island Pt and Fagatele Bay (see se parate accounts).

### Disturbances and Deficiencies

The reefs of American Samoa have suffered disturbance from a variety of causes, and a 1979 coral reef inventory

showed relatively few areas with more than 50% coral cover.

--- SENTENCE 105 ---
The reefs of Tutuila were subjected to a severe infestation of Acanthaster planci in 1978-1979 (Beulig et al., 1981; Birkeland and Randall, 1979).

--- SENTENCE 106 ---
Damage, particularly to Acropora, was widespread with reefs at the following sites being severely damaged or wiped out: Taema, Nafanua, Faga'itua Bay, Aunu'u, Onenoa, Aoa, Sailele, Masefau, Afono, Vatia, Tafeu Cove, Fagasa, Fagafue, Aasu, Aoloau, Fagamalo, Maloata, Poloa, Vailoatai, Fagatele, Fagalua (Larsen) Bay and the airport.

--- SENTENCE 107 ---
Recolonization has been slow and new colonies are still small.

--- SENTENCE 108 ---
Reefs of the Manu'a islands Surveys in 1987 revealed fair were unaffected.

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
concentrations of Acanthaster on Ofu although these are not considered to represent a major infestation (Itano, 1987 and in litt., 29.7.87).

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
Silt-laden freshwater from torrential rain often overlays the Pago Pago reefs and in 1924 was responsible for considerable coral death (Mayor, 1924a).

--- SENTENCE 111 ---
In 1966 a hurricane caused terrestrial damage but only minor harm to the reefs (Dahl and Lamberts, 1977).

--- SENTENCE 112 ---
Hurricane Tusi, on 17th January 1987, caused considerable terrestrial damage on the Manu'a islands but did not result in large destructive swells and thus caused relatively little direct damage to the reefs.

--- SENTENCE 113 ---
The vegetation has reportedly recovered well in general, with little excess erosion, although two drainage basins on the north coast of Ta'u (Auauli and Avatele) were gutted by excessive runoff, leading to increased sediment load on adjacent reefs with some evidence of coral smothering (Itano, 1987 and in litt., 29.7.87).

--- SENTENCE 114 ---
In many reef moats on Tutuila, large thickets of *Acropora formosa*, the lower parts of which are often dead and the upper parts killed in a sharply demarcated line, have been found presumably corresponding to extreme low water tide level (Dahl and Lamberts, 1977).

--- SENTENCE 115 ---
There has been some coral mortality where it is not known whether the cause was natural or man-induced.

--- SENTENCE 116 ---
On Tutuila, extensive coral death occurred in 1973 on the reefs bounded by Coconut Pt, Pago Pago Airport and out to the reef edge.

--- SENTENCE 117 ---
All the corals of the dominant suborder including Acropora, Astrocoeniina, Monti pora and Pocillo pora, died to a depth of 6 m within an area of at least 8 ha.

--- SENTENCE 118 ---
Fungiids and faviids remained healthy (Lamberts, 1983b).

--- SENTENCE 119 ---
It was postulated that the mortality was caused by some event connected with the erection of a fish trap in the area, such as the addition of poisons to the water, but subsequent experiments proved nothing conclusively.

--- SENTENCE 120 ---
Lamberts (1983b) studied recolonization rates of this reef area.

--- SENTENCE 121 ---
Most of the species that had died out have re-established themselves, but the natural processes are being modified by the dredging of borrow pits, which may also account for increased beach erosion along Coconut Pt.

--- SENTENCE 122 ---
The Acropora thickets previously found in these areas will probably never be fully re-established because of changes in substrate (increased sediment) but it is thought that the reef may eventually recover.

--- SENTENCE 123 ---
Similar areas of coral kill were observed in the north shore bay of Masefau and at the edge of moderately deep water in Faga'itua Bay.

--- SENTENCE 124 ---
Human activities are having an increasing impact on the reef.

--- SENTENCE 125 ---
Coastal areas, particularly Pago Pago Harbor, Pala Lagoon and the more populated inlets, have been fairly extensively degraded through pollution, coral smothering through sedimentation and siltation, fish dynamiting and

fish poisoning.

--- SENTENCE 126 ---
Littoral erosion arising from inadequate protection against wave energy has, in the past, removed considerable portions of the narrow coastal platform.

--- SENTENCE 127 ---
Increased agricultural usage of steep slopes and increasing numbers of dredging and construction projects including road construction have resulted in erosion with deposition of terrigenous silt in sheltered bays and on reef areas adjacent to stream outfalls; significant portions of Pago Pago Bay, Faga'itua Bay, Leone Bay and Pala Lagoon have been silted over.

--- SENTENCE 128 ---
Dredging and blasting activities in at least 20 sites around Tutuila and the Manu'a islands have resulted in direct destruction of the reef; dredging in some cases has been to provide access to isolated villages, but has often been carried out to provide a source of road-bed materials (SPREP, 1980; Swerdloff, 1973).

--- SENTENCE 129 ---
There are also problems with the containment of oil spills and the dislocation of coastal outline due to irregular reclamation practices including land filling with rubbish.

--- SENTENCE 130 ---
In recent years there has been a serious problem of littering and rubbish dumping on reefs and in lagoons adjacent to villages.

--- SENTENCE 131 ---
On Tutuila, the reefs in Pago Pago Bay have suffered particular damage.

--- SENTENCE 132 ---
Between 1942 and 1945, the military dredged several inshore areas for landfill, there was an increase in harbour traffic and shipping converted from coal to oil.

--- SENTENCE 133 ---
Tuna canneries were established on the north shore of the harbour in 1956 and dredging operations were expanded in 1960.

--- SENTENCE 134 ---
By 1973 the tuna canneries and a marine railway in the harbour serviced an ocean-going fleet of over 250 fishing ships and the port facilities are increasingly visited by tour ships and used as a freight transhipment point.

--- SENTENCE 135 ---
High turbidity and siltation caused the death of most corals in the inner half of the bay as described by Dahl and Lamberts (1977).

--- SENTENCE 136 ---
Approximately 95% of the reefs at the back of the bay fronting the villages of Fagatogo and Pago Pago have been filled (Wass, 1983).

--- SENTENCE 137 ---
Only the shallow reefs near the mouth of the bay, with a relatively high rate of water exchange, have remained viable, and a portion of that area has been destroyed by dredging.

--- SENTENCE 138 ---
Organic pollution is also a problem; untreated sewage, polluted streams and untreated wastes from two fish canneries flow into the bay which has changed from a clear-water coral reef regime to a turbid silty area.

--- SENTENCE 139 ---
The problem has been accentuated by the increased use of agricultural fertilizers.

--- SENTENCE 140 ---
Oil streaks often cover the bay, originating from the bilge residue of commercial vessels (primarily the long line fishing fleet), spillage at the fuel dock and leakage from deteriorating underground fuel oil pipe lines (Swerdloff, 1973).

--- SENTENCE 141 ---
Maragos (1972) monitored the growth and survival of transplanted corals at several sites in the harbour and reported greater mortality and least growth at the innermost site, Goat Island Reef (see separate account), concluding that poorer water quality inside the bay was the cause.

--- SENTENCE 142 ---
Dahl and Lamberts (1977) reported occasional small oil spills and the drainage of urban and industrial wastes into the harbour.

--- SENTENCE 143 ---
Treatment projects are, however, under way (see below) and Dahl and Lamberts (1977) suggested that Aua Reef is gradually recovering from earlier stress.

--- SENTENCE 144 ---
Significant damage was caused to reefs adjacent to Pala Lagoon by the extension of the commercial airfield out into the lagoon.

--- SENTENCE 145 ---
An assessment of the expected impact of dredging in Pala Lagoon for a proposed boat basin was carried out by Helfrich et al. (1975).

--- SENTENCE 146 ---
Devaney and Suzumoto (1977) carried out a survey of Auasi Harbor in the context of a harbour development project; some reef

damage may have occurred.

--- SENTENCE 147 ---
The reefs at Leone Bay are potentially threatened by a planned boat harbour (Itano in litt., 29.7.87).

--- SENTENCE 148 ---
The Manu'a Islands, although free from extensive construction and agricultural usage, have suffered some reef destruction.

--- SENTENCE 149 ---
Channels have been dredged at Ofu and Ta'u for small boat harbours and blasting of the Asaga Strait between Ofu and Olosega as part of a bridge and road project has caused some damage to reefs (Maragos, 1986).

--- SENTENCE 150 ---
A large land slide caused by road construction on Olosega had covered some 5 sq. km of reef flat by July 1987 and was expected to cause more damage as construction and associated erosion (Buckley in litt., 4.8.87).

--- SENTENCE 151 ---
Small boat harbour dredging and construction on Aunu'u, Ofu, and Fusi (Ta'u) has probably caused some reef damage but also created coral and fish habitat (Itano in litt., 29.7.87).

--- SENTENCE 152 ---
The recent rapid population growth has put considerable pressure on reef resources and traditional conservation practices have largely been discarded.

--- SENTENCE 153 ---
The dependancy of American Samoans on their marine resources is decreasing, due to the availability of canned and frozen foods, but seafood consumption remains high.

--- SENTENCE 154 ---
Increased mobility has led to a tendency to fish reefs of neighbouring villages.

--- SENTENCE 155 ---
Reef fishermen consider that yields are less than they were previously, but it is believed that while fish varieties have been reduced, there has probably not been a reduction in biomass.

--- SENTENCE 156 ---
Fish poisoning and dynamiting have caused a reduction in the variety of fish stocks.

--- SENTENCE 157 ---
Dynamiting has caused considerable damage to reefs around Tutuila and in the Manu'a group (Swerdloff, 1973; Thomas in litt., 9.7.87); it is less widespread than previously, but in the past three years has been recorded several times in Fagatele and Fagasa Bays and other areas around Tutuila (Thomas in litt., 9.7.87).

--- SENTENCE 158 ---
Giant Clams (Tridacna spp) have effectively been fished out in Samoan waters, other than at Rose Atoll (Maragos in litt., 10.7.87).

--- SENTENCE 159 ---
Turtle numbers around Tutuila are said to have declined considerably in the five years up to 1981 (Johannes, 1986).

#### Legislation and Management

A general overview of conservation activities is given in Eaton (1985) and OTA (1987) provides an analysis of coastal resource development and management.

--- SENTENCE 160 ---
Ownership of the reefs and their resources was traditionally vested in the chiefs of each village and a complex system of taboos, restricting efforts to certain seasons and locations arose, which served to protect the reefs from over-exploitation.

--- SENTENCE 161 ---
These rights have been largely abandoned but some elements remain.

--- SENTENCE 162 ---
At present, village councils occasionally limit fishing on the reefs fronting the village through temporary bans on fishing or by prohibiting fishermen from other villages (Johannes, in press; Wass, 1983).

--- SENTENCE 163 ---
It is still customary for outsiders to request permission to fish these reefs (Maragos in litt., 10.7.87).

--- SENTENCE 164 ---
Several villages do not allow fishing on Sundays and most prohibit the use of dynamite or bleach (Johannes, in press; Wass, 1983).

--- SENTENCE 165 ---
Under Federal Public Law 93-435, the American Samoan Government owns all submerged lands from the mean highwater mark out to the limit of the territorial sea.

--- SENTENCE 166 ---
Executive Order 3-80, which established the American

Samoa Coastal Management Program (ASCMP) in 1980, contains 16 policies which govern the use of the coastal zone, including reef protection, marine resource protection, protection of unique areas, improvement of recreational opportunities and control of shoreline development (USDC, 1980).

--- SENTENCE 167 ---
A detailed description of legislation relevant to reefs is given in USDC (1984).

--- SENTENCE 168 ---
Many of the U.S. Federal laws and regulations apply, including the Marine Protection, Research and Sanctuaries Act (1972), the Marine Mammal Protection Act (1972), the Fishery Conservation and Management Act (1976), the Endangered Species Act (1973), the Coastal Zone Management Act (1972), the River and Harbor Act (1899), the Clean Water Act (1977) and the National Environmental Policy Act.

Pollution control is now greatly improved (Maragos, The Environmental Protection Agency is represented and is active in controlling effluent from the tuna canneries; stricter controls affecting the dumping of nitrogen- and phosphate-rich wastes into Pago Pago Bay have been imposed and will have to be complied with by 1991.

--- SENTENCE 170 ---
A sewerage project is in progress for Pala Lagoon which should improve water quality in the estuary.

--- SENTENCE 171 ---
The Office of Coastal Zone Management actively enforces U.S. Coast Guard oil pollution regulations and funds a project responsible for oil pollution control and cleaning up debris and oil spill in Pago Pago Harbor.

--- SENTENCE 172 ---
It runs an Island Wide Metal Cleanup programme and a Marine Awareness programme for students which stresses the importance of the sea to Samoa, and the damage done by pollution, siltation and other activities.

--- SENTENCE 173 ---

--- SENTENCE 174 ---
Seawalls are being rebuilt along eroded sections of coast (SPREP, 1980).

--- SENTENCE 175 ---

--- SENTENCE 177 ---

--- SENTENCE 179 ---

# FAGATELE BAY NATIONAL MARINE SANCTUARY

Geographical Location 12 km south-west of Pago Pago Harbour on the southernmost point of Tutuila, including the whole bay up to mean high high water; 14°23'S, 170°46'W.

Area, Depth, Altitude 66 ha; 26-36 m depth to 122 m altitude.

--- SENTENCE 350 ---
Land Tenure American Samoan Government.

--- SENTENCE 351 ---
Physical Features Fagatele Bay is formed by a collapsed volcanic crater and is surrounded by steep cliffs and volcanic rocks.

--- SENTENCE 352 ---
Seumalo Ridge rises over 400 ft (122 m) along the western and northern sides, and the eastern side is bounded by Matautuloa Ridge, over 200 ft (61 m)

high.

--- SENTENCE 353 ---
Soils on the steep slopes surrounding the bay are silty clay loams.

--- SENTENCE 354 ---
The beaches are composed primarily of calcareous sand with a small amount of volcanic sand.

--- SENTENCE 355 ---
The sand deposits extend off shore for about 20-30 ft (6-9 m) until they merge with the reef platforms which are composed primarily of consolidated limestone and encrusting algae.

--- SENTENCE 356 ---
The platforms, approximately 200 ft (61 m) or less wide and 2 ft (0.6 m) deep, have bottom reliefs of 1 ft (0.3 m).

--- SENTENCE 357 ---
They fringe the shore of the bay, the widest platform occurring on the eastern side.

--- SENTENCE 358 ---
The reef front drops almost vertically to 5-10 ft (1.5-3.0 m), then gradually slopes seaward to 15-20 ft (4.5-6.0 m).

--- SENTENCE 359 ---
The reef front slope may extend up to 300 ft (91 m) off shore, and contains widely separated pinnacles rising from depths of 15-20 ft (4.5-6.0 m) to within 4-5 ft (1.2-1.5 m) of the surface.

--- SENTENCE 360 ---
The bay bottom reaches a depth of 120 ft (36 m) approximately 1100 ft (335 m) due west of the pocket beach and is covered with rubble.

--- SENTENCE 361 ---
Wave action (normally from the east) is damped by the encircling reef platform and because the bay faces south-west.

--- SENTENCE 362 ---
Tides are probably similar to Pago Pago Bay where they are diurnal with mean and spring tidal ranges of 2.5 ft (0.76 m) and 3.1 ft (0.95 m) respectively.

--- SENTENCE 363 ---
Water temperatures is 80-82°F (26.7-27.8°C) with little seasonal or diurnal change; salinity ranges from 35.5 ppt to 36.0 ppt.

--- SENTENCE 364 ---
Visibility is normally at least 50 ft (15 m) (USDC, 1984).

--- SENTENCE 365 ---
Reef Structure and Corals A fairly well-developed fringing reef flat exists within the protected portion of the bay, the submerged part of which varies in depth from 0.5 to 2 m. There is a sparse covering of corals Pavona, Porites, Acropora, Pocillopora and Millepora.

--- SENTENCE 366 ---
Along the eastern edge of the bay, 10% of the reef flat at a depth of about 2 ft (0.6 m) is covered by coral and a further 5% has dead coral heads.

--- SENTENCE 367 ---
The most conspicuous corals are Pocillopora verrucosa, Favia sp., Galaxea sp., Goniastrea sp., A humilis, Porites lutea and the soft coral Palythoa sp.

--- SENTENCE 368 ---
Other species have been recorded in Leone Bay, just west of Fagatele Bay, and may be present in the latter.

--- SENTENCE 369 ---
The reef terrace varies from 2 to 10 m depth with strong currents and surge in some areas.

--- SENTENCE 370 ---
The substrate is basalt in exposed areas and calcium carbonate in sheltered Prior to Acanthaster infestation, there was areas.

--- SENTENCE 371 ---
30-100% coral cover of Acropora, Porites, Montipora, The reef front borders the Pocillopora and others.

--- SENTENCE 372 ---
seaward edge of the reef terrace and consists of a portion of the fore-reef, 5-40 m deep, that slopes steeply to deep Prior to Acanthaster infestation, the upper portions supported the most luxuriant and diverse coral assemblages in the bay.

--- SENTENCE 373 ---
Nearly vertical basalt cliffs and faces extend from the surface to as deep as 80 m along the exposed outer portions of the bay which are characterized by strong currents and surge in the upper portions.

--- SENTENCE 374 ---
Scattered corals are found on these walls including large fan corals at depths below 40 m.

About 172 coral species have been recorded from the area (USDC, 1984).

--- SENTENCE 375 ---
Noteworthy Fauna and Flora The steep cliffs have typical coastal and littoral vegetation.

--- SENTENCE 376 ---
There is an abundant avifauna and the fruit bats *Pteropus samoensis* and *P. tonganus* roost in the area (Amerson et al., 1982;

Dahl, 1980; Itano in litt., 29.7.87).

--- SENTENCE 377 ---
The Humpback Whale Megaptera novaeangliae is found in the bay and adjacent waters from July to October, the breeding and calving season.

--- SENTENCE 378 ---
Sperm Whales Physeter catodon are occasionally sighted off shore.

--- SENTENCE 379 ---
Other cetaceans, including the Pacific Bottlenose Tursiops truncatus Spinner Stenella sp.

--- SENTENCE 380 ---
Dolphins also use the bay and adjacent waters (USDC, 1984).

--- SENTENCE 381 ---
Hawksbills Eretmochelys imbricata and Green Turtles Chelonia mydas are found frequently, and Leatherbacks Dermochelys coriacea.

--- SENTENCE 382 ---
Olive Ridleys Le pidochelys olivacea and Loggerheads Caretta caretta have been recorded (USDC, 1984).

--- SENTENCE 383 ---
The bay's configuration provides a protected habitat for an abundant fish fauna.

--- SENTENCE 384 ---
Surveys of fish located on the reef flat and reef front indicate a high diversity and over 80 species of fish have been recorded.

--- SENTENCE 385 ---
114 species have been recorded from the waters off the south-eastern tip of the bay (Wass, 1978; USDC, 1984).

--- SENTENCE 386 ---
Scientific Importance and Research A brief survey of the flora and fauna was carried out in 1979 and more detailed studies of the fish fauna have been made but these have not been published.

--- SENTENCE 387 ---
A further survey to gather baseline population data, by the University of Guam under contract to the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration and the Development Planning and Tourism Office, Government of American Samoa, was completed in April 1985 (Birkeland et al., in press), and the reefs will be re-surveyed in 1988 (Thomas in litt., 9.7.87;

--- SENTENCE 388 ---
Itano in litt., 29.7.87).

--- SENTENCE 389 ---
Reference collections of the fish, corals, algae and invertebrates are housed at the Office of Marine and Wildlife Resources, Pago Pago, and at the University of Guam (Itano in litt., 29.7.87).

--- SENTENCE 390 ---
Research on coral recolonization and changes in the composition and structure of inshore fish communities is under way.

--- SENTENCE 391 ---
The pristine nature of the bay provides ideal conditions for the study of coral regeneration following Acanthaster predation.

--- SENTENCE 392 ---
Future research requirements are described in USDC (1984), and range from broad surveys to monitoring of Acanthaster.

--- SENTENCE 393 ---
Economic Value and Social Benefits The steep cliffs surrounding the bay make it relatively inaccessible from land, although the beaches can be reached via a foot trail.

--- SENTENCE 394 ---
The area is located near the village of Leone (1700 inhabitants), and there are three smaller villages in the immediate vicinity.

--- SENTENCE 395 ---
The sanctuary has a number of invertebrates which serve as important subsistence food sources, including sea anemones, lobsters, limpets, clams, octopuses and sea urchins.

--- SENTENCE 396 ---
Subsistence fishing and recreational activities are both important in the area (USDC, 1984).

--- SENTENCE 397 ---
Disturbance or Deficiencies In late 1978 and early 1979, hard corals were heavily predated by *Acanthaster*, 90% of the reef being destroyed; recent surveys indicate that coral cover is regenerating (Thomas, 1985).

--- SENTENCE 398 ---
Human impact has been minimal, and the bay is considered one of the least disturbed areas on Tutuila, although dynamiting has occurred during the last three years (Thomas in litt., 9.7.87).

--- SENTENCE 399 ---
Water quality is considered to be very high as there is no urban or industrial run-off, agricultural activities on the surrounding ridges are limited and there are no permanent streams discharging into the bay.

--- SENTENCE 400 ---
Increased visitor use of the area could

affect the reefs, although the regulations should limit this (USDC, 1984).

--- SENTENCE 401 ---
Legal Protection Fagatele Bay was declared a marine park in October 1982 by the governor of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 402 ---
This gave the Department of Parks and Recreation of the American Samoan Government the authority to enforce and promulgate laws to protect the resources to the 10 fathom (18.3 m) line as well as collect any fees.

--- SENTENCE 403 ---
Protection was enhanced by the designation of the area as a National Marine Sanctuary on 17.4.85 under Title 3 of the Marine Protection, Research and Sanctuaries Act, 1972 (Thomas, 1985 and in litt., 9.7.87) which finally came into force in July 1987 (Buckley in litt., 30.9.87).

--- SENTENCE 404 ---
The sanctuary regulations prohibit activities, such as dredging and discharge of pollutants, which would threaten the bay's resources.

--- SENTENCE 405 ---
Traditional uses such as subsistence fishing and recreation are permitted.

--- SENTENCE 406 ---
In the past, local customary rules restricted fishing by outsiders, especially for commercial purposes, and also reinforced government bans on the use of dynamite and chemical poisons (Eaton, 1985).

--- SENTENCE 407 ---
The Sanctuary Programs Division, Management National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), of the U.S. Federal Government is responsible programme overall administration and implementation within the sanctuary.

--- SENTENCE 408 ---
A management plan has been drawn up for the sanctuary (USDC, 1984) and a manager is to be appointed in the 1988 fiscal year (which begins on 1st October 1987), funding not having been available for this so far.

--- SENTENCE 409 ---
The American Samoan Office of Economic Development Planning is the on-site agency which will take the lead in coordinating with the manager for the day-to-day management of the It will coordinate its Coastal Zone sanctuary.

--- SENTENCE 410 ---
Management Program activities with the Department of Parks and Recreation where appropriate (Thomas 1985 and in litt., 9.7.87).

--- SENTENCE 411 ---
The Department of Parks and Recreation is responsible for providing a park ranger to assist the future manager with enforcement; a local ranger has been hired to police the area and is receiving training in Hawaii (Itano in litt., 29.7.87).

--- SENTENCE 412 ---
The area is divided into two zones: an outer zone in which subsistence fishing is permitted, and an inner core zone which is a strict reserve.

--- SENTENCE 413 ---
It is anticipated that most visitors will come by boat from Leone which would give the park authority greater control (Eaton, 1985).

--- SENTENCE 414 ---
Recommendations Recommendations are provided in the management plan and include a detailed research programme, provision of mooring buoys, improved access, and the development of an interpretive programme and centre.

--- SENTENCE 415 ---
It was originally recommended that Fagalua (Larsen's) Bay be included in the sanctuary, but after extensive review, both the Territorial and Federal governments decided against it (Thomas in litt., 9.7.87).

# GOAT ISLAND POINT - UTULEI REEF

Geographical Location Along the western side of the outer portion of Pago Pago Bay, Tutuila, from eastern edge of oil dock at Fagatogo, around Goat Island Point

(Rainmaker Hotel), past Utulei Village to Tulutulu (Blunt's)

--- SENTENCE 417 ---
Point; 170°41'W, 14°17'S.

Area, Depth, Altitude 1800 m of shoreline; 0-50 m depth to base of reef slope.

--- SENTENCE 418 ---
Physical Features The site originally proposed as a marine sanctuary encompasses the intertidal area, reef flat and associated dredged area (10-250 m, wide) and the steeply sloping reef front to a depth of 50 m (Anon., n.d. a).

--- SENTENCE 419 ---
Reef Structure and Corals The reefs fronting Goat Island Point and Utulei Village are fairly typical of semi-protected reefs in the region and have a fairly diverse fauna.

--- SENTENCE 420 ---
An extensive dredged area along a portion of the shoreline provides habitat for organisms preferring a silty sand bottom (Anon., n.d. a).

--- SENTENCE 421 ---
Noteworthy Fauna and Flora No information.

--- SENTENCE 422 ---
Scientific Importance and Research The area was originally studied between 1917 and 1920 by Mayor (1918, 1924a, b, c and d) and subsequently by Maragos (1972) who monitored the growth and survival of transplanted corals here.

--- SENTENCE 423 ---
It is the innermost remaining structural reef (not yet dredged or filled) on the southern side of Pago Pago Harbour (Maragos in litt., 10.7.87).

--- SENTENCE 424 ---
Economic Value and Social Benefits

--- SENTENCE 425 ---
The area is important for recreational and subsistence fisheries, particularly for villagers from Utulei.

--- SENTENCE 426 ---
Annual catches are estimated at 4524 kg fish and 3271 kg invertebrates (Wass, 1983).

--- SENTENCE 427 ---
It is the most popular area for recreational diving, snorkelling, boating and water sports.

--- SENTENCE 428 ---
The only two public beach parks on Tutuila and the Pago Pago Yacht Club lie within the proposed site, and the major hotel on the island, the Rainmaker, is adjacent.

--- SENTENCE 429 ---
There are plans for additional watersport activities in the area (Anon., n.d. a).

--- SENTENCE 430 ---
Disturbance or Deficiencies A variety of pollutants including organic and chemical wastes from fish canneries, oil from vessel traffic and fuelling operations, silt from construction projects and increased run-off, and sewage have adversely affected the habitat over the years.

--- SENTENCE 431 ---
A sewage outfall is located at the southern boundary of the site off Utulei (Anon., n.d. a).

--- SENTENCE 432 ---
Species diversity has decreased, hard coral coverage has been reduced, and turbidity has increased.

--- SENTENCE 433 ---
Maragos (1972) found that coral growth and survival in 1970-71 was suboptimal probably due to sedimentation and other water pollution.

--- SENTENCE 434 ---
The fuelling of large vessels and the offloading of petroleum products on the north-west boundary of the proposed site pose a serious threat should a major oil spill occur.

--- SENTENCE 435 ---
Legal Protection None at present.

--- SENTENCE 436 ---
Recommendations The area was originally considered by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration as a potential marine sanctuary; it was not, however, included on the Final Site Evaluation List and thus remains unprotected (Thomas in litt., 9.7.87).

--- SENTENCE 437 ---
Management measures included in the original proposal included protection of corals, fish and shells on the reefs

surrounding the hotel for the benefit of tourists and residents.

--- SENTENCE 438 ---
The residents of Utulei were to be allowed to continue subsistence fishing (Anon., n.d. a).

#### PAPALOLOA POINT

Geographical Location Ofu Island; southernmost tip, along the edge of the runway at Ofu airport, eastward to Asagatai Point; 169°40'W, 14°11'S.

Area, Depth, Altitude 0.75 sq. mi.

--- SENTENCE 439 ---
with 3 miles (4.8 km) of shoreline; depth to 45 m.

Physical Features Includes shoreline and adjacent fringing reef, 150-180 m wide.

--- SENTENCE 440 ---
Visibility is good in the nearshore depression (see below) but is reduced near the Point (AF and AECOS, 1980).

--- SENTENCE 441 ---
Reef Structure and Corals Most of the fringing reef is shallow (0.6-1.0 m at high tide) and consists of rubble and consolidated limestone.

--- SENTENCE 442 ---
The inner reef flat 275 m north-west of Papaloloa Point is relatively barren with low coral cover.

--- SENTENCE 443 ---
A depression lies just off shore here, about 1.5-2.5 m deep, which has numerous large microatolls of Heliopora coerulea, 3-5 m in diameter.

--- SENTENCE 444 ---
Other abundant corals are Porites lutea, Millepora spp., especially M. tortuosa, and large patches of Montipora spp.

--- SENTENCE 445 ---
Thick branched Acropora intermedia grows in thickets in the deeper, more seaward part of the depression.

--- SENTENCE 446 ---
It has been suggested that the microatolls may be the result of regrowth of corals following dredging for fill material for Ofu airport.

--- SENTENCE 447 ---
Small amounts of coral grow on the relatively flat outer reef platform (AF and AECOS, 1980).

--- SENTENCE 448 ---
Noteworthy Fauna and Flora

--- SENTENCE 449 ---
The reef fronting the eastern end of the airport has a very diverse fish fauna, over 70 species having been described.

--- SENTENCE 450 ---
Giant Clams *Tridacna* spp. are present, and the Hawksbill Turtle *Eretmochelys imbricata* has been recorded (Itano in litt., 29.7.87).

--- SENTENCE 451 ---
The area is adjacent to Vaoto Marsh.

--- SENTENCE 452 ---
Scientific Importance and Research The reefs at Papaloloa Point were surveyed by the Office of Marine and Wildlife Resources in 1986 and 1987 (Itano in litt., 29.7.87).

--- SENTENCE 453 ---
They are an excellent example of a fringing reef community with a diverse and abundant fauna.

--- SENTENCE 454 ---
Itano (in litt., 29.7.87) considers them to be unique in the country for their beauty and coral and fish diversity; Blue Coral Heliopora coerulea is present and is more abundant around Ofu than at any of the other sites at which it is known to occur in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 455 ---
Economic Value and Social Benefits

--- SENTENCE 456 ---
The area is important for recreational and subsistence fishing.

--- SENTENCE 457 ---
Diving, snorkelling and swimming are popular activities and there is considerable potential for increased recreational use.

--- SENTENCE 458 ---
A small hotel (6-8 beds capacity) has been built near Ofu airport which encourages visitors to enjoy the reef through non-destructive activities such as snorkelling and photography; it is mainly used by contract workers on construction projects (Itano in litt., 29.7.87; Thomas in litt., 9.7.87).

--- SENTENCE 459 ---
Disturbance or Deficiencies The area was not affected by hurricane damage from Hurricane Tusi, which struck American Samoa on 17th Jan. 1987 (Itano *in litt.

--- SENTENCE 460 ---
The site is fairly remote and is therefore unaffected by pollution or over-exploitation at present.

--- SENTENCE 461 ---
Recommendations Proposed for national marine sanctuary designation and is on the National Marine Site Evaluation List (NOAA, 1983).

--- SENTENCE 462 ---
Protection of the area is considered a high priority in the Action Strategy for Protected Areas in the South Pacific Region (Anon., 1985).

#### ROSE ATOLL NATIONAL WILDLIFE REFUGE

Geographical Location 241 km east-south-east of Pago Pago Harbour; 14°32'S, 168°08'W.

Area, Depth, Altitude Lagoon 2 km wide and 20 m deep; 640 ha reef and lagoon; Rose Island 5.18 ha, 3 m alt. and 1.0 km shoreline length; Sand Island 2.59 ha, 5.2 m alt. and 0.5 km shoreline.

--- SENTENCE 463 ---
The exterior boundary of the refuge is the extreme low waterline outside the perimeter reef except at the entrance channel where the boundary is a line extending between the extreme low waterlines on each side of the entrance channel (Wass, 1987).

--- SENTENCE 464 ---
Land Tenure U.S. Federal Government ownership.

--- SENTENCE 465 ---
Physical Features One of the smallest coral atolls in the world consisting of two low sandy islets, Rose and Sand, on a coralline algal reef enclosing a lagoon.

--- SENTENCE 466 ---
A single channel 6-50 ft (1.8-15 m) deep links the lagoon to the sea.

--- SENTENCE 467 ---
There is no freshwater.

--- SENTENCE 468 ---
Information on the atoll is given in Sachet (1954) and Mayor (1924e).

--- SENTENCE 469 ---
Reef Structure and Corals A brief description of some of the reef habitats of the lagoon is given in Wass (n.d. a).

--- SENTENCE 470 ---
25-50% of the area between the inner edge of the reef flat and the drop-off to the floor of the lagoon is covered by coral blocks, the larger of which are exposed at low tide.

--- SENTENCE 471 ---
The tops of the larger blocks are flattened and encrusted with coralline algae; their sides are profusely covered with a diverse assortment of hard and soft corals, and algae.

--- SENTENCE 472 ---
The remainder of the area consists of rubble flat, encrusted with coralline algae and a few scattered small colonies of branching Acropora.

--- SENTENCE 473 ---
The lagoon floor has an undulating sandy bottom at 12-20 m, much of which is covered with algae; there are occasional small colonies of Acropora.

--- SENTENCE 474 ---
Jutting up from the floor of the lagoon to its surface are several coral pinnacles with flat tops and very steep sides.

--- SENTENCE 475 ---
The flattened tops are encrusted with coralline algae, while hard and soft corals and algae are found on the often vertical or undercut

--- SENTENCE 477 ---
The irregular substrate is calcareous and compacted with coralline algae predominating.

--- SENTENCE 478 ---
Corals are abundant and diverse, but table and staghorn *Acropora* are lacking.

--- SENTENCE 479 ---
The outer reef was surveyed by the Office of Marine and Wildlife Resources and five monitoring sites were chosen in 1986 (Itano, 1987).

--- SENTENCE 480 ---
The results of these surveys are not available at present.

--- SENTENCE 481 ---
Noteworthy Fauna and Flora There is a *Pisonia* grove on Rose Island and some coconuts.

--- SENTENCE 482 ---
Vegetation is described by Setchell (1924) and Amerson *et al.* (1982) and is the least diverse of that on any island in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 483 ---
There are only seven plant species.

--- SENTENCE 484 ---
Sand Island has no vegetation.

--- SENTENCE 485 ---
Hawksbills Eretmochelys imbricata and Green Turtles Chelonia mydas nest on the atoll.

--- SENTENCE 486 ---
About 97% of the total seabird population of American Samoa is resident on the atoll, with about 312 000 birds of 20 species.

--- SENTENCE 487 ---
There are large nesting colonies of seabirds including Greater Fregata minor and Lesser F. ariel Frigatebirds, Red-footed Sula sula, Brown S. leucogaster and Blue-faced S. dactylatra Boobies, Red-tailed Tropicbirds Phaeton rubricauda, White Gygis alba and Sooty Sterna fuscata Terns, Brown Anous stolidus and Black A minutus Noddies, Reef Herons Egretta gularis and a variety of shore birds (Amerson et al., 1982).

--- SENTENCE 488 ---
Over 200 species of fish have been recorded.

--- SENTENCE 489 ---
The lagoon fish fauna is similar to that of the rest of the Samoan Islands although there is a lack of damselfish species and biomass within the lagoon, and relatively few herbivorous fish (Wass, n.d. a).

--- SENTENCE 490 ---
The abundance of carnivorous fish is high, possibly due to the lack of fishing pressure.

--- SENTENCE 491 ---
The fish fauna of the reef front has a low diversity compared with other reef fronts around Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 492 ---
Surveys in early 1987 indicated little change in the reef fish community over the past six or seven years.

--- SENTENCE 493 ---
The Giant Clam Tridacna maxima is present throughout the shallow areas of the lagoon; in 1980 about 10% of the population was dead (Wass, n.d. c).

--- SENTENCE 494 ---
Scientific Importance and Research The atoll is considered one of the most isolated and least disturbed in the world.

--- SENTENCE 495 ---
Annual resource surveys are carried out by U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) and American Samoan government personnel; these include surveys of both the outer reef and lagoon as well as of the terrestrial biota of Sand and Rose Islands.

--- SENTENCE 496 ---
Itano (in litt., 29.7.87) suggests that the benthic community of Rose Atoll may be unique in American Samoa because of the overwhelming presence of encrusting coralline algae; soft corals are also abundant whilst hard corals are poorly represented.

--- SENTENCE 497 ---
The Tridacna maxima (Giant Clam) population is being studied by USFWS to determine management strategies (Radtke, 1985;

--- SENTENCE 498 ---
Itano in litt., 29.7.87).

--- SENTENCE 499 ---
The atoll has considerable scientific and educational value and has been visited by school teachers from the American Samoan Department of Education (Anon., 1987a).

--- SENTENCE 500 ---
Economic Value and Social Benefits

--- SENTENCE 501 ---
The atoll has apparently never been inhabited (Swerdloff, 1973).

--- SENTENCE 502 ---
Prior to its establishment as a wildlife refuge, the Samoans fished Giant Clams on the atoll.

--- SENTENCE 503 ---
Disturbance or Deficiencies The islands are virtually undisturbed apart from the presence of a concrete marker, a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Refuge sign and a few introduced coconut trees

--- SENTENCE 504 ---
Cocos nucifera which are reportedly not thriving.

--- SENTENCE 505 ---
Introduced Polynesian rats Rattus exulans feed on bird eggs, and perhaps birds and their young and hatchling turtles; there is however, little direct evidence of this and the rats do not appear to constitute a major threat to the island's biota (Amerson et al., 1982).

--- SENTENCE 506 ---
Hurricane Tusi in 1987 does not appear to have had any adverse impact on the reefs (Itano, 1987).

--- SENTENCE 507 ---
Legal Protection Established as a National Wildlife Refuge on 5.7.73, primarily for the protection of turtles and seabirds.

--- SENTENCE 508 ---
Management Administered as a strict natural area by the Hawaiian Islands and Pacific Islands National Wildlife Refuge Complex, Honolulu, in cooperation with the American Samoan Government.

--- SENTENCE 509 ---
A permit is required for entrance and all activities within the refuge generally require the issue of a Special Use Permit; these are issued only for activities which are beneficial to fish and wildlife resources and management of the refuge.

--- SENTENCE 510 ---
Fishing is permitted within the refuge but the catch must be consumed on site or released; any Giant Clam fishing requires a special permit.

--- SENTENCE 511 ---
Birds and turtles may not be disturbed or harvested and no animals or plant material may be taken ashore.

--- SENTENCE 512 ---
The number of people allowed to camp overnight at once on the islands is strictly limited (six on Rose Island and two on Sand Island) and no camping on beaches is allowed during the Green Turtle nesting season (Wass, 1987).

--- SENTENCE 513 ---
A brochure has been produced for visitors (Anon., n.d. b).

--- SENTENCE 514 ---
Recommendations The island was proposed as an Island for Science under the IBP programme.

--- SENTENCE 515 ---
Wass (1987) outlines policy and recommendations for the use of Rose Atoll to increase environmental awareness and appreciation amongst Samoan teachers.

--- SENTENCE 516 ---
It was recommended by the Government that harvesting of Giant Clams be resumed (Wass, n.d. c).

--- SENTENCE 517 ---
However it has been stressed that the value of Rose Atoll as a refuge and study site for an undisturbed population of *Tridacna* considerably outweighs any benefits which may be derived from a commercial harvest of the clams (Itano in litt., 29.7.87; Maragos in litt., 10.7.87).

## **SWAINS ATOLL**

Geographical Location 270 km north of Samoa; geographically and floristically part of the Tokelau Islands, 160 km to the north-west; 11°03'S, 171°03'W.

Area, Depth, Altitude Island is 210 ha;

--- SENTENCE 519 ---
less than 6 m.

Land Tenure Privately owned by the Jennings family and a sovereign (flag) possession of the U.S.A.

Physical Features

--- SENTENCE 520 ---
The island is a ring-shaped atoll with a large, completely enclosed brackish water lagoon in the centre.

--- SENTENCE 521 ---
Rainfall is about 2500 mm/year (Whistler, 1983).

--- SENTENCE 522 ---
The island is described by Bryan (1974).

--- SENTENCE 523 ---
Reef Structure and Corals

--- SENTENCE 524 ---
The reefs were surveyed by Itano (1987 and in litt., 29.7.87).

--- SENTENCE 525 ---
Prior to devastating storm damage in February 1987, the reefs were apparently virtually pristine, with 80-100% live coral cover.

--- SENTENCE 526 ---
The lagoon is land-locked and very little sediment reaches the reefs, leading to underwater visibilites exceeding 150 ft (46 m).

--- SENTENCE 527 ---
The community is dominated by Pocillopora and Montipora with isolated massive colonies of Porites lutea.

--- SENTENCE 528 ---
Stylophora mordax is fairly common and Pavona, Psammocora and Acropora were noted but not common; an explanate Porites was very common on the outer reef slope from 15 to 40 m depth on the north-east coast but was not observed on the west coast.

--- SENTENCE 529 ---
Mille pora was present and Helio pora caerulea was not uncommon on the reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 530 ---
The north-east coast appeared to be the only area unaffected by the storm.

--- SENTENCE 531 ---
Noteworthy Fauna and Flora The flora and vegetation of the island are described by Whistler (1983); there is some scrub and forest but the rest of the island is cultivated.

--- SENTENCE 532 ---
Birds are described by Clapp (1968).

--- SENTENCE 533 ---
Hernandia forest, uncommon elsewhere in American Samoa, is found on the island.

--- SENTENCE 534 ---
Green Turtles Chelonia mydas reportedly used to nest; in 1982 it was stated that they had only been recorded off shore in recent years (Amerson et al., 1982).

--- SENTENCE 535 ---
Prior to storm damage (and still on the north-east coast), the fish community was dominated by large predatory fishes such as carangids, serranids, lutjanids and large barracuda, and dogtooth tuna (Gymnosarda unicolor).

--- SENTENCE 536 ---
Fishing pressure was evidently low with very large and old wrasse (Cheilinus undulatus) and giant grouper present, and abundant jack populations.

--- SENTENCE 537 ---
Pomacentrids were low in diversity and abundance with the exception of Chromis acares which was common (Itano in litt., 29.7.87).

--- SENTENCE 538 ---
Scientific Importance and Research

--- SENTENCE 539 ---
The reefs have been surveyed by the Office of Marine and Wildlife Resources of the American Samoan Government and may more closely resemble those of Tokelau than American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 540 ---
Prior to storm damage, they were considered among the most beautiful and productive in American Samoa (Itano, 1987).

--- SENTENCE 541 ---
Economic Value and Social Benefits Until recently there was an important copra industry on the island, employing several hundred Tokelau Islanders (Amerson et al., 1982).

--- SENTENCE 542 ---
Copra export has now apparently ceased and by 1987 the population had declined to 18-20 Samoans.

--- SENTENCE 543 ---
The storm of early 1987 destroyed all buildings at Taulaga, the only village on the island, and the inhabitants have moved to Tutuila to await the construction of pre-fabricated buildings which will be

taken to the island in September 1987 (Itano in litt., 29.7.87).

--- SENTENCE 544 ---
Landing is made on the west side of the island, at Taulaga (Whistler, 1983).

--- SENTENCE 545 ---
In the early 1980s the inhabitants were to some extent reliant on turtle eggs and meat for subsistence (Balazs, 1982).

--- SENTENCE 546 ---
Disturbance or Deficiencies The reefs suffered devastating damage as a result of a storm in early 1987.

--- SENTENCE 547 ---
Damage was most severe around the western half of the island with near total destruction of living corals down to 60 ft (18 m) in some areas.

--- SENTENCE 548 ---
At one transect site facing due west, at Taulaga, live coral cover down to 50 ft (15 m) had decreased from 95-100% (recorded in December 1986) to 7-12% (April 1987).

--- SENTENCE 549 ---
branching Pocillo pora colonies had been broken off near their bases, and flat Montipora colonies had also been broken or apparently killed by sedimentation.

--- SENTENCE 550 ---
Many of the deeper corals were being smothered by coral rubble generated further up the reef.

--- SENTENCE 551 ---
The north-west facing reef was believed likely to be particularly susceptible to damage as this area is sheltered from the prevailing winds (the south-east trades) and swell, allowing development of more delicate coral growth forms.

--- SENTENCE 552 ---
The zone facing south-east had apparently not been greatly affected.

--- SENTENCE 553 ---
This area had previously not had very high coral cover, the coral fauna being dominated by soft corals and low growing robust forms which could withstand the south-east tradewinds and associated swells.

--- SENTENCE 554 ---
The eastern part of the island was least affected and a transect off the north-east shore showed live coral cover of nearly 100% with virtually no coral breakage.

--- SENTENCE 555 ---
There appeared to have been a concommitant reduction in fish diversity, other than off the east side of the island, with a notable lack of large predatory fishes; many of the smaller fishes were apparently still present but at lower densities.

--- SENTENCE 556 ---
There is concern that the storm damage to the reefs may lead to a reduction in available food for the villagers in the future (Itano, 1987).

--- SENTENCE 557 ---
The coconut trees on the island are slowly being replaced by littoral forest species, chiefly *Hernandia* and *Pandanus*

--- SENTENCE 558 ---
(Amerson *et al.*, 1982).

--- SENTENCE 559 ---
The Polynesian Rat *Rattus exulans* is common on the island and feral pigs are found in the coconut plantations (Amerson *et al.*, 1982).

--- SENTENCE 560 ---
Recommendations Dahl (1980) recommended that the outer reefs and lagoon should be surveyed; this has now been carried out and recommendations for management should be drawn up.

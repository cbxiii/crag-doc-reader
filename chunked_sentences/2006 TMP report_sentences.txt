**Abstract**

The 11 core transect sites at 9 m depth on Tutuila were resurveyed in 2006.

--- SENTENCE 9 ---
The results are almost identical to those of 2005, and the benthic communities continue to appear to be healthy.

--- SENTENCE 10 ---
Crustose calcareous algae had the most benthic cover, followed by corals, followed in turn by turf algae, and brown macroalgae was essentially non-existant.

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
The cover of visible crustose calcareous algae (CCA) was higher on the north side than the south side, and turf algae was higher on the south side than on the north.

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
Coral cover did not differ between north and south sites.

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
Zonation on the north side is such that CCA and mixed corals dominates shallow water, and in deeper water mixed corals dominate.

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
On the south side, CCA and mixed corals dominate deeper than on the north, and below that zone, plate corals and *Halimeda* algae often dominate.

--- SENTENCE 15 ---
A few locations are dominated at shallow or medium depths by a single species of coral.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
The average amounts of different types of benthic cover was almost identical to that for the previous year; changes were tiny and due to random effects.

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
Several other studies have reported quantitative data on benthic cover on Tutuila in recent years, and all agree that CCA dominates the reefs, and coral cover is close to 28%, even though they differ in methods and sites.

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
Other studies also found the same differences between the north and south sides in algal cover.

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
The coral cover was slightly higher than has been reported by an SPC (Secretariat of the Pacific Community) PROCFISH study of reefs in several South Pacific countries.

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
It was also higher than that found in the South Pacific and the Indo-Pacific as a whole by a recently published study.

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
And it is vastly better than the less than 10% average cover now being reported from the Caribbean.

--- SENTENCE 22 ---
However, it is only about half the coral cover estimated before the 1978 crown-of-thorns starfish outbreak when they ate most of the coral on the island.

--- SENTENCE 23 ---
The cover level recorded then was higher than generally reported elsewhere, and there are several considerations that suggest that the estimates should be treated with caution.

--- SENTENCE 24 ---
The live coral index was much higher than reported by the SPC PROCFISH study for other South Pacific reefs, and very close to that reported last year by this program.

--- SENTENCE 25 ---
The corals in transects were dominated by encrusting corals, with columnar second.

--- SENTENCE 26 ---
The amount of the different coral lifeforms was almost identical to that last year.

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
The most common genus was *Montipora*, followed by *Pavona* and then *Porites*.

--- SENTENCE 28 ---
The number of genera per site was about 10 and was the same as last year.

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
was the most common species, followed by *Porites rus* and *Pavona varians*, in that order.

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
There were two additional sites this year, one of which was in an area where there were large areas of *Lobophyllia hemprichii* at medium depths.

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
When those sites are added, encrusting *Montipora* spp. is the most common species, followed by *Porites rus*, *Lobophyllia hemprichii*, and *Pavona varians*, in that order.

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
When the core 11 sites are compared with last year, the abundances of different species are unchanged.

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
Like other diverse ecosystems, most coral species are rare in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 34 ---
If the log of the abundances of species is plotted against the log of the order of

abundance of the species, the points fall near a straight line.

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
Several measures of species diversity, including number of species (S), species richness (Margalef d), Shannon-Wiener diversity (H'), Evenness (J), and Simpson's diversity index (D) all showed no change in diversity from last year.

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
Further, all of these indices were highly correlated with each other.

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
In the biodiversity search dives, a total of 147 species were found compared to 152 last year.

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
About 70 species were found per site, which is insignificantly greater than last year.

--- SENTENCE 39 ---
The number of species found at each site in the biodiversity dives did not correlate with the number of species found in the transects at the same sites.

--- SENTENCE 40 ---
*Porites rus* had the highest abundance rating, followed by encrusting *Montipora* spp.

--- SENTENCE 41 ---
and then *Pavona varians*.

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
*Porites rus* had a much higher abundance rating this year than last, for unknown reasons.

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
The abundance ratings for the different species correlated well with the ratings from last year, except for *Porites rus*.

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
The abundance ratings of the species correlates well with their prevalence (percent of sites in which the species was found).

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
Because most species have a single lifeform, the lifeforms of the most abundant species were determined, and from that the most abundant lifeform in the biodiversity dives was columnar, followed by encrusting, branching, and massive, in that order.

--- SENTENCE 46 ---
A variety of diversity measures were tested to see if they correlated with human population density, but no significant correlations were found.

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
Monitoring of bleaching in backreef pools continued, and the staghorn corals bleached this summer as in previous summers.

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
Extreme low tides early in the year continued to kill corals on the reef flats.

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
Coral disease is reported in a separate report.

#### **Invertebrates**

Very few diurnal, non-cryptic macroinvertebrates were recorded, in part because targeted searching was not used.

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
The most common species was a small burrowing urchin, followed by a small orange sponge and a thin grey encrusting sponge.

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
Other invertebrates were rare.

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
This was very similar to the results for 2005.

--- SENTENCE 53 ---

# **Methods**

The 11 core sites are shown in the map below.

!

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
The benthic methods were very similar to in 2005, with a couple small changes.

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
In the core monitoring, two 50-m tapes were laid on a depth contour between 8 and 10 m deep.

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
A space between them of about 15 m was kept.

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
Benthic categories were recorded under each 0.5 m point on the tape.

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
Benthic categories included live coral, dead coral, dead coral with algae, crustose calcareous algae, branching coralline algae, fleshy macroalgae, turf algae, rock, sand, rubble, soft coral, and sponge.

--- SENTENCE 61 ---
Corals were identified to lifeform, genus, and species when possible, and if the macroalgae was *Halimeda* that was recorded.

--- SENTENCE 62 ---
Soft corals were recorded to genus when possible.

--- SENTENCE 63 ---
Lifeforms included encrusting, massive, foliose, branching, columnar, submassive, mushroom, *Millepora*, *Acropora* branching, *Acropora* table, *Acropora* digitate, and *Acropora* encrusting.

--- SENTENCE 64 ---
Horizontal visibility was recorded using the tape on some transects.

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
Two tapes were done on the first dive, and an additional two tapes were done on the second dive.

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
Invertebrates were recorded on a return pass.

--- SENTENCE 67 ---
Sites were re-located using the GPS and markers as indicated in the 2005 report.

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
One day was required for each site.

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
The supplemental biodiversity monitoring was done by making a search from the bottom of the reef upward to as near to the crest as could be safely reached, recording all coral species found and estimates of abundances.

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
Abundances were estimated on the DAFOR scale, Dominant, Abundant, Frequent, Occasional, Rare.

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
The same 11 core sites on Tutuila as recorded in 2005 were repeated, which are shown in the map above.

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
Changes include the rugosity measurements which were omitted, because a third team member was not available and when included it lengthened dive times to the point where

running out of air was a distinct possibility, thus reducing the margin of safety.

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
Further, it appears that the measurement depends primarily on exactly where the chain falls, and that changes in rugosity caused by coral growth will take quite a few years before they would be detectable.

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
A hurricane could make changes in rugosity quickly by removing corals, and if significant hurricane damage occurs, the rugosity measurements can be repeated.

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
Until changes in coral cover or other rugosity changes are apparent, repeating the measurement of rugosity is not worth the increased risk of running out of air.

--- SENTENCE 76 ---
More care was taken to be sure to record dead corals which were covered with calcareous algae, instead of just recording the calcareous algae, in order to have an accurate measure of dead corals and the "live coral index."

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
Two sites were added, Fatumafuti as a practice site for which only 2 transects were done, and Amaua E due to the lack of a GPS unit and an inaccurate visual estimate of the location.

--- SENTENCE 78 ---
The original selection of sites was based on a desire for a rough balance between sites on the north and south, east and west, to include some good reef sites, and to monitor reefs not bare basalt substrates on points.

--- SENTENCE 79 ---
The approach was not a formal stratified random design.

--- SENTENCE 80 ---
The addition of Amaua E allows baseline data to be taken for a very different coral community than the other sites, dominated by a coral that does not dominate any of the other sites, that is, *Lobophyllia hemprichii*.

--- SENTENCE 81 ---
It is, however, common on the reef slope along much of the SE coast.

--- SENTENCE 82 ---
If something happened to this community, such as having that species heavily impacted by disease or bleaching or some other disturbance, and there was no baseline, we would not be able to monitor the change.

--- SENTENCE 83 ---
There is one disease that has only been seen in this species.

#### **Invertebrates**

Diurnal non-cryptic macroinvertebrates were recorded in a half-meter wide belt along each of the 50 m transect tapes used for the point-intercept benthic transects.

--- SENTENCE 89 ---
Only those invertebrates visible as the diver swam above the belt were recorded.

--- SENTENCE 90 ---
The recorder did not search under any ledges, rubble, or algae, nor did he search between the branches of corals such as *Pocillopora* that frequently harbor large numbers of commensals.

--- SENTENCE 91 ---
Hard and soft corals were not recorded as they were recorded in the point-intercept transects.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
The thin light grey sponge *Dysidea* sp. was recorded, although it had not been recorded in 2005.

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
Many if not most invertebrate species on reefs are nocturnal and/or cryptic, and specific searching strategies are necessary to find them, though some like sponges and tunicates are sessile and cannot hide during the daytime.

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
Night searches and targeted searches in specific niches would reveal many more invertebrates, but night diving is logistically much too difficult and dangerous in American Samoa, and time does not allow targeted search strategies.

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
Targeted searching between coral branches reveals large numbers of commensal crabs on *Pocillopora* colonies (e.g., Brainard, et al. 2007).

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
The results should not be interpreted as accurate representations of total invertebrate populations, which it is not, but rather a restricted but repeatable monitoring strategy.

# **Results**

 For background information on the coral reefs of American Samoa, see Wells (1988), Craig (2005), Craig et al. (2005), Sabater and Tofaeono (2006) and Whalen and Fenner (2006).

### Benthic Cover

 There were no major disturbances on the reefs of American Samoa since the previous collection of monitoring data in 2005.

--- SENTENCE 98 ---
The reef benthos on slopes continues to appear to be in good condition, and no changes were visually apparent.

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
Benthic data from transects for the 11 core sites plus two additional sites (Amaua E and Fatumafuti) that were added this year are presented in Fig.

--- SENTENCE 100 ---
1

 The mean for all sites is shown on the right hand bar.

--- SENTENCE 101 ---
Crustose calcareous algae was the most common type of benthic cover, with live coral second, and turf algae a distant third.

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
Turf is a better term than filamentous algae, since some of the turf is thin branching algae that is not actually filaments that are made of strings of single cells.

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
Fleshy macroalgae was a very minor part of the benthic cover.

--- SENTENCE 104 ---
This supports the view that the reef slopes of Tutuila are healthy, dominated by crustose calcareous algae and corals.

--- SENTENCE 105 ---
Crustose calcareous algae binds together loose rubble, making a firm substrate capable of supporting coral settlement and growth.

--- SENTENCE 106 ---
It also releases chemicals that act like flypaper to attract the larvae of corals to settle.

--- SENTENCE 107 ---
Calcareous algae covers new surfaces in American Samoa very quickly, stabilizes rubble, and attracts coral larvae to settle, making possible a succession from dead coral produced by disturbances through crustose calcareous algae to recovered coral communities.

--- SENTENCE 108 ---
The term "crustose calcareous algae" is broader than crustose coralline algae, because there are some crustose calcareous algae that are not actually coralline algae.

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
Further, the reader is reminded that only the top visible layer was recorded, and the nature of what was under the green macroalgae *Halimeda* was not recorded, nor what was under plate corals, overhangs, etc.

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
Coralline algae are common on the upper surfaces of such cryptic habitats, so the present data do not reflect its total abundance, and places where it was not recorded are not necessarily lacking in it.

--- SENTENCE 111 ---
Brown macroalgae have been identified as dominating surfaces on some reefs elsewhere which have lost all herbivores.

--- SENTENCE 112 ---
The brown macroalgae competes with corals, shading them, abrading them, and making it more difficult for coral larvae to settle and restore coral communities.

--- SENTENCE 113 ---
Most of the fleshy macroalgae on American Samoan reefs are *Halimeda*, a green calcarious alga which is slow growing and has not

been reported to cause problems.

--- SENTENCE 114 ---
Likely it is a natural part of the reefs in American Samoa and not a cause for concern.

--- SENTENCE 116 ---
Thus, the sites on the left in Figure 1, from Fagamalo to Aoa, are on the north side of Tutuila, and sites from Amaua to Leone are on the south side of Tutuila.

It appears from Figure 1 that there are differences between the north and south sides of the island.

--- SENTENCE 123 ---

--- SENTENCE 124 ---
The macroalgae difference is real as well.

--- SENTENCE 125 ---
The differences in algae are similar to that reported in the last annual report, for 2005 (Whalen and Fenner, 2006).

--- SENTENCE 126 ---
Turf algae in 2006 was more common in sites on the north side of Tutuila than the south side (t = 3.29, p < .022), and visible calcareous algae was more common on the south side than the north side (t = 3.84, p < .005).

--- SENTENCE 127 ---
Figure 4 shows this pattern in the same way it was shown in the 2005 report.

!

--- SENTENCE 128 ---
This pattern may be a result of the pattern of exposure to wave surge.

--- SENTENCE 129 ---
During about half the year, trade winds blow steadily out of the southeast, putting much more wave energy along the south coast than the north coast.

--- SENTENCE 130 ---
During the other half of the year, wind patterns are variable and usually light.

--- SENTENCE 131 ---
Hurricanes usually hit the north side harder, perhaps accounting for the more precipitous cliffs, and the restriction of reefs to bays, as they may remove most living coral from points.

--- SENTENCE 132 ---
A hurricane hits on the average about once in five years, so most years do not have a hurricane strike.

--- SENTENCE 133 ---
Thus, wave energy is greater on the average on the south than the north.

--- SENTENCE 134 ---
Coralline algae requires that its surfaces be clean of sediment and overgrowing algae.

--- SENTENCE 135 ---
Waves clean the coralline algae of sediment, encouraging its growth.

--- SENTENCE 136 ---
Casual observation reveals that on some north side slopes there is high visible crustose calcareous algae cover near the reef crest, but it decreases rapidly with depth and ends at a depth above the transect depth of about 8.5-9 m. On the south side, crustose calcareous algae appears to extend into deeper water than on the north, extending to about 12-15 m at some sites, and much deeper at others.

--- SENTENCE 137 ---
A graph illustrating these informal observations is presented in Figure 5.

--- SENTENCE 148 ---
Benthic cover in all categories changed by less than 3.6%, with coral cover decreasing by just 0.2%.

--- SENTENCE 149 ---
It should be kept in mind that transect locations are only relocated within a few meters at best, and even if they were relocated exactly and the tape placed at exactly the same location, wave surge moves the tape back and forth and points on the tape will not be at the same location as in previous years.

--- SENTENCE 150 ---
The variation in the location of the tape is well more than the size of most corals or other benthic items.

--- SENTENCE 151 ---
Thus, if anything it is surprising that the numbers for 2006 are so close to those for 2005.

--- SENTENCE 152 ---
None of the differences should be taken as evidence of any change at all, even the 3.6% difference is remarkably close to no difference.

--- SENTENCE 153 ---
In fact, the very small differences found supports the view that the average for all 11 sites for the whole island may be quite sensitive to changes, so that the program may be able to detect rather small changes in the average for the whole island.

--- SENTENCE 154 ---
The variation at any one site is larger because the sample is smaller, and the variation for any one of the four transects at a site would be greatest of all.

--- SENTENCE 155 ---
Thus, the program should be able to detect rather small changes that occur all around the island, but would require larger changes at an individual site to be able to reliably detect it.

--- SENTENCE 156 ---
Many management concerns are likely to be questions of whether the reefs as a whole are improving or deteriorating, and the question of whether there are highly localized changes could be much less important.

--- SENTENCE 157 ---
Of course, at a small enough scale, we know that the community is naturally dynamic, with changes on a small scale that are frequent and large.

--- SENTENCE 158 ---
But that is not a concern for management, while an overall deterioration of the reefs of Tutuila would certainly be.

--- SENTENCE 159 ---
No deterioration of the reefs of American Samoa was detected by this monitoring program from 2005 to 2006.

--- SENTENCE 160 ---
The coral cover is very close to the 27% cover found for 24 different sites by Sabater and Tafaeo (2006).

--- SENTENCE 161 ---
The mean of five recent studies for coral cover was 28% as shown in

Figure 8.

--- SENTENCE 162 ---
Most studies agree that coralline or calcareous algae cover is high, with the average of five studies being 41% (Figure 8).

--- SENTENCE 163 ---
All studies also agree that turf or filamentous algal cover is less than coral and coralline or calcareous algae.

--- SENTENCE 164 ---
Each study collected data from different sites, so any agreement indicates that these are general features of Tutuila reefs.

!

--- SENTENCE 165 ---
An older study, Green (2002), reported an average of 45% coral cover, which is higher than more recent studies have reported.

--- SENTENCE 166 ---
The same author reported much lower coral cover of only about 18% in 1996 (Green, 2002).

--- SENTENCE 167 ---
Figure 9 presents long term monitoring data from Tutuila reef slopes from a variety of studies.

--- SENTENCE 168 ---
This graph builds on a graph presented in Craig et al. (2005).

--- SENTENCE 169 ---
The two points from this report and Waylen and Fenner (2006) have been added on the right, and a point added on the left from an unpublished study by Wass (1982).

--- SENTENCE 170 ---
The Wass study just reported estimates of coral cover for several areas, and the point was computed as a mean of those estimates.

--- SENTENCE 171 ---
It appears to be the only existing data that reflects coral cover before the crown-of-thorns outbreak in the late 1970's which was reported to have eaten most of the live coral.

--- SENTENCE 172 ---
The large drop in coral cover that followed is very likely due to that outbreak.

--- SENTENCE 173 ---
The swings in coral cover since that time are likely due to a series of hurricanes and mass bleaching events, with recovery between events.

--- SENTENCE 174 ---
Long term studies often show that reefs cycle between natural destructive disturbances and recovery (e.g., Connell et al. 2004).

--- SENTENCE 175 ---
The term "resilience" refers to a reef's ability to recover after such disturbances.

--- SENTENCE 176 ---
Reefs that receive chronic destructive human impacts are likely to have reduced resilience in the face of natural disturbances.

--- SENTENCE 177 ---
At this point, it appears that the benthic communities of the reefs of American Samoa have good resilience.

--- SENTENCE 178 ---
Clear oceanic water, strong wave surge, high coralline/calcareous algae cover, little fleshy macroalgae, and a fish community dominated by detritivorous and herbivorous fish, may all contribute to that resilience.

!

--- SENTENCE 179 ---
The comments made about the coral cover in the 2005 report (Whalen and Fenner, 2006) continue to hold.

--- SENTENCE 180 ---
Although live coral cover is not exceptionally high, it is higher than reported for reef slopes by the PROCFISH program of the Secretariat of the South Pacific (SPC) for several Pacific Island countries.

--- SENTENCE 181 ---
It is also higher than a recent study (Bruno and Selig, 2007) reported for the South Pacific, and for the entire Pacific, and much higher than the current average coral cover for the Caribbean (Gardiner, et al 2003), as can be seen in Figure 10.

--- SENTENCE 182 ---
Mean coral cover on reefs in the Great Barrier Reef are now about 25% (Bellwood, et al, 2004), so coral cover here is higher than there as well.

--- SENTENCE 183 ---
It should be borne in mind that coral cover varies greatly between different reefs on the GBR, as well as over time for some reefs.

!

--- SENTENCE 184 ---
10.

 PROCFISH has also reported a live coral index, produced by dividing the live coral cover by all coral cover (alive and dead).

--- SENTENCE 185 ---
As reported in the 2005 report, the live coral index here is much higher than PROCFISH reported for other Pacific islands.

--- SENTENCE 186 ---
In 2006, extra care was taken to be sure that all standing dead coral was correctly recorded in transects, even if they are covered with other organisms that would otherwise be recorded in other categories, such as crustose calcareous algae or turf algae.

--- SENTENCE 187 ---
The result was a very slight decrease in the live coral index, which probably more accurately represents the true situation.

--- SENTENCE 188 ---
The live coral cover here remains much higher than reported by PROCFISH for other Pacific islands, and it is always long dead coral covered with algae, not newly killed corals (which are white or light colored and not covered with other organisms which have settled).

--- SENTENCE 189 ---
If in the future something kills significant amounts of coral, this index, shown in Figure 11, will pick it up.

!

--- SENTENCE 190 ---
The percentage cover of crustose calcareous algae recorded increased very slightly from 2005 to 2006 in this study.

--- SENTENCE 191 ---
G. Aeby found in benthic data collected by the coral disease program and by Birkeland et al. in 1987 that crustose calcareous algae decreased steadily from 1987 to 2006.

--- SENTENCE 192 ---
Further, in the coral disease program data, crustose calcareous algae cover decreased at each of seven sites, so the overall decrease is not due to monitoring different sites in different years.

--- SENTENCE 193 ---
The CRED program also recorded a decrease in calcareous algae from 2004 to 2006.

--- SENTENCE 194 ---
The cause of the discrepancy between those studies and the present study is not clear.

--- SENTENCE 195 ---
A decrease in crustose calcareous algae could be a cause for concern, since crustose calcareous algae promote the recruitment of corals and is probably a contributor to reef resilience.

--- SENTENCE 196 ---
However, if it was replaced by coral it might not be a concern.

--- SENTENCE 197 ---
Any change in crustose calcareous algae cover is not visually obvious, it appears to remain very abundant in many locations, particularly near the reef crest.

--- SENTENCE 198 ---
The present coral cover in American Samoa is slightly better than the average for other locations in the South Pacific, better than the average for the Pacific, and much

better than the average for the Indo-Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 199 ---

It was part of the original intention of the design of the monitoring program to supplement the transect data with the biodiversity data, knowing that the biodiversity dives would cover a larger area and a much wider depth range, but produce less quantitative abundance measures.

--- SENTENCE 407 ---
The biodiversity dives are done because they provide additional information that compliments or supplements the transect information, not because they measure the same thing.

--- SENTENCE 408 ---
The biodiversity dives sample a much larger area, giving data on a larger part of the reef, and with the larger sample are able to provide information about species too rare to be recorded in the transects, which provide a smaller sample of coral species and thus tend to include only the more common species.

--- SENTENCE 409 ---
The biodiversity dive data provides greater area and taxonomic coverage for corals, but less quantitative accuracy.

--- SENTENCE 410 ---
In addition, observations during the biodiversity dives can detect major changes that affect the reef at depths other than the 9m depth where transects are done.

# Non-Point Pollution Impact

 Peter Hoek has led a program with the American Samoa Environmental Protection Agency (ASEPA) to develop biocriteria on coral reefs to detect the effects of humans on coral reef communities.

--- SENTENCE 412 ---
In our report on our 2005 data, we reported that we did not find significant correlations between coral cover at our 11 core sites and the human impact ratings which EPA had designated for different areas of the islands (Whaylen and Fenner, 2005).

--- SENTENCE 413 ---
In the 2006 data, the percentage of coral cover also did not show a significant correlation with impact ratings, r = .3969, p > .1.

--- SENTENCE 414 ---
This is shown in Figure 64.

!

--- SENTENCE 415 ---
Turf algae also did not show a correlation with human impact, r = .2649, p > .1, and there was no correlation with algae cover, r = .0714, p > .1.

--- SENTENCE 416 ---
All three decreased with increasing impact.

--- SENTENCE 417 ---
The hypothesis that human impact was damaging the reefs would predict decreasing coral cover with increasing impact, but increasing turf or macro algae with impact.

--- SENTENCE 418 ---
The first fits the hypothesis, but the other two do not.

--- SENTENCE 419 ---
Houk et al. (2005) did not find evidence of human impact on coral cover, but did report evidence of impact on diversity measures.

--- SENTENCE 420 ---
In the present study, correlations were run with each of four diversity measures for the transect data and two for the biodiversity data, with the impact ratings.

--- SENTENCE 421 ---
None the correlations were even close to being significant.

--- SENTENCE 425 ---
In their study, all their north side sites were constructional, and all but one of their south side sites were framework.

--- SENTENCE 426 ---
So for this study, north side sites were considered constructional except for Vatia, and south side sites were considered framework.

### **Bleaching**

--- SENTENCE 454 ---
Although 2006 was just the second year of collecting reef slope monitoring data, it was the third year of collecting coral bleaching data in the back reef pools.

--- SENTENCE 455 ---
Bleaching was recorded in two pools, one at the airport and one at Alofau.

--- SENTENCE 456 ---
These pools were dredged in the reef flat to provide material for building the platform for the airport runway, and for extending the land for the village of Alofau.

--- SENTENCE 457 ---
Bleaching was recorded only among staghorns, *Acropora muricata*

--- SENTENCE 458 ---
(= *A. formosa*), *A. nobilis* and *A. pulchra*.

--- SENTENCE 459 ---
Both pools are co-dominated by *Acropora muricata* and *Porites cylindrica*, but the latter has not been observed to bleach.

--- SENTENCE 460 ---
Bleaching was recorded by visual estimation of the percent bleached during a one-hour swim.

--- SENTENCE 461 ---
For more background information on bleaching in American Samoa, see Whalen and Fenner (2006).

--- SENTENCE 462 ---
Staghorns in the two backreef pools bleached again beginning late October 2005 at Alofau and mid-November at the airport, and extending to early June 2006 at both sites (Figure 62, 63).

--- SENTENCE 463 ---
A three-week period in February, 2006 of unusually heavy cloud cover and rain resulted in a marked decrease in bleaching of staghorns at the airport.

--- SENTENCE 464 ---
Following the end of the cloudy rainy period, bleaching returned at the airport (Figure 65).

--- SENTENCE 465 ---
Bleaching was not interrupted by the cloudy period at Alofau (Figure 66).

--- SENTENCE 466 ---
Bleaching returned in October at Alofau, and in December at the airport.

--- SENTENCE 467 ---
Bleaching was intense at Alofau by the end of the year (Figure 66), but had just begun at the airport (Figure 65).

--- SENTENCE 468 ---
Bleaching at Alofau is more intense in the deeper pool, and less intense in the shallower areas, for unknown reasons (Figure 66).

--- SENTENCE 469 ---
At times, the water is warmer near the surface than deeper, suggesting bleaching should be greater in shallow water.

--- SENTENCE 470 ---
Similarly, in the airport bleaching is generally more intense in the outer part of the pool and less in the inner area closer to the runway.

--- SENTENCE 471 ---
These areas at the airport do not differ noticeably in depth.

!

--- SENTENCE 472 ---
At the time of writing in 2007, staghorn bleaching in the pools is tapering off.

--- SENTENCE 473 ---
Late 2006 and early 2007 is the fourth summer in a row that this program has documented staghorn bleaching in these pools.

--- SENTENCE 474 ---
Further, bleaching on reef slopes was reported for the previous two summers, so staghorn in the pools almost certainly bleached then as well.

--- SENTENCE 475 ---
Thus, they have bleached for at least 6 years in a row, quite possibly more.

--- SENTENCE 476 ---
In Alofau,

they are spending less time recovered than bleached.

--- SENTENCE 477 ---
Bleaching on reef slopes during this period has been very light and scattered.

--- SENTENCE 478 ---
Bleaching very likely slows or stops growth, and has been reported to block sexual reproduction for a year, likely due to the loss of energy reserves.

--- SENTENCE 479 ---
Thus, these corals are likely growing less and not reproducing at all (other than asexually by fragmentation).

--- SENTENCE 480 ---
Bleaching is very likely to having a chronic negative impact on these coral populations.

--- SENTENCE 481 ---
Mass coral bleaching has been predicted to become an annual summer event in coming decades due to global warming, and these staghorns appear to be the first multi-species population in the world exhibiting annual summer bleaching (*Oculina patigonica* in Israel and *Favia fragum* in Florida exhibit annual summer and fall bleaching, respectively, but only of the one species at a location).

--- SENTENCE 482 ---
Further, after at least six consecutive summers of bleaching, there is no sign of any increase in resistance to bleaching.

--- SENTENCE 483 ---
The "adaptive bleaching hypothesis" says that bleaching occurs so corals can acquire new more resistant strains of zooxanthellae.

--- SENTENCE 484 ---
These records give no indication that these corals have acquired more resistant zooxanthellae.

#### **Major Disturbances**

 There were no hurricanes, mass bleaching events, disease outbreaks, or crown-ofthorns starfish outbreaks in 2006.

--- SENTENCE 485 ---
Extreme low tides continued in one week of each month early in the year.

--- SENTENCE 486 ---
Additional reef flat coral mortality appears to have been caused, primarily in the bushy staghorn, *Acropora aspera*.

--- SENTENCE 487 ---
Most patches of this species are now completely dead, and some of the branches have broken and are now rubble.

--- SENTENCE 488 ---
However, in places where it grows in depressions in the reef flat, it appears healthy.

--- SENTENCE 489 ---
Live *A. aspera* remain in Onososopo, Alofau, Matu'u, Faga'itua, and other locations.

--- SENTENCE 490 ---
In addition, lumps of crustose calcareous algae on reef flats were observed to have been killed.

--- SENTENCE 491 ---
Normally, unless repeated exposure occurs, calcareous algae recovers or resettles quickly.

--- SENTENCE 492 ---
Quantitative measures of reef flats were not taken in 2006, but have already been taken in 2007 at the time of writing, and will be incorporated into the report for 2007.

--- SENTENCE 493 ---
Corals were checked in Faga'alu Bay after the end of the 3 week period of monsoonal rains in late February and early March, 2006.

--- SENTENCE 494 ---
This bay is frequently seen after rainstorms with a very large sediment plume in the waters of the bay, usually on the ava side where water flows outward.

--- SENTENCE 495 ---
The stream that feeds the bay carries large quantities of fine silt following rain storms.

--- SENTENCE 496 ---
Some staghorn corals were recently dead, still white.

--- SENTENCE 497 ---
Observation revealed that the dead staghorn was in water at least 10 feet deep, and staghorn near the surface was healthy.

--- SENTENCE 498 ---
Depressions in the upper surfaces of massive corals had up to about 1 cm very fine silt accumulated, which had killed the coral tissue underneath it.

--- SENTENCE 499 ---
A visual estimate was that about 40% of the living staghorns may have been killed, though the highly patchy nature of the coral and the deaths made estimation difficult; later estimates were lower.

--- SENTENCE 500 ---
All backreef pools were checked, and there were small amounts of dead staghorn in the airport and Nu'uuli pools, and no dead staghorns in any other pools.

--- SENTENCE 501 ---
The explanation that best fit the observations was that the combination of heavy cloud cover and thick suspended silt in the water reduced light so much that deeper corals were killed.

#### **Coral Taxonomy**

 Progress has been slow on this topic, as it has been considered to be of lower priority than collecting monitoring data.

--- SENTENCE 502 ---
Monitoring data must be collected on a timely basis, while coral taxonomy work can be done any time.

--- SENTENCE 503 ---
See Whalen and Fenner (2006) for a discussion of how many coral species may be in the territory, and the number of new coral sightings.

--- SENTENCE 504 ---
During 2006, about half of the DMWR coral collection has been examined to confirm species identifications.

--- SENTENCE 505 ---
All known literature giving lists of coral species in the territory have now been summarized in a single table.

--- SENTENCE 506 ---
The results of 13 reports are included, including from one (Coles et al. 2003) that summarizes the results of 16 other studies.

--- SENTENCE 507 ---
Currently, about 436 names have been applied to corals in American Samoa, about 365 of which are currently valid names.

--- SENTENCE 508 ---
This study has observed about 250 species, some of which have not previously been reported.

--- SENTENCE 509 ---
Some of the 365 valid names have probably been applied incorrectly, but since only three studies (Hoffmeister, 1925; Lamberts, 1983; and an unpublished study by Richard Randall) have collected reference collections, there is no way to determine whether many of those 365 names were applied correctly, except when the species can be found and collected currently to verify species.

--- SENTENCE 510 ---
Once all corals in the DMWR collection are identified, all species that have been observed which are not in the collection will need to be collected to verify those species.

--- SENTENCE 511 ---
That has not yet begun but hopefully will begin in 2007 or perhaps 2008.

### **Recommendations for the Future**

 Reef flats are exposed to very different stresses and disturbances than reef slopes, and have very different biological communities.

--- SENTENCE 523 ---
The results of monitoring slopes cannot be extrapolated to the reef flats.

--- SENTENCE 524 ---
For instance, extreme low tides have killed much of the coral on the reef flats but had no effects on the reef slopes.

--- SENTENCE 525 ---
Further, terrestrial influences such as sediment runoff and gleaning are much greater on the reef flats than the slopes.

--- SENTENCE 526 ---
Reef flats are just as much a part of the reefs as the slopes.

--- SENTENCE 527 ---
Reef flat monitoring is urgently needed.

--- SENTENCE 528 ---
At the time of writing, reef flat monitoring data collection has almost been completed for 2007, so it will be part of the 2007 data and report.

--- SENTENCE 529 ---
Reef flats in the harbor are a special case.

--- SENTENCE 530 ---
Reefs in the harbor have been damaged over the years, by dredging and probably by cannery effluent and other runoff.

--- SENTENCE 531 ---
There is a need to survey harbor reefs to assess damage and have a baseline to monitor recovery or further damage.

--- SENTENCE 532 ---
Baseline data has already been taken for 2007 at the time of writing of this report.

--- SENTENCE 533 ---
Reef slopes in American Samoa show zonation.

--- SENTENCE 534 ---
Slope communities at some sites vary strongly with depth.

--- SENTENCE 535 ---
Some disturbances can have differential effects on different depths on the slopes as well, so for instance hurricanes commonly damage shallow areas more than deep areas, and bleaching may affect one depth more than another.

--- SENTENCE 536 ---
Because there is zonation of the communities and disturbances can affect different depths to different degrees, the results at 9m depth cannot be fully extrapolated to other depths.

--- SENTENCE 537 ---
It appears that a transect laid at a shallower depth, and a transect laid at a deeper depth may reveal more about zonation and be able to detect changes that are not detected at the 9 m depth.

--- SENTENCE 538 ---
At the time of writing, recording of transects at 4.5m and 18 m depth for 2007 have begun.

--- SENTENCE 539 ---
Some sites have unique communities or dominant corals.

--- SENTENCE 540 ---
Some disturbance events such as disease, hurricanes, crown-of-thorns or bleaching, affect different species to very different degrees.

--- SENTENCE 541 ---
For instance, the two most common coral diseases in American Samoa, white syndrome and growth anomaly, are much more common on *Acropora* than other genera, and more common on table *Acropora* than other *Acropora*.

--- SENTENCE 542 ---
Hurricanes usually damage delicate branching and foliose species far more than massive species.

--- SENTENCE 543 ---
*Acropora* and *Millepora* are more sensitive to bleaching than other corals.

--- SENTENCE 544 ---
Insofar as it is possible, it would be good to monitor these unique communities, in case a disturbance damages one of them while leaving other communities unscathed.

--- SENTENCE 545 ---
This year the site at Amaua E. was added with the dominant coral *Lobophyllia hemprichii*.

--- SENTENCE 546 ---
In the future, a site at the west side of Fagatelle Bay should be added that would include the dominant stand of *Acropora hyacinthus*, and a site at the mouth of Vatia Bay should be added where there is a diverse community of *Acropora* tables and staghorns.

--- SENTENCE 547 ---
*Acropora* is particularly sensitive to bleaching, hurricanes, disease, and crown-of-thorns, and this community may be unusual in American Samoa, particularly at this relatively shallow depth.

--- SENTENCE 548 ---
There should be a renewed effort to expand the monitoring program to the Manu'a Islands of Ofu-Olosega and Ta'u.

--- SENTENCE 549 ---
This has not been possible so far due to the late hiring of the second team member (B. Carroll) in 2006, and the difficulty of logistics in this remote location.

--- SENTENCE 550 ---
However, if a larger vessel is acquired, this would greatly facilitate beginning monitoring in the Manu'a islands.

--- SENTENCE 551 ---
Bleaching should be recorded on reef flats and reef slopes as well as in the backreef pools, to verify that there is little bleaching there concomitant with the bleaching in the pools.

--- SENTENCE 552 ---
Such recording has begun at the beginning of 2007 at Alofau.

--- SENTENCE 553 ---
It would also be good to record the growth of staghorn to see if they grow less when bleached than when not bleached.

--- SENTENCE 554 ---
Tags to do this have been attached at the time of this writing.

# <span id="page-7-0"></span>**INTRODUCTION**

# <span id="page-7-1"></span>**History of Aquaculture in the Pacific Region**

Since 1950, th[e](https://aquaculture.spc.int/)

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
[Secretariat of the Pacific Community](https://aquaculture.spc.int/) has studied and attempted to develop several aquaculture programs throughout the Pacific region with varying degrees of success.

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
The majority of these operations were developed on land (using fresh water).

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
However some included nearshore operations in saltwater lagoons and include hatchery production operations to help develop "seed" stock, such as the giant clam.

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
In [American Samoa,](https://aquaculture.spc.int/index.php?option=com_countries&view=country&id=1&Itemid=4) baitfish, tilapia and giant clam aquaculture programs were developed, but only tilapia is currently grown in one facility on land.

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
The United States Territory of American Samoa is an isolated archipelago in the South Pacific located at 14 degrees south latitude, approximately 5,000 miles south of Hawaii (Figure 1), with a limited economic base that relies predominantly on tuna canning (StarKist) and the territorial and federal governments for employment.

!

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
American Samoa's location in the South Pacific Ocean, with increasing resolution to the Territory, then to the major islands of the territory.

--- SENTENCE 54 ---
*

American Samoa's territorial [Ocean Plan](https://pacificislandsrpb.org/american-samoa/)[1](#page-7-2) (December 2018) describes mariculture as potentially important to the Territory's food and economic security.

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
The finalization of the Ocean Plan, a three-year undertaking by territorial staff, federal representatives, and non-profit organizations, provides a solid, verifiable foundation and justification for the development of a mariculture feasibility study: the plan highlights the spatial planning of aquaculture siting as a necessary component of economic development.

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
Similarly, other Pacific Islands, such as the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands (CNMI), have successfully assessed the feasibility of mariculture with respect to positive impact to local economies; achieve the parallel goal of minimizing impact to native species; and identify, evaluate, and prioritize sitting and socioeconomic opportunities and

The American Samoa Ocean Plan can be found on the AS Port Administration's website at: [https://portadministration.as.gov/images/documents/AS-Ocean-Plan-FNL-Interactive\\_9MB.pdf.](https://portadministration.as.gov/images/documents/AS-Ocean-Plan-FNL-Interactive_9MB.pdf)

challenges (Northern Marianas College 2011).

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
In addition, Western Samoa developed a [Aquaculture Management and Development Plan](https://fame-archive.spc.int/doc/part13/Samoa-Aquaculture-Plan-23052013.pdf) (2013-2018) to set "out a clear guide for the future expansion of the aquaculture sector in Samoa, and a comprehensive pathway for the Fisheries Division to ensure the long-term sustainability of the activity."

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
Finally, the SPC provided a [framework for developing aquaculture in the Pacific Region](https://brusselsbriefings.files.wordpress.com/2013/07/study-on-the-potential-of-aquaculture-in-the-pacific.pdf) in 2014 to guide further discussion and potential development.

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
This information can help identify previous successes and failures to help inform decision-making for interested parties in developing aquaculture or mariculture in American Samoa.

# <span id="page-8-0"></span>**Current Status of Aquaculture in American Samoa**

There currently are no active mariculture or aquaculture ventures in American Samoa besides a few "backyard" tilapia aquaculture pens.

--- SENTENCE 62 ---
Various mariculture ventures have been attempted in American Samoa, with some limited positive results.

--- SENTENCE 63 ---
However, these were established in an adhoc way with limited information and little to no community engagement.

--- SENTENCE 64 ---
This feasibility study will therefore assess the receptiveness of the social climate in American Samoa at the village level.

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
Success must include increasing the capacity of interested villages to provide enforcement and monitoring of mariculture sites (local village enforcement is part of the culture of Samoa, with the community-owned land managed by the village chiefs).

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
At the village level, the villages provide their own enforcement of their nearshore waters because those are the waters that provide sustenance for their families.

--- SENTENCE 67 ---
The chief includes the village in decisions about their waters, but is the ultimate decision maker.

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
Thus, engaging with village chiefs and their villages, or "community stakeholders," we can ensure that the fishing communities are engaged and participating in the feedback about siting and types of mariculture.

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
Significant challenges to mariculture exist in American Samoa, including but not limited to: time and complexity of obtaining permits; limited land availability; acquiring seed species; building and facilities start-up costs; infrequent shipping schedules and availability of supplies on island.

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
Biological constraints include ensuring the species selected will thrive in the tropical climate of American Samoa - whether it is an on-land small tank tilapia pen, recirculating aquaculture facility that requires pumping seawater from depths so colder water species can live, or determining the best sites for coastal or open ocean mariculture based on biophysical characteristics of the area and the species.

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
Overlaying these factors are basic questions regarding the sociocultural acceptance of mariculture in general and in its specific forms, as well as community concerns about the distribution of benefits and ensuring security for mariculture ventures.

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
Basic requirements such as readily available ice and refrigeration, outlets to sell products locally, and facilities for processing products are further potential barriers to entry.

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
Additional important considerations include the costs associated with and availability/ accessibility of export distribution channels to US and international markets.

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
For example, Tahitian pearl mariculture may require extensive shipping support, from ice to product processing depending on if the entire oyster or just the pearl is being shipped (which would also require security).

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
However, a locallyfocused seaweed mariculture venture may require few low cost supplies to sustain the product long enough to reach target markets.

--- SENTENCE 76 ---
Some fish may require processing prior to distribution across Tutuila or export to Independent Samoa, New Zealand, or Japan (examples), or may need minimal ice/refrigeration to be shipped from the Manu`a Islands to Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
# <span id="page-9-0"></span>**Need for Aquaculture Feasibility Study**

Mariculture and aquaculture have been identified by local planners in both the American Samoa Comprehensive Economic Strategy (ASCES) and the American Samoa Ocean Plan as one prong of economic development and as a way to increase resilience and food security of the Territory.

--- SENTENCE 78 ---
Implemented on a broader scale, mariculture could contribute significantly to job creation, and the production of healthy food for residents of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 79 ---
The ASCES identified mariculture as a key priority and possibly highly valuable activity that could expand the Territory's relatively narrow economic base[\[2\]](#page-9-2) and benefit the local population.

--- SENTENCE 80 ---
It also identified mariculture as critical to promoting sustainability of its regional fish stocks and habitats.

--- SENTENCE 81 ---
Mariculture can reduce negative stressors on local fish populations while providing food security and/or economic security.

--- SENTENCE 82 ---
Our approach identified which fish and shellfish species, technologies, and practices are most appropriate for American Samoa and most likely to result in an economically robust and sustainable mariculture industry, as well as those that are not feasible at this point.

--- SENTENCE 83 ---
These include but are not necessarily limited to: nearshore aquaculture of various fish, shellfish, and seaweed species; offshore aquaculture such as tuna; and onshore aquaculture and mariculture.

--- SENTENCE 84 ---
We also conducted comprehensive, evidence-based cost benefit analyses on various mariculture approaches to assess potential benefits to the local resources should a species be shifted to aquaculture harvest from wild harvest.

--- SENTENCE 85 ---
For example, wild sea cucumber could be harvested by aquaculture for shipment to foreign countries instead of harvested from local waters.

--- SENTENCE 86 ---
While harvesting from local waters may have improved the economic resilience for some people, i[t](https://www.radionz.co.nz/international/pacific-news/230575/american-samoa-bans-sea-cucumber-fishing-for-first-time)

--- SENTENCE 87 ---
[devastated the local sea cucumber population](https://www.radionz.co.nz/international/pacific-news/230575/american-samoa-bans-sea-cucumber-fishing-for-first-time) and resulted in a [moratorium on sea cucumber](http://www.samoanews.com/moratorium-removal-sea-cucumbers)

--- SENTENCE 88 ---
[harvests](http://www.samoanews.com/moratorium-removal-sea-cucumbers)igned in 2013 by Governor Lolo Moliga.

--- SENTENCE 89 ---
Shifting to mariculture before wild sea cucumber populations were depleted may have been wise.

--- SENTENCE 90 ---
Success of an aquaculture venture also depends on other factors, including establishing operations in areas conducive to growing and harvesting the desired species.

--- SENTENCE 91 ---
Some species more than others thrive in a mariculture environment, and the biophysical characteristics of particular nearshore and marine environments can greatly influence which species will thrive.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
Our study will identify species that are most appropriate to rear on small tropical islands with steep bathymetry and warm air temperatures.

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
However, this study will not delve into the multi-agency analyses required by National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) for each recommended site.

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
That extensive in-depth analysis is more properly the purview of mariculture ventures.

# <span id="page-11-0"></span>**Study for Determining Local Sentiment and Potential for Mariculture**

We conducted an interactive presentation of mariculture information to villages, the Office of Samoan Affairs, and several businesses during which we asked about perceptions of and interest in mariculture.

--- SENTENCE 123 ---
Because many people understand the word aquaculture, but not necessarily mariculture, we utilized "aquaculture" in our surveys and presentations.

--- SENTENCE 124 ---
We provided a paper survey to participants that attended village meetings so that participants could anonymously provide information they may have been hesitant to provide verbally in a group setting.

--- SENTENCE 125 ---
We interviewed local business, including freight handling/shipping companies, hardware supply stores, engineering experts, grocers, restaurateurs, and educational and government institutions.

--- SENTENCE 126 ---
The questionnaires focused on overall sentiment of mariculture and thoughts regarding the feasibility of mariculture on land, nearshore or offshore ocean areas, and which species respondents were interested in growing/purchasing.

--- SENTENCE 127 ---
In addition, the questionnaires included six main areas to gather local knowledge or experience: Shipping and Transportation, Environmental Analysis, Labor Force, Local Restaurant and Grocers, Engineering Support, and Supplies and Equipment.

--- SENTENCE 128 ---
A summary and discussion of their responses are provided in the following sections.

--- SENTENCE 129 ---
The overall sentiment of respondents was positive regarding the potential development of mariculture, whether this occurs on land, in the nearshore or open ocean areas.

--- SENTENCE 130 ---
Most respondents supported on-land operations despite the limited flat land and potential cost increases in plant operations due to infrastructure construction, reliable power sources and continuous/reliable food sources for growth.

--- SENTENCE 131 ---
This is predominantly due to residents already having conducted tilapia aquaculture.

--- SENTENCE 132 ---
They also supported nearshore aquaculture.

--- SENTENCE 133 ---
Respondents focused far less on offshore aquaculture.

--- SENTENCE 134 ---
In addition, if mariculture could be done in a sustainable, environmentally friendly way yet provide employment, there would likely be long-term support.

--- SENTENCE 135 ---
In general, respondents believe that locally sourced products (island-wide or a single village) would benefit their community, provide a consistent supply of certain products and provid[e](https://www.usaid.gov/agriculture-and-food-security#:~:text=Food%20security%20means%20having%2C%20at,a%20productive%20and%20healthy%20life.)

--- SENTENCE 136 ---
Most respondents, particularly in villages, focused on food security necessitating aquaculture, as opposed to financial gain.

--- SENTENCE 137 ---
For instance, one village resident commented that the cost of a case of chicken has gone up 30 percent the past couple years.

--- SENTENCE 138 ---
Additionally, there is a strong desire to purchase and support fresh, locally produced products (fish and vegetables) rather than importing products from mainland areas, even if the price is higher (e.g., vegetables, frozen fish or aquaculture products).

--- SENTENCE 139 ---
Fishermen cannot always provide a consistent supply or diverse product line for local consumption, therefore aquaculture and mariculture may provide a consistent supply of other desirable species such as mud crabs, limu (seaweed), faisua (giant clam), freshwater eel, fe'e (octopus), kuikui (urchin), mullet, sea cucumber, and alili (sea snail).

# <span id="page-11-1"></span>**SPECIES RESEARCHED**

Several species were identified for their potential for aquaculture, including faisua (giant clam), tilapia, freshwater eel, freshwater prawn, mullet, red limu, green limu (sea grapes), sea cucumber, alili (turban snail), mud crab (or mangrove crab), oyster, milkfish, fe'e (octopus), and kuikui (urchin).

--- SENTENCE 141 ---
These are the species that the villages and restaurateurs mentioned they would be interested in having grown locally.

--- SENTENCE 142 ---
This list however contains species that may not be that easy to propagate and grow in captivity, such as the freshwater eel (Table 1), as well as species that were only mentioned once or twice.

--- SENTENCE 143 ---
We present thorough information in Appendix A, including for species not identified by the community members during our visit (e.g. tuna).

--- SENTENCE 144 ---
Here we will provide brief information for the most mentioned desired species broken into sections: freshwater or brackish fish, saltwater fish, crustaceans, mollusks, seaweed, and echinoderms.

#### **Freshwater or brackish water fish**

The chief advantage of freshwater fish aquaculture is that it is already widely practiced throughout the world and extensive resources are available to guide any project in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 145 ---
Additionally, most freshwater fish can be stocked at higher densities than marine fish, and will tolerate greater variation in water temperature and conditions.

--- SENTENCE 146 ---
In general, it is easier to breed freshwater fish in captivity and establishing a broodstock for aquaculture is not a major challenge, unlike marine fish aquaculture.

--- SENTENCE 147 ---
Most freshwater aquaculture is relatively low tech, and does not require advanced training to practice.

--- SENTENCE 148 ---
The chief challenge of freshwater fish aquaculture is that these species typically do not have a high market price and must be produced at high volume if commercial profit is desired.

--- SENTENCE 149 ---
Intensive aquaculture of these species can produce a significant amount of waste that requires extra effort and infrastructure to manage sustainably.

--- SENTENCE 150 ---
On the other hand, the relatively low cost and low technical requirements can make these species good candidates for non-commercial efforts to promote local food security and reduce the dependence on imported fish.

--- SENTENCE 151 ---
Here, we summarize three species that may be of interest.

--- SENTENCE 152 ---
Tilapia is one of the most commonly farmed fish species in the world and exemplifies many of the points discussed above.

--- SENTENCE 153 ---
Similarly, the farming of various catfish from the genus Pangasius is widely practiced.

--- SENTENCE 154 ---
Also included in this section is a discussion of aquaculture opportunities for species of mullet.

--- SENTENCE 155 ---
Mullets are euryhaline, and can be adapted to fresh, brackish, or marine environments.

--- SENTENCE 156 ---
The requirements for mullet aquaculture are more similar to those of freshwater species than of other marine fish, which typically require more sophisticated facilities and techniques.

#### **Tilapia**

The term "tilapia" refers to a wide range of cichlid fish species in the genera *Oreochromis*, *Sarotherodon* and *Tilapia*.

--- SENTENCE 157 ---
Each species, as well as hybrids between species, have unique characteristics that must be evaluated in the context of the local situation (Haws et al. 2020).

--- SENTENCE 158 ---
There is great variation in their feeding and reproductive habits, as well as salinity tolerance.

--- SENTENCE 159 ---
Tilapia have become one of the most common freshwater species for aquaculture on a global basis due to their rapid growth, tolerance of extreme environmental conditions and ease of reproduction.

--- SENTENCE 160 ---
Some tilapia species can benefit from fertilizing the pond which produces an algal bloom and zooplankton which the fish can eat (Haws et al. 2020).

--- SENTENCE 161 ---
This can greatly reduce the amount of feed required.

--- SENTENCE 162 ---
Despite being one of the easiest fish to culture, caution is indicated because there is a requirement for some degree of technical expertise (Haws et al. 2020).

--- SENTENCE 163 ---
American Samoa imports a large amount of frozen fish which could possibly be replaced by local tilapia production (Haws et al. 2020).

--- SENTENCE 164 ---
Locally produced tilapia could become profitable if feed costs can be reduced by developing local sources.

--- SENTENCE 165 ---
Previous studies suggest that tilapia culture is ready for expansion and could become a significant source of food and income, particularly with the already-established tanks on Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 166 ---
Additionally, even small-scale production can be important as a local food source.

--- SENTENCE 167 ---
Many countries have school-based food production programs including fish culture, which can provide a steady source of food for school children (Haws et al. 2020).

--- SENTENCE 168 ---
Tilapia culture could be another way to take advantage of the freshwater resources of American

Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 169 ---
Particularly in the case of villages which surround wetlands, freshwater could be extracted to support tilapia culture.

--- SENTENCE 170 ---
This is best done by diverting a limited amount of water from the upland areas to supply ponds built outside of the wetlands, rather than citing ponds in the wetlands (Haws et al. 2020).

--- SENTENCE 171 ---
Wetland areas are generally poor sites for ponds due to flooding and because ponds built in low-lying areas cannot be drained.

--- SENTENCE 172 ---
The latter is particularly important for tilapia farming because these species tend to breed prolifically[3] in ponds and exceed the carrying capacity.

--- SENTENCE 173 ---
The ponds must be periodically drained and dried to get rid of the overabundance of small fish (Haws et al. 2020).

--- SENTENCE 174 ---
In any case, if tilapia farming is expanded, it may be important to calculate a water budget for the farming area to avoid creating competition for freshwater between residents and fish farmers.

# **Freshwater eel**[4][5]

Three species of native, tropical Pacific freshwater eels occur throughout American Samoa: *Anguilla australis australis* (shortfin eel), *A. celebesensis* (Celebes longfin eel) and *A. marmorata* (giant mottled eel).

--- SENTENCE 176 ---
Typically, the giant mottled eel is harvested in streams in American Samoa for subsistence.

--- SENTENCE 177 ---
Freshwater eels are considered an economically important high-valued species worldwide.

--- SENTENCE 178 ---
The most common eels that are currently being farmed around the world are the European eel (*Anguilla anguilla*) mainly in Netherlands, Italy, and Denmark; American eel (*Anguilla rostrata*) in eastern United States; Japanese eel (*Anguilla japonica*) in Japan, Korea, China and Taiwan; and longfin eel (*Anguilla reinhardtii*) and shortfin eel (*Anguilla australis*) in Australia and New Zealand.

--- SENTENCE 179 ---
Freshwater eels are catadromous, spending their juvenile and adult stages in freshwater and must return to the ocean to spawn.

--- SENTENCE 180 ---
The fertilized eggs are carried by the ocean current as they change into larvae, and then after around 18 months they develop into "glass eels" (thefishsite.org 2023a).

--- SENTENCE 181 ---
These juvenile eels are under-developed and have a transparent appearance, hence the name.

--- SENTENCE 182 ---
This brood stock then returns to rivers and streams (around age 2 to 3) to live to adulthood.

--- SENTENCE 183 ---
Since this life cycle is complex, breeding eels for commercial farming purposes has not been successful.

--- SENTENCE 184 ---
Therefore, eels are harvested in the wild and grown out to marketable sizes.

--- SENTENCE 185 ---
Many eels are harvested as "glass eels" then shipped around the world to grow out facilities or consumed (glass eels are known to fetch up to \$2,000 per lb).

--- SENTENCE 186 ---
World demand for freshwater eels has been increasing due to the market expansion and increased popularity of several eel dishes.

--- SENTENCE 187 ---
In American Samoa, glass eel collection should be done locally; therefore, collection sites would need to be identified.

--- SENTENCE 188 ---
Glass eels are collected using fishing gears such as fyke nets, scoop nets, fry bulldozer (trawling) and fine mesh nets, as well as other materials, such as light and oxygen tanks.

--- SENTENCE 189 ---
There are three general main methods to grow eels.

--- SENTENCE 190 ---
The primary way they would be grown in American Samoa is with a recirculating system that includes a system of square or circular tanks (25-100 m²), usually built of cement or fiberglass.

--- SENTENCE 191 ---
A few people expressed interest in freshwater eel aquaculture on land for food security and could use already-built concrete tanks; others have interest in building tanks to aquaculture eels.

--- SENTENCE 192 ---
This method will require continuous water exchange to remove impurities and fecal matter; tanks also require proper temperature ranges and control, and aeration.

--- SENTENCE 193 ---
Individual growth rates vary and grading every 6 weeks is necessary to reach a high overall growth performance and reduce cannibalism (FAO 2023a).

--- SENTENCE 194 ---
Freshwater eels are carnivorous species that feed on a wide range of diets.

--- SENTENCE 195 ---
Water parameters, aeration systems, and feed for eels is discussed in Appendix B. Eels are typically large enough for harvest within 10-12 months.

--- SENTENCE 196 ---
Electricity for the tanks to run the aeration and filtration is necessary, thus communication with American Samoa Power Authority (ASPA) is vital for determining the best route for continuous power.

--- SENTENCE 197 ---
This includes the option to have solar and/or generator backup power.

#### **Marine fish**

Marine fish species present a different set of challenges and potential opportunities from freshwater fish.

--- SENTENCE 198 ---
Marine species are typically accustomed to larger spaces, lower population density, and more stable water conditions than are found in freshwater environments.

--- SENTENCE 199 ---
These factors raise the technical requirements for any farming operation.

--- SENTENCE 200 ---
While many freshwater species can be reared in outdoor ponds, marine species are often raised in pens or enclosures located in lagoons or other sheltered marine environments.

--- SENTENCE 201 ---
This limits the number of suitable sites relative to freshwater aquaculture.

--- SENTENCE 202 ---
Marine aquaculture can also be located offshore which allows sites to be located more easily, but this method requires complex engineering to construct and greater logistical effort to provide supplies and labor to offshore locations.

--- SENTENCE 203 ---
If raised indoors or in artificial pools, water quality, temperature, dissolved oxygen, and other variables must be monitored and controlled, and high quality filtration systems are required.

--- SENTENCE 204 ---
Marine fish often require higher quality feed products, and they often rely on wild caught "trash" or non-marketable fish.

--- SENTENCE 205 ---
Due to the difficulty growing these fish, only mullet, which is readily available to catch and grow to maturity in a nearshore net pen, is included here.

#### **Mullet**

Mullets (Mullidae) are a group of small to moderate sized fish with a wide distribution throughout the world.

--- SENTENCE 206 ---
The most commonly farmed species is the grey mullet (*Mugil cephalus*), which does occur in American Samoa, and aquaculture of this species is well established in places like Egypt and India (thefishsite.com 2022b).

--- SENTENCE 207 ---
In Egypt and India, mullet have been grown in small ponds or small enclosures near estuaries for centuries, and the technical requirements for a basic operation are quite low.

--- SENTENCE 208 ---
Most mullet species are detritivores, and can be fed from agricultural scraps, and a significant amount of their diet can come from algae that occurs naturally in ponds (thefishsite.com 2022b).

--- SENTENCE 209 ---
This species can also be grown in polyculture with other species such as tilapia, where it will consume any leftover feed from the other fish.

--- SENTENCE 210 ---
The grey mullet is tolerant of a broad range of salinities, meaning aquaculture facilities can be placed near either fresh or saltwater depending on availability and the needs of the community (thefishsite.com 2022b).

--- SENTENCE 211 ---
While widely consumed around the world, mullet is not a high value fish and is typically marketed and eaten within the local community (thefishsite.com 2022b, NMFS 2018).

--- SENTENCE 212 ---
The fringelip mullet (*Crenimugil crenilabis*) is most commonly caught and consumed in American Samoa (WPFMC 2021).

--- SENTENCE 213 ---
Aquaculture for this species is scarce, though a small program was initiated in Papua New Guinea aimed at supplementing declining wild populations.

--- SENTENCE 214 ---
A further investigation of this species may prove useful, especially if the wild population can be used as a source of fingerlings.

--- SENTENCE 215 ---
During meetings with the villages, residents expressed interest in possibly catching mullet to rear to maturity in nearshore net pens.

--- SENTENCE 216 ---
Similar to other species, the main challenge involves establishing a broodstock.

--- SENTENCE 217 ---
Many mullets migrate in order to spawn, and the change in temperature and salinity often appears necessary to trigger reproduction (thefishsite.com 2022b).

--- SENTENCE 218 ---
These conditions are usually difficult to replicate in captivity, and some operations use hormone injections instead.

--- SENTENCE 219 ---
A second option is to capture eggs, larvae, or fingerlings in the wild and transport them to a secure pond or enclosure until they reach harvest size (thefishsite.com 2022b).

--- SENTENCE 220 ---
This method is less technical, but does require some organization and requires fine mesh nets that may differ from the gear used in other fishing boats.

--- SENTENCE 221 ---
The locations where wild fish spawn and where aggregations of larval fish can be found must be known, and it is necessary to gradually transition larval fish to new conditions on the farm.

--- SENTENCE 222 ---
In American Samoa, fishermen know exactly where to locate passing mullet schools, and likely could locate juveniles as well.

--- SENTENCE 223 ---
Pre-industrial aquaculture of this species relied on small ponds attached to estuaries that could be opened during particular times of year to allow small fish to enter, and then closed again to trap and raise them (NMFS 2018).

--- SENTENCE 224 ---
In American Samoa, as mentioned above, catching the younger mullet and raising them in a nearshore pen would be the easiest avenue.

--- SENTENCE 225 ---
However, given the ability to harvest wild mullet, unless the purpose is to sell continually to restaurants and grocers, consumption for the village will most likely occur through fishing the wild stocks.

--- SENTENCE 226 ---
Additionally, harvesting wild stock to grow and sell may harm the wild population numbers, thus would need additional research on feasibility.

#### **Crustaceans**

Crustacean aquaculture is another possibility and has requirements somewhat intermediate between freshwater and marine fish aquaculture.

--- SENTENCE 227 ---
As with marine fish, one of the chief concerns is establishing a breeding program that can be propagated out each generation.

--- SENTENCE 228 ---
However, unlike marine fish, there are many successful examples and even some commercially available products that can ease the early phases of any aquaculture operation.

--- SENTENCE 229 ---
Crustacean facilities generally consist of a land based area where the egg and larval stages are raised and then juveniles and adults can be grown out in ponds, or enclosed areas attached to wetlands.

--- SENTENCE 230 ---
Because crustaceans are often grown at high densities, a system to manage waste products leaving the facility should be planned and developed from the outset.

--- SENTENCE 231 ---
The simplest crustacean to culture is most likely a variety of shrimp.

--- SENTENCE 232 ---
Shrimp farming is well established with clear methods and is expected to continue growing, though it has been associated with environmental degradation in some places.

--- SENTENCE 233 ---
Cultivation of mud crabs is a third option with difficulties and requirements similar to those for lobster (detailed in Appendix A).

--- SENTENCE 234 ---
#### **Shrimp**

Shrimp aquaculture is similar to crabs in many respects, and is widely practiced across Southeast Asia.

--- SENTENCE 235 ---
The whiteleg shrimp (*Litopenaeus vannamei*) and tiger prawn (*Penaeus monodon*) are the most common species (Seachoice.org 2023).

--- SENTENCE 236 ---
The technology necessary to breed shrimp is well established and not especially complex relative to other forms of aquaculture.

--- SENTENCE 237 ---
Shrimp can be bred in indoor tanks, and the eggs are collected and moved to a hatching tank.

--- SENTENCE 238 ---
The shrimp larvae are transitioned to progressively larger tanks as they grow before being moved to outdoor ponds or enclosures placed in wetland areas (Seachoice.org 2023).

--- SENTENCE 239 ---
Shrimp require less protein in their feed than other crustaceans and can be given a variety of inexpensive foods, though fish is still often used.

--- SENTENCE 240 ---
In theory, shrimp provides a better yield of marketable protein relative to feed than either fish or other crustaceans.

--- SENTENCE 241 ---
It is also possible to polyculture shrimp with other crustaceans such as crabs, though procedures and stocking densities must be adjusted.

--- SENTENCE 242 ---
Shrimp are popular worldwide and there is usually a strong market for them.

--- SENTENCE 243 ---
Intensive farming of shrimp has caused major damage to wetland and mangrove areas due to deforestation and the waste products leaking into natural areas, and plans should be made to mitigate any such effects from the outset (Seachoice.org 2023).

--- SENTENCE 244 ---
Aquacultured shrimp can also be vulnerable to disease, which may require chemicals or antibiotics additives to be added, which can then also leak into the environment.

--- SENTENCE 245 ---
Because they can be raised in tanks, there is a trend towards moving shrimp aquaculture further inland to separate it from wetlands (thefishsite.org 2020).

--- SENTENCE 246 ---
While these operations are more complex and tend to be smaller, they offer more options for responsibly managing waste products and are more ecologically sustainable.

--- SENTENCE 247 ---
There are freshwater prawns in the streams across Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 248 ---
Many people in the villages expressed interest in aquaculture of these prawns.

#### **Crabs**

Mud crabs or mangrove crabs may refer to a variety of crab species depending on the location, usually of the genus *Scylla*.

--- SENTENCE 249 ---
These animals are typically found on mud or silt bottoms and will not venture onto land, though they may be caught there during low tides.

--- SENTENCE 250 ---
Seagrass and mangrove areas may be preferred, depending on the species and local habitat (Shelley et al. 2011).

--- SENTENCE 251 ---
A simple method of farming crabs is similar to lobster, in that it involves capturing larvae or juveniles and raising them in a tank or pen until they reach adult size.

--- SENTENCE 252 ---
Breeding crabs in captivity is less complicated than lobster and is widely practiced in Australia and Vietnam, and commercial manuals and products are available to assist in the design and implementation of an aquaculture program (Shelley et al. 2011).

--- SENTENCE 253 ---
While much of the process is not particularly technical, the rearing process can be somewhat complex as the developing crabs must be separated during certain stages to avoid cannibalism.

--- SENTENCE 254 ---
Crabs can typically be fed using manufactured feed products, though will exhibit faster growth using miscellaneous fish or other high protein products (Shelley et al. 2011).

--- SENTENCE 255 ---
Intensive farming of crabs can lead to a build-up of waste products and damage to the surrounding environment.

--- SENTENCE 256 ---
In particular, pens are often located near mangroves as this is the natural habitat for many species (Haws et al. 2020).

--- SENTENCE 257 ---
Development of high density aquaculture around these vulnerable habitats should be pursued with caution and careful planning.

--- SENTENCE 258 ---
Mangrove crabs, or mud crabs, were mentioned several times as a desired species to mariculture.

--- SENTENCE 259 ---
They would be grown using seawater on land.

--- SENTENCE 260 ---
The source of the stock is harvested from the nearshore environment in a few mangrove locations around Tutuila (e.g. Leone and Nuuuli Pala), thus there would be concern about a full scale mariculture operation leading to population declines.

--- SENTENCE 261 ---
The effort is nascent, with two individuals expressing a lot of interest, one of whom had started but abandoned the effort when his assistant moved off island.

--- SENTENCE 262 ---
The other intends to begin in 2024.

#### **Mollusks**

Aquaculture of sessile mollusks presents a different set of potential benefits and challenges from any of the types discussed thus far.

--- SENTENCE 263 ---
These animals are filter feeders and typically do not require feed or enclosures, making their hatcheries very simple to build and operate.

--- SENTENCE 264 ---
However, yields are typically lower and most of these organisms grow and mature slowly, requiring at least two years to reach marketable size.

--- SENTENCE 265 ---
Choosing a site for oyster or clam beds is a major consideration, and can have a large impact on the potential size and sustainability of the venture.

--- SENTENCE 266 ---
Additionally, these animals may accumulate bacteria or toxins, so it is important that they be located some distance from human populations or any significant source of waste.

--- SENTENCE 267 ---
Two types are considered in this document: giant clams (faisua) and oysters.

--- SENTENCE 268 ---
Faisua are one of the simplest forms of mariculture available, but they do not produce a high yield or commercial value and face some novel difficulties, notably theft or poaching.

--- SENTENCE 269 ---
Oyster aquaculture is somewhat similar to other forms of aquaculture, but special effort is required to find suitable sites that have the proper water conditions.

#### **Faisua**

Faisua is Samoan for giant clam.

--- SENTENCE 270 ---
The term giant clam can refer to any member of the genus *Tridacna*, which typically reach a large size (Ellis 2000).

--- SENTENCE 271 ---
The methods necessary to culture giant clams are well established and successful examples exist in several places throughout southeast Asia, notably Samoa (Moorhead 2018).

--- SENTENCE 272 ---
The production cycle for giant clams typically involves a moderately sized central breeding facility that houses adult clams that are used for broodstock and several additional tanks used for growing the early life stages.

--- SENTENCE 273 ---
The larvae are reared in this facility until they reach the stage of maturity where they become sessile and implant into the substrate.

--- SENTENCE 274 ---
They are then grown until they reach a suitable size and their shells harden enough to survive transport, usually 5-10 mm (Ellis 2000).

--- SENTENCE 275 ---
At this point, the clams can be moved to favorable locations in a wild habitat and where they will grow naturally.

--- SENTENCE 276 ---
The clams may be placed in the seabed, or are sometimes grown for a time in small cages or enclosures to keep away fish and octopus that can feed on smaller clams (Ellis 2000).

--- SENTENCE 277 ---
Farmers inspect their clams periodically to look for signs of disease, poor growth, or predation and take appropriate measures.

--- SENTENCE 278 ---
Depending on conditions and the particular species, it can take up to 5 years for the clams to grow to harvest size.

--- SENTENCE 279 ---
Many successful giant clam aquaculture operations work as a partnership between a central facility (sometimes government operated) and several villages or smaller operations.

--- SENTENCE 280 ---
The central facility maintains the broodstock and provides small clams to their partners at various times around the year (Moorhead 2018).

--- SENTENCE 281 ---
Because the most technically complex aspects of the operation are easy to centralize, this improves efficiency and helps to keep costs low.

--- SENTENCE 282 ---
Despite their size, much of the body of giant clams is inedible and can not be sold.

--- SENTENCE 283 ---
Given their long growth cycle before harvest, giant clams work well as a means of providing food security to village areas rather than as an export product (Haws et al. 2020).

--- SENTENCE 284 ---
Giant clams can also be used as part of efforts to restore reef areas, and once mature they may provide a tourist attraction for divers.

--- SENTENCE 285 ---
Faisua was the most mentioned and desired mariculture species in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 286 ---
Restaurateurs and community members alike desire the faisua meat.

--- SENTENCE 287 ---
DMWR is working hard to develop an aquaculture facility that could not just rear faisua, but also propagate.

--- SENTENCE 288 ---
It's easy to grow and highly desired, thus would be a great species to mariculture.

--- SENTENCE 289 ---
Western Samoa is giving American Samoa 600 young giant clams as a gift out of the Two Samoas talks in 2022.

--- SENTENCE 290 ---
In early 2023, the Division of Marine and Wildlife Resources (DMWR) was determining the best locations to grow the clams on Tutuila in cages on or near the reef based on community desire and engagement, as well as biophysical needs.

--- SENTENCE 291 ---
The faisua that are typically grown in mariculture are Palauan, with smoother shells and a different habitat requirement than Samoan faisua.

--- SENTENCE 292 ---
Palauan faisua are typically grown in a sandy environment in cages, instead of outplanted to a reef where you would find Samoan faisua.

--- SENTENCE 293 ---
Many giant clam aquaculture operations have been inhibited by the problem of theft (Haws et al. 2020, Moorhead 2018).

--- SENTENCE 294 ---
Because they are often grown in the ocean and with few barriers, boats can come into the bay at night to steal the cage(s), often never seen or able to be confronted quickly if they are spotted.

--- SENTENCE 295 ---
Since each clam may represent years of investment, this loss is often enough to render the entire effort unsustainable; a previous attempt to institute giant clam aquaculture in American Samoa failed for this reason (Haws et al. 2020).

--- SENTENCE 296 ---
While it is possible to grow clams in cages that provide additional security, Samoa and the Philippines have had some success with local villages instituting cultural measures that seem to reduce theft to a tolerable level (Moorhead 2018).

--- SENTENCE 297 ---
It is recommended that any effort in American Samoa also attempt to replicate this success by focusing on community relations.

#### **Oysters and other bivalves**

Many places in southeast Asia and the Pacific islands practice aquaculture of bivalves and a variety of tropical oysters and clams are potentially suitable for aquaculture in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 298 ---
While each species has some distinctions, the overall requirements are similar and this section focuses on oysters.

--- SENTENCE 299 ---
While a dedicated hatchery is necessary for the breeding of larvae, these species are grown in wild habitats with relatively simple methods and little specialized infrastructure.

--- SENTENCE 300 ---
Oyster growth is partially dependent on temperature, meaning that tropical regions can achieve faster growth rates and reduce the time before harvest (Nowland et al. 2019).

--- SENTENCE 301 ---
Examples of successful bivalve aquaculture are split between technically simpler operations aimed at local food security and sustainability versus larger and more complex hatcheries geared towards the global market (Nowland et al. 2019).

--- SENTENCE 302 ---
Oyster aquaculture begins by identifying a source of "spat," which is the juvenile stage of development that occurs immediately after the free swimming larvae attach to a hard surface.

--- SENTENCE 303 ---
Spat can be obtained from a hatchery or collected from the wild.

--- SENTENCE 304 ---
Hatchery developed spat have the advantage of consistency and can be used to implement selective-breeding programs or other valuable features (Nowland et al. 2019).

--- SENTENCE 305 ---
However, this requires a considerable investment in facilities and expertise.

--- SENTENCE 306 ---
A simple alternative to collect spat from the wild and transfer them to the desired location for continued growth.

--- SENTENCE 307 ---
The spat can be attached to a variety of cages, ropes, or meshes that facilitate easy monitoring and cleaning (Haws et al. 2020).

--- SENTENCE 308 ---
Depending on the species and conditions, tropical oysters may reach harvest size in one year or less (Nowland et al. 2019).

--- SENTENCE 309 ---
An early decision in oyster aquaculture is whether to invest in developing methods for the local species of the genus *Saccostrea* or to import the Pacific oysters (*Crassostrea gigas)* from Hawaii or elsewhere.

--- SENTENCE 310 ---
Importation of spat is not expensive (Haws et al. 2020), but the local species allow for wild collection and may be better adapted to local conditions.

--- SENTENCE 311 ---
A second major concern is choosing a location with suitable water quality.

--- SENTENCE 312 ---
As filter-feeders, oysters will accumulate bacteria and chemicals from the water, and they must be monitored to ensure these levels remain safe for human consumption (Haws et al. 2020).

--- SENTENCE 313 ---
On occasion, oysters can also be used as a tool in environmental restoration because they will filter pollutants out of the water, though this renders them unsafe to eat.

#### **Seaweed**

Several types of seaweed are cultivated in different parts of the world at both an industrial and at a smaller scale.

--- SENTENCE 314 ---
At either level, it is one of the easiest products to grow and requires almost no specialized expertise or equipment.

--- SENTENCE 315 ---
The simplest method is to identify a flat sand bed or similar growing area and lay out several long lines of rope to which seaweed cuttings have been attached.

--- SENTENCE 316 ---
These lines can be hauled to shore to collect the harvest and attach new cuttings (Bjerregaard et al. 2016).

--- SENTENCE 317 ---
More advanced systems make use of floating buoys with wire or mesh cages with cuttings attached.

--- SENTENCE 318 ---
While slightly more complicated to operate, the materials are still inexpensive relative to almost any other aquaculture venture and the investment in equipment may reduce time and labor costs (Bjerregaard et al. 2016).

--- SENTENCE 319 ---
The ability to locate materials further from shore can also reduce conflict with other community members who make use of near-shore environments.

--- SENTENCE 320 ---
Reducing costs is important because seaweed does not typically fetch a high price at market, and many potential farmers might prefer to invest in more lucrative fish or shellfish farms (Haws et al. 2020).

--- SENTENCE 321 ---
In theory, seaweed has incredible potential as a sustainable product, but demand remains relatively low thus far.

--- SENTENCE 322 ---
While seaweed is a part of traditional culture in American Samoa, the practice appears to be in decline among younger generations (Haws et al. 2020).

--- SENTENCE 323 ---
At least three species of seaweed are consumed in American Samoa and a pilot study should be conducted to determine which, if any, are economically viable (Haws et al. 2020).

--- SENTENCE 324 ---
The predominant seaweed, or limu, mentioned during our visits were red limu and green sea grapes.

--- SENTENCE 325 ---
Communities and restaurateurs both expressed a desire to have a consistent limu supply.

--- SENTENCE 326 ---
Importation of other species for aquaculture is not recommended due to the risk of the non-native species escaping and becoming invasive.

--- SENTENCE 327 ---
In addition to food resources, seaweed cultivation can also fill a variety of other roles.

--- SENTENCE 328 ---
A few varieties of seaweed are important as a source of valuable chemical compounds and can be sold to specialized pharmaceutical or nutrition companies (Haws et al. 2020).

--- SENTENCE 329 ---
However, this requires careful monitoring of the chemical composition of the seaweed and the investment in lab facilities and personnel.

--- SENTENCE 330 ---
Alternatively, seaweeds can be used in restoration to remove nitrogen and phosphorus that results from human waste or agricultural runoff, as well as providing habitat for animal species (Haws et al. 2020, Bjerregaard et al. 2016).

#### **Echinoderms and Polychaetes**

Sea cucumbers are the only echinoderm that was mentioned for food security and export purposes during our village visits.

--- SENTENCE 331 ---
There are plenty along the coastlines upon which to begin a mariculture venture.

--- SENTENCE 332 ---
The sea cucumber venture would primarily be aimed at the production of high quality goods for export, and may have little community involvement beyond hiring labor.

#### **Sea cucumbers**

Sea cucumbers (family *Holothuriidae*) have been harvested for hundreds of years for trade with Asia and were probably one of the first real 'exports' from the Pacific Islands (Hair et al. 2012).

--- SENTENCE 333 ---
Sea cucumbers are threatened worldwide with over exploitation.

--- SENTENCE 334 ---
There have been documented efforts to cultivate these animals.

--- SENTENCE 335 ---
Researchers in the Indian Ocean region with regards to *Holothuriidae* aquaculture summarized in their assessment, "it is in the context of depleted fisheries, high export demand and weak governance that the development of sea cucumber aquaculture is gaining momentum in the region."

--- SENTENCE 336 ---
(Eriksson et al. 2012)

--- SENTENCE 337 ---
A research team in Japan performed a study showing the possibility of integrated multi-trophic aquaculture in which *Apostichopus japonicus* was cultured successfully in the water column below fish cages, because the survival and growth of the sea cucumbers were enhanced due to the ability to avoid predator interactions and adverse environmental conditions as well as nutritional feed supply from the fish cage (Yokohama 2013).

--- SENTENCE 338 ---
There are at least two species of sea cucumbers that American Samoa residents consume.

--- SENTENCE 339 ---
*Actinopyga mauritiana* is a large brown and white speckled sea cucumber (Atafua et al. 2008), and *Holothuria atra* (loli).

--- SENTENCE 340 ---
*H. atra* species is extremely useful in the Samoan culture as it is considered a food source by many families.

--- SENTENCE 341 ---
People in the Pacific scrape off the black skin of the *H. atra* until they can see or feel the white layer below.

--- SENTENCE 342 ---
The white flesh is cut into cubes and stored in sea water until eaten (Atafua et al. 2008).

--- SENTENCE 343 ---
Factors that work in favor of successful aquaculture include pristine marine environments, long familiarity with sea cucumbers as a commodity, and traditional marine tenure systems that in some places can provide a basis for management of released sea cucumbers.

--- SENTENCE 344 ---
Challenges include lack of technical capacity, unproven effectiveness of sea cucumber releases and poaching (Hair et al. 2012).

--- SENTENCE 345 ---
Sea cucumbers seem relatively easy to grow in an ocean setting.

--- SENTENCE 346 ---
However, there is an extensive hatchery rearing period, which would make growing sea cucumbers in American Samoa more difficult due to a lack of facilities.

--- SENTENCE 347 ---
Production costs are relatively high during the cultivation period (thefishsite.com 2023b).

####

--- SENTENCE 348 ---
**Polychaete Worms**

The predominant species of polychaete worm being raised in aquaculture for food and fish feed is the "Ragworm" (*Hediste diversicolor*, formerly known as *Nereis diversicolor*).

--- SENTENCE 349 ---
Ragworms are commonly used as live bait in recreational and commercial fishing due to their attractiveness to various fish species.

--- SENTENCE 350 ---
There are species of ragworms that can be found in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 351 ---
One example is the species *Hediste (Nereis) diadroma*, which is commonly known as the Pacific ragworm, prevalent in various marine environments, including sandy and muddy substrates along the coasts.

--- SENTENCE 352 ---
These Nereididae polychaetes are relatively easy to cultivate and can be grown in controlled environments, making them suitable for aquaculture purposes.

--- SENTENCE 353 ---
They are also rich in protein and critical essential nutrients, such as HUFAs, which makes them a valuable food source for fish and other aquatic organisms.

--- SENTENCE 354 ---
Polychaetes are able to adapt their feeding behaviors to their environmental conditions, switching among carnivorous, herbivorous, planktivorous, bacterivorous, detritivorous and omnivorous feeding (Bischoff 2014).

--- SENTENCE 355 ---
*Nereis diversicolor*, a marine polychaete belonging to the family Nereididae, can tolerate a wide range of surrounding temperatures (4 to 25 degrees-C) as well as salinities, from only a few units to more than double the salinity prevalent in the oceans.

--- SENTENCE 356 ---
Its reproduction is monotelic, so it reproduces only once in its lifetime, with death always following reproduction (Bischoff 2014).

--- SENTENCE 357 ---
Increased aquaculture production will result in increased amounts of waste produced, and these wastes are highly underexploited.

--- SENTENCE 358 ---
Polychaetes might effectively consume such waste and transfer them into compounds as marine proteins and lipids.

--- SENTENCE 359 ---
In a study using the polychaete *H. diversicolor*, the authors showed that not only can these polychaetes be successfully reared on waste sludge from land-based salmon smolt aquaculture, but they also contain high valuable compounds, and therefore can help to increase the protein and lipid availability meanwhile decreasing the environmental impact from aquaculture activities (Wang et al. 2019).

--- SENTENCE 360 ---
Another study demonstrated that cultured polychaetes were able to grow using the clam feces.

--- SENTENCE 361 ---
These findings suggest that a suspension of bivalve feces can be used in the rearing of juvenile *N. diversicolor* in an integrated polyculture system (Batista et al. 2003).

--- SENTENCE 362 ---
A polychaete worm of particular interest in American Samoa is the palolo worm, *Palola viridis*, which has been harvested from the wild for generations and enjoyed as a delicacy either raw or cooked.

--- SENTENCE 363 ---
This worm is very important culturally and economically as well as a boon for tourism in October and November when they spawn.

--- SENTENCE 364 ---
It is entirely feasible to use this species in the place of more traditional cultured polychaete marine worms as a primary culture species or in combination with other species to help remove detritus, recycle waste and excess feed, as well as recycle the worms themselves as feedstock for carnivorous fishes and or invertebrates.

## <span id="page-24-0"></span>**SOCIAL CLIMATE**

We researched the social climate in American Samoa with respect to interest and receptiveness in aquaculture to determine if there were particular villages more interested in aquaculture than others.

--- SENTENCE 376 ---
Success of a venture depends on the willingness of village elders and members.

--- SENTENCE 377 ---
Many villages were interested in the idea of mariculture, but did not know how to get an endeavor started.

--- SENTENCE 378 ---
Residents and agency personnel alike cautioned that start up funds would be necessary and enquired about assistance for grant writing.

--- SENTENCE 379 ---
The potential for loans set up specifically for aquaculture was raised.

--- SENTENCE 380 ---
Residents in Fagali'i stressed that aquaculture is important for the economy and food security, but raised concerns about past efforts.

--- SENTENCE 381 ---
However, Manua residents pointed out that it would be great to have local fish on more menus to replace chicken, as well as more opportunities to purchase fish in markets.

--- SENTENCE 382 ---
They offered that time is of the essence - we should not just be talking with them about their interest, but telling them what they should do, and that the focus should first be on local consumption.

--- SENTENCE 383 ---
During discussion with staff at ASCC, we learned that mud crab aquaculture has been tried, but folded.

--- SENTENCE 384 ---
However, tilapia aquaculture projects that provided tilapia to the stores found the fish sold out in a couple hours.

--- SENTENCE 385 ---
Limu is also in demand and easy to culture.

--- SENTENCE 386 ---
The small clams, tuane, were sold by residents of American Samoa to build their villages, and they could be a possible mariculture species.

--- SENTENCE 387 ---
Of the issues raised, theft and training were predominant after funding needs.

--- SENTENCE 388 ---
Many villages talked about the theft of the faisua cages.

--- SENTENCE 389 ---
Many also talked about the need for trained individuals.

--- SENTENCE 390 ---
It is easy to come into a village and assist in setting up an aquaculture or mariculture venture, but if no one is skilled to maintain it, the effort will fail.

--- SENTENCE 391 ---
Another issue raised was that villages want fast results.

--- SENTENCE 392 ---
It can be difficult to watch an animal grow very slowly.

--- SENTENCE 393 ---
Thus, projects are abandoned before the results can be realized.

--- SENTENCE 394 ---
This is where dedicated trained individuals will be crucial to a project's success.

--- SENTENCE 395 ---
The last issue that was raised was raised by American Samoa Power Authority.

--- SENTENCE 396 ---
Sewer outfalls in Tafuna and Pago could lead to tainting of maricultured products.

--- SENTENCE 397 ---
On the north and west sides of Tutuila, residents utilize septic systems, thus may also have issues in their nearshore environment.

--- SENTENCE 398 ---
However, they offered on a positive note that they are installing wind turbines and a solar farm, which would mitigate costs of aquaculture projects.

# <span id="page-24-1"></span>**Local Restaurant and Grocers**

# <span id="page-58-0"></span>**ECONOMIC OPPORTUNITIES, CHALLENGES, AND CONSTRAINTS[29][30][31][32]**

Various forms of mariculture and aquaculture have become profitable in recent decades, which has contributed to aquaculture equaling wild capture fisheries in quantity and exceeding it in value produced (FAO 2022b).

--- SENTENCE 564 ---
Some of these fish and shellfish farming operations raise concerns about sustainability (e.g., shrimp–see previous discussion) and profitability is not assured.

--- SENTENCE 565 ---
As with any business venture, the profitability will depend on the costs of inputs, the efficiency of production, and the value of products and associated sales costs.

--- SENTENCE 566 ---
American Samoa is exploring aquaculture as part of a broader effort to diversify its economy.

--- SENTENCE 567 ---
The economy is currently dominated by government services and tuna canning, the latter of which has been in decline in recent years (GAO 2020).

--- SENTENCE 568 ---
Part of this decline comes from geopolitical competition.

--- SENTENCE 569 ---
Chinese catches of pelagic fishes like tuna has skyrocketed since 2000, and may be dramatically higher than reported values due to misreporting (Asche et al., 2022) and underreporting (Myers et al. 2022) by a heavily-subsidized distant water fleet (Sala et al., 2018).

--- SENTENCE 570 ---
This competition may also influence access by the U.S. tuna fleet to other countries' EEZs (e.g., Felton 2022; Kwan 2022).

--- SENTENCE 571 ---
At the same time the American Samoa tuna industry is facing tougher global competition, it faces growing costs in the form of increasing minimum wages, and reduced local fishing grounds through the proposed designation of the Pacific Remote Islands National Marine Sanctuary, and of green turtle critical habitat.

--- SENTENCE 572 ---
In the case of mariculture and aquaculture in American Samoa, specific factors affect these elements of profitability.

--- SENTENCE 573 ---
The islands are remote, which increases the costs of materials and equipment and the expense of shipping products.

--- SENTENCE 574 ---
They are mountainous with relatively little flat coastal acreage, driving land prices higher.

--- SENTENCE 575 ---
They are also small, so that any production is likely to be primarily focused on export markets.

--- SENTENCE 576 ---
As a territory of the U.S., American Samoa is held to environmental and labor standards that can be stricter than competitors, making costs higher.

--- SENTENCE 577 ---
In this economic analysis, these factors will be considered across a range of inputs, outputs, and efficiency considerations.

--- SENTENCE 578 ---
Then, specific case studies of aquaculture operations elsewhere will be examined through a lens of differential economic conditions for American Samoa, with conclusions made regarding profitability in the islands.

--- SENTENCE 579 ---

# **CASE STUDY: TILAPIA (***Oreochromis niloticus***)**

Tilapia production is well-developed world-wide.

--- SENTENCE 660 ---
The extensive experience has led to advances and efficiencies in production.

--- SENTENCE 661 ---
And, while this production in concept could have saturated markets, the reality is that the demand for mild white fish fillets remains strong and the consistent availability of tilapia has likely increased its value.

--- SENTENCE 662 ---
Producing tilapia from fingerlings can be costly.

--- SENTENCE 663 ---
Analyses of start-up costs based on Philippine experience, indicated initial investment required of approximately \$65,000.

--- SENTENCE 664 ---
Approximately \$50,000 of this initial investment would cover such items as pond construction, and other land improvements, and solid waste and wastewater treatment systems.

--- SENTENCE 665 ---
An additional approximately \$15,000 would cover a vehicle and miscellaneous other equipment.

--- SENTENCE 666 ---
On top of these initial investments, annual production is anticipated to cost approximately \$125,000, including land rental, labor, energy and water use, fingerlings, feed, and miscellaneous materials.

--- SENTENCE 667 ---
Much of this cost comes from water and labor.

--- SENTENCE 668 ---
This expense is estimated to produce 20 metric tons of tilapia.

--- SENTENCE 669 ---
At \$3 per pound (typical price when sold in American Samoa), annual revenues are expected to be \$132,000.

--- SENTENCE 670 ---
Thus, profit margins are predicted to be thin but positive, and with a valuable community service in providing jobs and a source of fresh protein.

--- SENTENCE 671 ---
Given the thin margins, it is expected that there would be no discounted return on investment even after 20 years.

--- SENTENCE 672 ---
However, if the government or other sources believed the benefits of tilapia production to society were worth providing grants, the balance could shift.

--- SENTENCE 673 ---
It is estimated that a \$25,000 per farm grant would result in a 41% discounted return on investment in tilapia cultivation over the course of 20 years (Table 2).

--- SENTENCE 674 ---
Alternatively, the American Samoa Community College's Land Grant Aquaculture Program has been providing all resources except feed to farmers producing tilapia in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 675 ---
Feed costs approximately \$0.25 per pound, and 1.5 pounds of feed are necessary to produce a pound of tilapia.

--- SENTENCE 676 ---
Under these circumstances, the annual production costs drop to \$16,500 and result in a nearly 2000% discounted return on investment over 20 years (100% per year on average).

--- SENTENCE 677 ---
These figures show that, without subsidies, tilapia is a challenging industry.

--- SENTENCE 678 ---
With current subsidies, though, it would be highly profitable to expand.

The United States federal government has a longstanding policy of prioritizing the purchase of domestically produced goods, including food items, for use by federal agencies, including the military.

--- SENTENCE 747 ---
This policy, often referred to as the Buy American Act, was enacted in 1933 and is intended to support domestic industries and protect American jobs.

--- SENTENCE 748 ---
Products made in American Samoa support this policy.[3](#page-67-0)

--- SENTENCE 749 ---

## **Buy American Act (41 U.S.C. 10a-c)**

The BAA requires federal agencies to give preference to domestic end products in their procurement processes.

--- SENTENCE 751 ---
In the context of food, this means agencies are required to prioritize purchasing food that is grown and processed within the United States, provided that the food is available at a reasonable cost and is of satisfactory quality.

--- SENTENCE 752 ---
This could mean that aquaculture and mariculture products grown in American Samoa may be required to supplant the current products at the local Army Reserves.

--- SENTENCE 753 ---
The Act does not ban foreign purchases, but adds a price preference to domestic products to make them more competitive in the bidding process.

--- SENTENCE 754 ---
The price preference adds a specified percentage to the price of any foreign products, including import duties, and then measures this adjusted price against the domestic product.

--- SENTENCE 755 ---
For example, large businesses get a 20 percent price preference, and small businesses get a 30 percent price preference.[4](#page-67-1)

--- SENTENCE 756 ---
This still means pricing is important, and a domestic product that surpasses a foreign product's price, even with the added price preference, may fail to secure the contract.

--- SENTENCE 757 ---
But it provides a huge advantage to domestic products in procurement.

#### **The Berry Amendment (10 U.S.C. §2533a)**

Another critical piece of legislation that impacts military food procurement is the Berry Amendment, which requires the Department of Defense to give preference to domestically produced, manufactured, or home-grown products, including food.

--- SENTENCE 758 ---
This is not a price preference, as in the BAA, but a prohibition on purchasing foreign products except in limited circumstances.

--- SENTENCE 759 ---
Only if DOD cannot procure a product in the quality or quantity necessary may it seek nondomestic sources.[5](#page-67-2)

--- SENTENCE 760 ---
The most restrictive regulations apply to seafood.

--- SENTENCE 761 ---
The Berry Amendment requires that DOD procure seafood only from U.S. flagged vessels or obtained from fishing within the U.S. AND be processed in the U.S.[6](#page-67-3) This would apply to aquaculture in American Samoa waters, that is also processed in the territory, or elsewhere in the U.S.

# <span id="page-71-0"></span>**ANALYSIS OF REGULATIONS**

This report provides a comprehensive overview of the regulatory landscape for aquaculture operations in American Samoa, with a focus on both onshore aquaculture and off-shore mariculture.

--- SENTENCE 831 ---
It is intended to serve as a guide for government decision-makers, regulators, and potential aquaculture operators in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 832 ---
Aquaculture in American Samoa operates under a complex framework of local, federal, and international regulations.

--- SENTENCE 833 ---
The regulatory framework is significantly different between on-shore and off-shore projects, as the latter involves more intense regulation, especially at the federal level.

--- SENTENCE 834 ---
At the local level, the American Samoa Department of Marine and Wildlife Resources (DMWR) and the Project Notification and Review System (PNRS) Board play key roles in overseeing aquaculture operations.

--- SENTENCE 835 ---
At the federal level, several agencies have jurisdiction over aspects of aquaculture operations, including the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS), the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (USACE), and others.

--- SENTENCE 836 ---
Key federal statutes and regulations include the Magnuson-Stevens Fishery Conservation and Management Act (MSA), the National Aquaculture Act, and the Lacey Act.

--- SENTENCE 837 ---
Internationally, the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES) is particularly relevant for aquaculture operations that involve international trade of species listed in the CITES Appendices, such as giant clams, sea cucumbers, and corals.

--- SENTENCE 838 ---
Starting and operating an aquaculture operation in American Samoa involves navigating a complex regulatory landscape.

--- SENTENCE 839 ---
However, with careful planning and compliance with all relevant regulations, aquaculture presents a promising opportunity for sustainable economic development in the territory.

# <span id="page-71-2"></span>**Overview of the Federal Regulatory Landscape**

As a U.S. Territory, American Samoa is subject to federal laws, especially those related to natural resources and environment.

--- SENTENCE 841 ---
Many more federal regulations apply to mariculture than on-shore aquaculture, as the territory's coastal area includes delicate habitats for important species, marine mammals, and natural resources.

# <span id="page-77-0"></span>**Local Regulations in American Samoa**

# <span id="page-77-1"></span>**Overview of local regulations and authorities overseeing aquaculture**

Aquaculture in American Samoa operates under a comprehensive local regulatory framework that is designed to ensure that aquaculture operations are conducted responsibly and sustainably, with due consideration for environmental protection, conservation of resources, and public health.

--- SENTENCE 973 ---
However, given the realities on the ground, it is not always clear which regulations apply, and who is tasked with enforcing them.

--- SENTENCE 974 ---
Aquaculture in American Samoa is enforced through two permitting schemes: the aquaculture permit and PNRS.

--- SENTENCE 975 ---
The primary local authority overseeing aquaculture in American Samoa is DMWR, which is responsible for the management and conservation of the territory's marine and wildlife resources.

--- SENTENCE 976 ---
As part of its mandate, DMWR issues permits for various aquaculture activities.

--- SENTENCE 977 ---
All aquaculture operations in the territory require an aquaculture permit from DMWR under ASAC 24.0980.

--- SENTENCE 978 ---
This permit covers a wide range of aquaculture activities, and there may be a special aquaculture permit for giant claims (ASAC 24.0953).

--- SENTENCE 979 ---
The DMWR has applications for these permits at its offices.

--- SENTENCE 980 ---
The other major permit is managed and issued by the American Samoa Coastal Management Program's (ASCMP)

--- SENTENCE 981 ---
Project Notification and Review System (PNRS) Board.

--- SENTENCE 982 ---
The PNRS Board has jurisdiction over any land use development in the territory's coastal zone, which for American Samoa, encompasses the entire island territory.

--- SENTENCE 983 ---
ASCMP also provides federal consistency reviews for any federal or federally funded project, but these reviews are parallel and part of the PNRS Board review process.

--- SENTENCE 984 ---
The agencies on the Board are: Department of Commerce (as represented by ASCMP), American Samoa EPA, American Samoa Historic Preservation Office, the American Samoa Power Authority, the Department of Health, DMWR, the Department of Parks and Recreation, and the Department of Public Works.

--- SENTENCE 985 ---
Part of the PNRS Board's task is to evaluate a project's impact on the environment.

--- SENTENCE 986 ---
This includes avoiding water pollution and the proper disposal of solid waste.

--- SENTENCE 987 ---
Dead fish from aquaculture operations are classified as solid waste under American Samoa's Solid Waste regulations (A.S.A.C. Title 25, Chapter 5).

--- SENTENCE 988 ---
Dead animals, including fish, must be removed within a reasonable time after death, or before they constitute a nuisance (A.S.A.C. 25.0507(a)).

--- SENTENCE 989 ---
Dead animals are to be accepted at disposal facilities during operating hours (A.S.A.C. 25.0507(c) and (d)).

--- SENTENCE 990 ---
The volume of dead fish is a deciding factor in how the fish should be disposed of.

--- SENTENCE 991 ---
Failure to discard solid waste, or otherwise contaminate the water would be a violation of American Samoa's Environmental Quality Act (A.S.C.A.

--- SENTENCE 992 ---
Title 24, Chapter 1).

--- SENTENCE 993 ---
"water pollution" is defined as the presence in the water of various materials that can harm the environment or aquatic life (A.S.C.A. 24.0103(a)(22)).

--- SENTENCE 994 ---
The penalty for the discharge of water pollutants is \$500 for each initial separate violation, and \$1,000 for each subsequent separate violation.

--- SENTENCE 995 ---
Each day of violation constitutes a separate offense (A.S.C.A. 24.0167).

--- SENTENCE 996 ---
Since ASCMP managed the program under the federal Coastal Zone Management Act, it has certain authorities even over federal projects.

--- SENTENCE 997 ---
It is possible that other local permits may be required in certain cases.

--- SENTENCE 998 ---
For example, an operation involving a large construction project, or a building, may require a building permit from the Department of Public Works, or zoning approval from the Department of Commerce.

--- SENTENCE 999 ---
If the operation is a business, it may require a business license from the Department of Revenue, and other related approvals.

- Corals: Many species of corals are listed in Appendix II of CITES.

--- SENTENCE 1026 ---
As a U.S. Territory, such permits would be issued by the U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service's Division of Management Authority.

--- SENTENCE 1028 ---

--- SENTENCE 1030 ---
World Organisation for Animal Health (OIE) Aquatic Animal Health Code: The OIE Aquatic Animal Health Code provides standards for the health of aquatic animals, including those used in aquaculture.

--- SENTENCE 1031 ---

--- SENTENCE 1032 ---
These international regulations and treaties provide a framework for sustainable and responsible aquaculture practices.

# <span id="page-79-0"></span>**Comparison of Regulations for Inshore and Marine Aquaculture**

Aquaculture operations can take place in a variety of environments, each with its own unique regulatory considerations.

--- SENTENCE 1037 ---
In American Samoa, these environments primarily include inshore (near the coast) and marine (in the ocean) locations.

--- SENTENCE 1038 ---
While many regulations apply to aquaculture operations in general, there are some key differences between inshore and marine aquaculture that should be noted.

#### **Inshore Aquaculture**

Inshore aquaculture operations are typically located in coastal waters or other bodies of water close to land.

--- SENTENCE 1039 ---
These operations are often more accessible and easier to monitor, but they may also be more likely to interact with human activities and terrestrial ecosystems.

--- SENTENCE 1040 ---
Land Use Permits: In-shore aquaculture operations may require land use permits, particularly if they involve the construction of facilities on land or in coastal waters.

--- SENTENCE 1041 ---
These permits are issued by the Project Notification and Review System (PNRS) Board and require a detailed application process (ASAC 26.0207).

--- SENTENCE 1042 ---
In-shore aquaculture operations are subject to solid waste disposal regulations, which require the proper disposal of dead fish and other waste products (A.S.A.C. Title 25, Chapter 5).

--- SENTENCE 1043 ---
Special Management Areas: Certain coastal areas in American Samoa are designated as Special Management Areas, including Pago Harbor, Leone Pala Lagoon, and Nuuuli Pala Lagoon.

--- SENTENCE 1044 ---
Inshore aquaculture operations in these areas require a public hearing and are classified as major projects (ASAC 26.0221).

#### **Marine Aquaculture**

Marine aquaculture operations are located in the open ocean, often far from land.

--- SENTENCE 1045 ---
These operations can be more challenging to manage and monitor due to their remote locations, but they may also have less direct interaction with human activities and terrestrial ecosystems.

--- SENTENCE 1046 ---
Lease for Territorial Waters: Marine aquaculture operations in territorial waters likely require a lease (ASAC 26.0222(D)).

--- SENTENCE 1047 ---
This lease may include specific environmental requirements to protect the marine environment.

--- SENTENCE 1048 ---
Federal Regulations: Mariculture operations may be subject to additional federal regulations, particularly those related to the protection of marine mammals and endangered species.

--- SENTENCE 1049 ---
These regulations are enforced by agencies such as NOAA and the USFWS.

--- SENTENCE 1050 ---
Marine aquaculture operations must comply with water pollution regulations under the American Samoa's Environmental Quality Act (A.S.C.A.

--- SENTENCE 1051 ---
Title 24, Chapter 1).

--- SENTENCE 1052 ---
These regulations aim to prevent the discharge of pollutants into the waters of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1053 ---
In both inshore and marine aquaculture, operators must comply with a range of regulations to ensure the sustainability of their operations and the protection of the environment.

--- SENTENCE 1054 ---
However, the specific regulatory requirements may vary depending on the location and nature of the aquaculture operation.

Starting an aquaculture operation in American Samoa involves several steps to ensure compliance with local, federal, and international regulations.

--- SENTENCE 1056 ---
Here is a step-by-step guide to help navigate this process:

**Business Plan and Species Selection:**

--- SENTENCE 1057 ---
The operator should first develop a comprehensive business plan that includes details about the type of aquaculture operation, the species to be cultivated, the technology and equipment needed, and the projected costs and revenues.

--- SENTENCE 1058 ---

consider this carefully.

--- SENTENCE 1060 ---

--- SENTENCE 1061 ---
The operator should review all relevant local, federal, and international regulations to understand the requirements for your specific aquaculture operation.

--- SENTENCE 1062 ---
This includes regulations related to aquaculture permits, environmental protection, animal health, and the trade of endangered species.

--- SENTENCE 1063 ---
Part of this may be done with the relevant stakeholders, as most agencies will provide comments and review prior to the permitting process.

The process of obtaining necessary permits and licenses for aquaculture operations in American Samoa involves several steps and interactions with local authorities, particularly DMWR and the PNRS Board.

#### 1.

--- SENTENCE 1065 ---
PNRS Application

Any aquaculture project should start with an application to the PNRS Board.

--- SENTENCE 1066 ---
Since it consists of various agencies, PNRS acts a[14](#page-81-0)s a "one-stop shop" for many regulations.

--- SENTENCE 1067 ---
The application process includes a scoping meeting, where the applicant has a chance to speak with a permitting officer to go over the project, the regulations, and identify any special approvals that may be necessary.

--- SENTENCE 1068 ---

--- SENTENCE 1069 ---

--- SENTENCE 1070 ---

--- SENTENCE 1071 ---
The PNRS application is found and delivered at the front desk of the American Samoa Department of Commerce.

--- SENTENCE 1072 ---
The application packet requires several components, such as a detailed project description, site plans, and an environmental assessment (ASAC 26.0207).

--- SENTENCE 1073 ---
Fees for the application are based on the cost of the project (26.0207(I)(1)(b)).

--- SENTENCE 1078 ---

--- SENTENCE 1081 ---
Note that Mariculture is also a protected use for Pago Harbor under the DMWR regulations, ASAC 24.0205.)

--- SENTENCE 1083 ---

The PNRS Permit is not the end of the permitting process, but rather the beginning.

--- SENTENCE 1102 ---
As the permit requirements are identified, the applicant must then apply for any other relevant permits.

--- SENTENCE 1103 ---
Any aquaculture project will require a permit from the Department of Marine and Wildlife Resources (DMWR) under ASAC 24.0980.

--- SENTENCE 1104 ---
This permit covers a wide range of aquaculture activities.

--- SENTENCE 1105 ---
There may be a special aquaculture permit for giant claims (ASAC 24.0953).

--- SENTENCE 1106 ---

--- SENTENCE 1107 ---
It needs to be stressed that many regulations and procedures in American Samoa are not well defined.

--- SENTENCE 1108 ---

--- SENTENCE 1109 ---

--- SENTENCE 1110 ---

--- SENTENCE 1112 ---

--- SENTENCE 1113 ---
Who makes that determination?

These, and many other questions, remain unanswered.

--- SENTENCE 1115 ---
It is not because the review has not been thorough, but because *currently there are no answers*.

--- SENTENCE 1116 ---
It is very common for two similar projects to have two very different experiences.

--- SENTENCE 1117 ---

#### **Pangasius catfish**

The genus *Pangasius* includes several species of catfish that are commonly farmed throughout southeast Asia and are sold under a variety of trade names (swai, basa, etc.) around the world.

--- SENTENCE 1283 ---
These fish are tolerant to very high seasonal temperatures and can be grown at very high densities in pens or earthen ponds.

--- SENTENCE 1284 ---
These catfish do not tolerate salinity greater than 1% and so freshwater must be diverted for aquaculture ponds and facilities (thefishsite.com 2022a).

--- SENTENCE 1285 ---
Developing a broodstock is the chief challenge for these species, as they often rely on particular temperature conditions to trigger spawning.

--- SENTENCE 1286 ---
If they do not spawn naturally, spawning can be induced with hormone injections at the appropriate time of year (thefishsite.com 2022a).

--- SENTENCE 1287 ---
Eggs are transferred into growing tanks and the larvae are fed prepared food for several weeks, until they can be transferred to a growing pond.

--- SENTENCE 1288 ---
Most fish reach harvest size in approximately 8 months (thefishsite.com 2022a).

--- SENTENCE 1289 ---
In general, growth considerations are similar to tilapia.

--- SENTENCE 1290 ---
Pangasid catfish do not require complex or expensive feed, and can be grown with relatively simple facilities (ASCF 2022).

--- SENTENCE 1291 ---
Although it is a type of catfish, pangasids can not be sold as "catfish" in US markets due to regulations meant to distinguish it from the native ictalurid catfish species that are farmed in several states (ASCF 2022).

--- SENTENCE 1292 ---
However, this has not reduced the demand for these products and they are one of the top 10 fish species consumed in the US by volume (Global Seafood Alliance 2004).

**Marine fish**

Marine fish species present a different set of challenges and potential opportunities from freshwater fish.

--- SENTENCE 1294 ---
As mentioned above, marine species are typically accustomed to larger spaces, lower population density, and more stable water conditions that are found in freshwater environments.

--- SENTENCE 1295 ---
Marine aquaculture can be located offshore, but this requires complex engineering and greater logistical support.

--- SENTENCE 1296 ---
If raised indoors or in artificial pools, water quality, temperature, dissolved oxygen, and other variables must be monitored and controlled, and high quality filtration systems are required.

--- SENTENCE 1297 ---
A primary motivator of marine fish aquaculture is that many marine fish are premium products in the international market.

--- SENTENCE 1298 ---
If supply chain issues are resolved, high-grade tuna can be sold in Japan or elsewhere for a strong profit.

--- SENTENCE 1299 ---
Additionally, natural populations of many prized species are depleted and there is strong demand for aquacultured substitutes.

--- SENTENCE 1300 ---
The potential for high profits can compensate for the higher startup costs associated with these species.

--- SENTENCE 1301 ---
As marine aquaculture often requires freshly caught wild fish as feed, it is not suitable for food sustainability efforts; several pounds of small fish may be used as feed for each pound of mature fish that is harvested.

--- SENTENCE 1302 ---
This section provides background for several species that may be potential candidates for American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1303 ---
Each fits the above description in various ways.

--- SENTENCE 1304 ---
Canned tuna is already one of the major exports for American Samoa, but an aquaculture program aimed at higher grade tuna might be feasible given enough effort.

--- SENTENCE 1305 ---
Similarly, amberjack (*Seriola*) is a popular fish in Japanese cuisine and can fetch a high price when sold as sashimi.

--- SENTENCE 1306 ---
Groupers are another group that has seen a notable increase in aquaculture operations in recent years, largely due to declines or closures of wild fisheries.

--- SENTENCE 1307 ---
Lastly, the options for culture of parrotfish are examined.

--- SENTENCE 1308 ---
There have been few successful attempts to culture parrotfishes, but it may be worthwhile given the importance of this group to the environment and culture of American Samoa.

#### **Tuna**

Tuna aquaculture, often referred to as "ranching," is increasingly practiced in a number of countries including Australia.

--- SENTENCE 1309 ---
At current, it is not practical to spawn tuna in captivity.

--- SENTENCE 1310 ---
A tuna ranch begins by capturing juvenile fish and placing them in a large floating pen while still at sea.

--- SENTENCE 1311 ---
This large pen is then slowly towed to suitable a location where it can be anchored or docked for the long term (Global Seafood Alliance 2009).

--- SENTENCE 1312 ---
Captured tuna are fed squid, sardines, or similar fish during growth, and may be moved to additional enclosures if necessary (Global Seafood Alliance 2009).

--- SENTENCE 1313 ---
Harvested tuna are sold as steaks, or sometimes shipped whole to Japan where they can obtain a high price as sashimi.

--- SENTENCE 1314 ---
Each season, additional juveniles are caught and added to the ranch.

--- SENTENCE 1315 ---
Farming tuna is complex and expensive, and is not economically viable for common canned species like skipjack and albacore tuna, and instead it focuses on high priced sashimi species.

--- SENTENCE 1316 ---
Yellowtail tuna is the most commonly encountered species in American Samoa that would be suitable for this kind of aquaculture (WPFMC 2021).

--- SENTENCE 1317 ---
Although the tuna industry is well established, an aquaculture operation would require significant investments in infrastructure for the various pens, as well as the boats needed to catch the initial stock of juveniles.

--- SENTENCE 1318 ---
While feed can be imported, it is more economical to source these items from local fisheries and it is unclear if the current fleet in American Samoa is positioned to provide these items in sufficient quantities.

--- SENTENCE 1319 ---
In contrast to full-cycle aquaculture where animals are bred in tanks, the practice of ranching has been criticized for its potential adverse effects on wild populations.

--- SENTENCE 1320 ---
Removing juveniles from the wild population may contribute to the overall decline of tuna stocks worldwide (Global Seafood Alliance 2009).

--- SENTENCE 1321 ---
Aquaculture operations of this sort may also provoke resistance by competing with local fisheries for a valuable and limited species.

--- SENTENCE 1322 ---
However, the demand for this kind of tuna is very high and there is significant ongoing research aimed at improving the environmental and economic sustainability of ranching operations (Bonafide 2019).

#### **Amberjack**

Amberjack can refer to any of several species of fish from the genus *Seriola*.

--- SENTENCE 1323 ---
These are fast swimming carnivorous fish somewhat similar to tuna that live in tropical and subtropical reef environments around the world.

--- SENTENCE 1324 ---
Two species are commonly farmed: the Japanese amberjack (*S. quinqueradiata*) and the greater amberjack (also called Mediterranean amberjack; *S. dumerili*) (Herrera and Agius 2016).

--- SENTENCE 1325 ---
Japan is the leader in aquaculture of this group with most efforts focused on *S. quinqueradiata* (Frimodt 1995), but a number of Mediterranean countries have developed

successful aquaculture methods for *S. dumerili* in recent years ( Herrera and Agius 2016).

--- SENTENCE 1326 ---
In either case, the goal of aquaculture is to produce high-grade sashimi for a premium domestic or international market.

--- SENTENCE 1327 ---
As such, the technical requirements for the operation and the logistics of freezing and shipping the harvested product are complex.

--- SENTENCE 1328 ---
Amberjack fingerlings can be either wild caught or bred in tanks, though each method involves some complications.

--- SENTENCE 1329 ---
Amberjack have been successfully bred in captivity, though the process requires large tanks with excellent filtration and water movement (Herrera and Agius 2016).

--- SENTENCE 1330 ---
Even in successful farms, fish often must be induced to spawn via hormone injections.

--- SENTENCE 1331 ---
A second option is to catch wild fry and transport them to tanks or pens where they can be grown to marketable size.

--- SENTENCE 1332 ---
This method is less complex and the fish often grow well in their new environment (Herrera and Agius 2016, thefishsite.com 2022c).

--- SENTENCE 1333 ---
However, it does require reliable information on spawning and growth of the species in the local environment, as well as appropriate fishing equipment.

--- SENTENCE 1334 ---
Captive fish can be fed high quality pellets when young, but must be given fresh or chopped fish as they grow in size.

--- SENTENCE 1335 ---
Fish reach a harvest size approximately 2-3 years after the fingerling stage (Herrera and Agius 2016).

--- SENTENCE 1336 ---
In order to control costs, an aquaculture effort in American Samoa would likely begin by locating a source of wild fingerlings.

--- SENTENCE 1337 ---
While the Japanese amberjack is not found south of Taiwan and the Ryukyu Islands, the greater amberjack is known to occur around American Samoa (Herrera and Agius 2016).

--- SENTENCE 1338 ---
However, it is not currently targeted in the fishery, and it does not receive a stock assessment or population estimate (WPFMC 2021).

--- SENTENCE 1339 ---
Considering these factors, additional preliminary research would be necessary to determine the feasibility of culturing this species.

--- SENTENCE 1340 ---
The almaco jack (*Seriola rivoliana*), also called the longfin yellowtail jack is currently being cultured by Blue Ocean Mariculture after considerable investment of time and resources has successfully domesticated broodstock of this species, reared fingerlings from eggs raised in a nursery then transported and grown these fish to market size in offshore cages near Kona, Hawaii.

--- SENTENCE 1341 ---
Researchers in Mexico did a study indicating that wild caught broostock (*Seriola rivoliana*) showed a better reproductive performance than domesticated broodstock in terms of fertilization rate, total number of spawning, monthly spawning frequency and total number of eggs produced.

--- SENTENCE 1342 ---
However, biochemical composition and egg diameter did not show statistical differences (P < 0.05) between the two groups, the domesticated group being grown from fingerlings obtained from Kona Blue sea farm (now Blue Ocean Mariculture) (Quiñones-Arreola et al. 2017).

--- SENTENCE 1343 ---
This species could also be grown the same way as Hawaii in American Samoa, but may require major investment in offshore cage technology and monitoring equipment.

--- SENTENCE 1344 ---
Potential farmers could rely on Hawaiian farmers for fingerlings; however, it is notable that most *Seriola* farmers in Japan rely on wild caught fingerlings grown out in cages nearshore (thefishsite.com 2022c).

--- SENTENCE 1345 ---
Another potential option for raising Seriola species in American Samoa is using Recirculating Aquaculture System technology, RAS.

--- SENTENCE 1346 ---
Researchers in Chile published a paper on the culture of yellowtail kingfish (*Seriola lalandi*) in a marine recirculating aquaculture system (RAS) with artificial seawater in 2014 where the author states they had produced fingerlings for this species at a commercial export scale since 2009 (Orellana et al. 2014).

--- SENTENCE 1347 ---
RAS technology offers the necessary biosecurity for cultivating non-native species, ensuring water quality control, and efficient waste management.

--- SENTENCE 1348 ---
Biosecure RAS also mitigates the risk of disease outbreaks and parasite infestations due to the absence of intermediate hosts (Orellana et al. 2014).

--- SENTENCE 1349 ---
Modern closed RAS can function using artificial seawater and require less than 1% water renewal per day.

--- SENTENCE 1350 ---
These advanced systems enable the land-based cultivation of 'exotic' species with high commercial value, close to consumers, and with no discharge of nutrients and organic matter into natural ecosystems when combined with multi-trophic integrated aquaculture (IMTA).

--- SENTENCE 1351 ---
Additionally, these systems allow for product traceability.

--- SENTENCE 1352 ---
Such technology is environmentally sustainable and contributes to the overall sustainability of aquatic food production (Orellana et al. 2014).

--- SENTENCE 1353 ---
Sustainability advocate Seafoodwatch.org recommends as a "best choice" *Seriola* raised in indoor RAS systems with wastewater treatment.

--- SENTENCE 1354 ---
They also report that *Seriola* reared in marine cages is also a "good alternative" in those countries with strong fisheries protections (Monterey Bay Aquarium Seafood Watch 2023).

--- SENTENCE 1355 ---
RAS technology is still expensive and typically requires high amounts of electricity to run pumps, cooling equipment and back up generators.

#### **Grouper**

Groupers (*Epinephelinae*) are a widespread group of moderate to large fish that dwell in tropical and subtropical reef habitats.

--- SENTENCE 1356 ---
In its natural habitat, the fish feeds on a variety of crustaceans and smaller fish, and groupers are often among the largest predatory fish on many reefs.

--- SENTENCE 1357 ---
Some species are particularly colorful and are prized by aquarists.

--- SENTENCE 1358 ---
Grouper is a popular species for consumption around the world and is typically sold at a high price (thefishsite.com 2022d).

--- SENTENCE 1359 ---
Many species of groupers have been overfished in their native habitats which has led to a significant interest in aquaculture efforts in the last 20 years, with successful operations established in Thailand, Australia, and the United States.

--- SENTENCE 1360 ---
The chief obstacle to grouper aquaculture is the need to develop a broodstock.

--- SENTENCE 1361 ---
While it is possible to purchase fingerlings from other producers or harvest them from the wild, this should only be a temporary measure.

--- SENTENCE 1362 ---
Many species of grouper display sequential hermaphroditism, usually with individuals beginning life as female and they may undergo several spawning cycles as female until a certain size is reached and the fish develop into males (ASCF 2023).

--- SENTENCE 1363 ---
While the sex of fish can be manipulated with hormone injections, many individuals will not spawn during their first year in captivity and may require several years before they acclimate to farm conditions.

--- SENTENCE 1364 ---
Particular conditions or a special diet is often necessary to induce spawning, and a few years of experimentation may be required to produce a stable broodstock.

--- SENTENCE 1365 ---
Fortunately, future generations adapt to the captive environment and outputs can be expected to increase over time.

--- SENTENCE 1366 ---
Once spawned, eggs and larvae are raised in tanks until they reach the juvenile stage.

--- SENTENCE 1367 ---
At this point, they can be transitioned to outdoor pens located along the coast (thefishsite.com 2022d).

--- SENTENCE 1368 ---
Grouper grow slowly on processed feeds and fresh caught fish is often used to supplement their diet (thefishsite.com 2022d, 20).

--- SENTENCE 1369 ---
Several species of grouper are native to American Samoa and additional studies would be necessary to determine the best aquaculture candidate (WPFMC 2021a).

--- SENTENCE 1370 ---
As described above, the upfront costs associated with grouper aquaculture are high, and several years of focused effort must be devoted to establishing a breeding population.

--- SENTENCE 1371 ---
If this can be accomplished, grouper aquaculture can turn a high profit and helps reduce pressure on wild populations.

--- SENTENCE 1372 ---
The primary issue identified by DMWR with grouper mariculture in American Samoa is feed supply.

--- SENTENCE 1373 ---
#### **Parrotfish**

Parrotfish (Scaridae) are an important part of the local economy and culture of many Pacific islands, but they present a number of challenges to any attempts at aquaculture.

--- SENTENCE 1374 ---
Parrotfish are found on tropical reefs throughout the world where they feed on a variety of algae associated with rocky or coraline habitats.

--- SENTENCE 1375 ---
Larger species have crushing jaw morphologies that allow them to feed on coral directly and are an important part of the natural cycle of erosion are reefs.

--- SENTENCE 1376 ---
Many species are very colorful and the largest species are often popular attractions for divers.

--- SENTENCE 1377 ---
These characteristics, as well as ongoing threats to the health of coral reefs, have led to significant concern about many parrotfish populations and their harvest.

--- SENTENCE 1378 ---
The only successful example of parrotfish aquaculture was part of a large-scale research program on Palau, which was focused on environmental restoration and not food production (reef2rainforest.com 2023).

--- SENTENCE 1379 ---
This program found success by incorporating parrotfish into coral restoration and farming, with the intention of releasing the mature fish to bolster struggling populations (reef2rainforest.com 2023, ko-fi.com 2023).

--- SENTENCE 1380 ---
While not economically profitable, given their ecological and cultural importance, this may be an interesting option, particularly if combined with a coral growth program.

#### **Rabbitfish**

Rabbitfish *Siganidae* have considerable potential for small-scale sustainable aquaculture across Southeast Asia: They are relatively easy to grow, have high local demand and are herbivorous, feeding on a variety of freely available feeds.

--- SENTENCE 1381 ---
Rabbitfish are mainly cultured in the Philippines, due mainly to government investment in rabbitfish aquaculture research, they are also cultured in Egypt, India, Indonesia and Saudi Arabia (FAO 2022a).

--- SENTENCE 1382 ---
There are generally two types of rabbitfish, the brightly colored pairing site-tenacious type and the drab colored schooling type.

--- SENTENCE 1383 ---
These schooling species are important food fishes, typical examples are *S. argenteus* and *S. canaliculatus* (FAO 2022a).

--- SENTENCE 1384 ---
Of which the forktail rabbitfish, *S. argenteas* is found in the National Park of American Samoa (Botany.hawaii.edu 2023).

--- SENTENCE 1385 ---
Another native rabbitfish species listed in the National Park of American Samoa that is used in research aquaculture is the dusky rabbitfish or *S. fuscescens* (botany.hawaii.edu 2023a).

--- SENTENCE 1386 ---
Rabbitfish aquaculture is fairly well understood and documented.

--- SENTENCE 1387 ---
More importantly, this group is very abundant during its recruitment periods, making it easy to catch in the shallows and thus keeping down the costs of acquiring fingerlings (Teitelbaum, et al. 2008).

--- SENTENCE 1388 ---
Rabbitfish are herbivores, feeding mainly on macroalgae of the genus *Caulerpa* and *Sargassum.*

--- SENTENCE 1389 ---
Herbivores require less protein in their diet for good growth than many species of marine fishes.

--- SENTENCE 1390 ---
Their plant-based diet means that formulated diets for them can have less protein and more plantbased materials, which improves sustainability and lowers costs (Seale and Ellis 2019).

--- SENTENCE 1391 ---
Some species of rabbitfish have additional traits that make them good for aquaculture, namely schooling behavior, or grouping together, and mass spawning in large numbers (Seale and Ellis 2019).

--- SENTENCE 1392 ---
The non-aggressive and herbivorous nature of rabbitfish makes them an ideal species to be grown in polyculture pond systems with milkfish, mud crab or shrimp.

--- SENTENCE 1393 ---
They have also been cultured with grouper, with small numbers of rabbitfish added to grouper cages to keep the floating nets relatively free of algae and seaweeds (FAO 2022a).

--- SENTENCE 1394 ---
Culturing marine herbivores such as rabbitfish, which feed low in the food chain, is crucial for ensuring food security in developing countries surrounded by vast amounts of seawater (FAO 2022a).

--- SENTENCE 1395 ---
Farmers can save money raising rabbitfish because they can feed on a variety of natural food sources, such as macroalgae and seagrass, which may be available in coastal areas.

--- SENTENCE 1396 ---
Incorporating these natural feeds into their diet can reduce the reliance on costly formulated feeds.

--- SENTENCE 1397 ---
By implementing integrated farming systems

rabbitfish farmers can also be economically beneficial (Seale and Ellis 2019).

--- SENTENCE 1398 ---
According to Seale and Ellis suitable sites for rabbitfish grow-out cages will have these characteristics:

- Near to the farmer's house, making the cages readily accessible on a daily basis.
- Deeper areas or "blue holes" at least 30 feet (10 m) deep suitable for cage placement.

--- SENTENCE 1399 ---
- Well flushed by oxygen-rich clean water.

--- SENTENCE 1400 ---
- Sheltered from strong winds.

--- SENTENCE 1401 ---
- Far enough from rivers to ensure salinity is not regularly lower than 12 ppt.
- Surrounded by their natural seagrass habitats (Seale and Ellis 2019).

#### **Snapper**

There are over two dozen species of Lutjanidae found in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1402 ---
There is at least one species found in AS that has been cultured intensively for some time in Asia, *Lutjanus argentimaculatus*, the mangrove or river snapper (botany.hawaii.edu 2023b).

--- SENTENCE 1403 ---
It is a fast-growing fish that can be reared easily in captivity and can survive well in all phases of culture, from hatchery to grow-out (SEAFDEC/AQD 2018a).

--- SENTENCE 1404 ---
Mangrove snapper is considered as a euryhaline, hardy and fast growing fish capable of tolerating stress and crowding (Leu et al. 2003).

--- SENTENCE 1405 ---
This species also has potential for freshwater aquaculture in ponds or in floating net cages in marine waters.

--- SENTENCE 1406 ---
World production of mangrove red snapper in 2016 was 9,815 tons from capture fisheries, while aquaculture production was recorded at 10,240 tons (FAO 2018).

--- SENTENCE 1407 ---
SEAFDEC Aquaculture Department (SEAFDEC/AQD) in the Philippines recommends using net pens for snapper nurseries, feeding 4-5 times daily and harvesting at around 50 grams, then transferring fingerlings to grow out in ponds where pumps may be used to do water changes at high tide or maintain the pond level which would require a coastal situation (SEAFDEC/AQD 2018b).

--- SENTENCE 1408 ---
Most of the aquaculture production of *L. argentimaculatus* comes from Southeast Asia where they are reared in marine cages and brackish water ponds (Leu et al. 2003).

--- SENTENCE 1409 ---
However, the culture of this species is still exclusively dependent on wild fry (2003) where the supply is limited, seasonal and unpredictable (Emata and Borlongan 2003).

--- SENTENCE 1410 ---
This limits the sustainability of its aquaculture.

--- SENTENCE 1411 ---
Thus, a reliable breeding and fry production technique must be developed to ensure consistent production of good quality fry to meet the demand of the industry (Emata and Borlongan 2003).

--- SENTENCE 1412 ---
In 2015, Australian researchers were able to grow 4,000 fingerlings of mangrove jack, *L. argentimaculatus* from captive broodstock through induced spawning and stripping eggs manually (Vorotnikov 2015).

--- SENTENCE 1413 ---
Philippine researchers were able to acclimate wild captured fingerlings (3.1 g mean weight, 2.5 cm mean TL) of *L. argentimaculatus* to fresh water from 30 ppt seawater in seven days and found a diet of trash fish and formulated dry pellets to be the most cost effective to grow out size (Muyot et al. 2021).

--- SENTENCE 1414 ---
Another native snapper species that could potentially be cultured is the crimson snapper or crimson jobfish, (Hawaii: Opakapaka) (*Pristipomoides filamentosus*).

--- SENTENCE 1415 ---
Crimson jobfish—commonly known as 'ōpakapaka—is a species of snapper fish found throughout the tropical Pacific Ocean, including American Samoa, Guam, Hawaii, and the Northern Mariana Islands.

--- SENTENCE 1416 ---
They are one of the Main Hawaiian Islands (MHI) Deep 7 bottomfish and live at depths from 240 to 720 feet (fisheries.noaa.gov 2023).

--- SENTENCE 1417 ---
'Ōpakapaka is a popular and important species for the fishing industry in and around the Pacific Islands region.

--- SENTENCE 1418 ---
It is caught year-round and is highly prized for its mild flavor and premium texture.

--- SENTENCE 1419 ---
In Hawaii, smaller-sized fish is often prepared whole by steaming or baking, also for soup and sashimi (fisheries.noaa.gov 2023).

--- SENTENCE 1420 ---
The population status of the American Samoa Bottomfish Multi-species Complex, which includes crimson jobfish, is overfished and

subject to overfishing (2019 stock assessment) (fisheries.noaa.gov 2023).

--- SENTENCE 1421 ---
Opakapaka fillets are well suited for an array of preparations, including baking, poaching and sauteing (Hawaii-Seafood.org 2023).

--- SENTENCE 1422 ---
This would make an ideal candidate because smaller fish take less time to bring to market.

--- SENTENCE 1423 ---
However their habitat is marine and they typically inhabit hard bottoms at depths from 40 to 120 fathoms (fisheries.noaa.gov 2023).

--- SENTENCE 1424 ---
University of Hawaii Manoa researchers have reared captive bred pink snapper, *P. filamentosus*, since the early 2000s and are an important source of literature, seedstock and technology dissemination for the culture of this species.

--- SENTENCE 1425 ---
Clyde Tamaru et al. published a paper where they describe teaching high school students aquaculture techniques for raising *P. filamentosus* from embryos and the outcomes from that exercise (Tamaru et al. 2014).

--- SENTENCE 1426 ---
This could be important to the future, potentially using university resources and grants for outreach to the people interested in American Samoan mariculture.

--- SENTENCE 1427 ---
The primary issue identified by DMWR with snapper mariculture in American Samoa is feed supply.

#### **Crustaceans**

Crustacean aquaculture is another possibility and has requirements somewhat intermediate between freshwater and marine fish aquaculture.

--- SENTENCE 1429 ---
As with marine fish, one of the chief concerns is establishing a breeding program that can be propagated out each generation.

--- SENTENCE 1430 ---
However, there are many successful examples and even some commercially available products that can ease the early phases of any aquaculture operation.

--- SENTENCE 1431 ---
Tropical rock lobsters are an option that is becoming increasingly popular and may provide a good fit due to its presence in the culture of American Samoa.

#### **Lobster**

Farming of tropical lobsters of the genus Panulirus, known as spiny or rock lobsters, is practiced in Vietnam, Australia, and Indonesia.

--- SENTENCE 1432 ---
The simplest method involves capturing free swimming larval lobsters or "puereli" from local waters and then raising them to adulthood (thefishsite.com 2023c).

--- SENTENCE 1433 ---
This is the most widely practiced method, as most species have an extended pelagic period that may last up to 6 months before they move close to shore.

--- SENTENCE 1434 ---
A number of research efforts in Australia have successfully managed to breed lobster, but the technology is in the early stages of commercial development (Global Seafood Alliance 2010).

--- SENTENCE 1435 ---
However they are obtained, the puereli must be raised in tanks until they reach the juvenile stage and begin to walk on the bottom.

--- SENTENCE 1436 ---
At this point, they can be moved into larger tanks, or to cages located on the edge of wetland or mangrove areas (thefishsite.com 2023).

--- SENTENCE 1437 ---
While lobsters can be given formulated feeds, this may result in poor growth, and most successful lobster farms feed a variety of fresh fish as a supplement to other feeds.

--- SENTENCE 1438 ---
Depending on the species used, lobsters grow in these cages for 9 months to 2 years before harvest (thefishsite.com 2023c).

--- SENTENCE 1439 ---
The most widely farmed variety of lobster is the ornate spiny lobster *P. ornatus* (thefishsite.com 2023c).

--- SENTENCE 1440 ---
The most common species caught and eaten in American Samoa is the green spiny lobster P. penicillatus (WPFMC 2021), which is somewhat smaller than *P. ornatus*.

--- SENTENCE 1441 ---
While the P penicillatus is more commonly eaten and consumed, both species are native to American Samoa, and both species have been successfully farmed elsewhere.

--- SENTENCE 1442 ---
Additional study may help determine if either species is a superior fit for aquaculture in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1443 ---
While lobster is a popular food item that can be exported profitably, lobsters are not frequently marketed in American Samoa (WPFMC 2021).

--- SENTENCE 1444 ---
Instead, they are often caught on a small scale by local fisherman or divers and traded or bartered, usually to be used in holiday dishes or at other special occasions.

--- SENTENCE 1445 ---
While the lack of a local market may be a hindrance in the early stages of developing a large aquaculture operation, it also suggests that a smaller scale effort focused on food security might be viable.

#### **Mollusks**

Aquaculture of sessile mollusks presents a different set of potential benefits and challenges from any of the types discussed thus far.

--- SENTENCE 1446 ---
These animals are filter feeders and typically do not require feed or enclosures, making their hatcheries very simple to build and operate.

--- SENTENCE 1447 ---
However, yields are typically lower and most of these organisms grow and mature slowly, requiring at least two years to reach marketable size.

--- SENTENCE 1448 ---
Giant clams (faisua) and oysters are discussed above.

--- SENTENCE 1449 ---
Here we discuss pearl aquaculture.

--- SENTENCE 1450 ---
Cultured pearls can be a lucrative field but it requires a large investment and entails significant economic risks.

#### **Pearl Oysters**

Pearls are produced when sand or grit becomes lodged in the mantle cavity of a bivalve mollusk.

--- SENTENCE 1451 ---
Over time, the grit is covered in a smooth material called nacre (mother of pearl) which accumulates in layers over time.

--- SENTENCE 1452 ---
Natural pearls are very rare, but they can be cultured by introducing a small irritant such as a tiny plastic bead into an oyster to stimulate the process.

--- SENTENCE 1453 ---
This process is carried out by skilled technicians who must individually implant every oyster and typically command high rates for their services.

--- SENTENCE 1454 ---
The oysters can then be attached to ropes, cages, or platforms that are placed at an appropriate growth location.

--- SENTENCE 1455 ---
After 2-3 years, the implanted pearl can be harvested, cleaned, assessed for quality, and hopefully sold.

--- SENTENCE 1456 ---
Only certain species of oyster are suitable for culturing pearls, such as members of the genus *Pinctada*.

--- SENTENCE 1457 ---
These oysters typically grow at deeper depths somewhat below coral reefs and only a few specimens have been observed in American Samoa (Haws et al. 2020).

--- SENTENCE 1458 ---
Since this genus does not appear to be common, a hatchery would be necessary to produce the oyster spat in sufficient numbers for pearl culture.

--- SENTENCE 1459 ---
This increases the upfront costs, but hatchery produced oysters can be selectively bred to produce higher quality nacre and seem to tolerate the grafting process better than ones collected from the wild.

--- SENTENCE 1460 ---
Culturing pearls requires a considerable investment in infrastructure and expertise and entails considerable risk.

--- SENTENCE 1461 ---
After 2-3 years, the first pearls can be harvested, but it may take 2-3 harvests to begin making money (Haws 2002).

--- SENTENCE 1462 ---
Even in a successful farm, upwards of 90% of cultured pearls may be below minimal quality and unmarketable.

--- SENTENCE 1463 ---
The entire profit of the enterprise is thus dependent on the production of a few high quality pearls and can fail due to mistakes or inattention at any point in the chain.

--- SENTENCE 1464 ---
Pearls also make an attractive target for thieves and the security of the growth site needs to be considered.

#### **Ornamental Products**

While food production is the chief reason to promote aquaculture and mariculture, a variety of other businesses make use of marine species and the environment.

--- SENTENCE 1465 ---
The nature of these enterprises vary in terms of complexity and their community impact.

--- SENTENCE 1466 ---
For the most part, these businesses are aimed at the production of high quality goods for export, and may have little community involvement beyond hiring labor.

--- SENTENCE 1467 ---
As such, logistics, advertising, and other considerations are likely to play a large role in the success or failure of these projects.

--- SENTENCE 1468 ---
Many involve the sale of live animals, which involves a carefully planned system for transporting the product to customers.

--- SENTENCE 1469 ---
Residents we spoke to did not express much interest in ornamental products, despite the ease of live rock propagation.

--- SENTENCE 1470 ---
This document outlines a number of non-agricultural aquaculture businesses that are potentially possible in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1471 ---
Live rock is a necessary component of saltwater aquaria, and it can be produced very easily.

--- SENTENCE 1472 ---
Coral cultivation is often complex and requires a high level of technical expertise, but can be very lucrative.

--- SENTENCE 1473 ---
Similarly, ornamental fish require considerable experience to care and raise, but can be sold internationally at a high profit.

#### **Live Rock**

Live rock is rock that has been colonized by various forms of marine microorganisms, particularly certain types of bacteria and algae.

--- SENTENCE 1474 ---
When placed in a saltwater aquarium, these organisms will help to process waste products produced by animals in the tank.

--- SENTENCE 1475 ---
The rocks are also used as structural elements to make crevasses for fish and invertebrates in the aquarium or as attachment points for corals, and some may provide aesthetic value on their own.

--- SENTENCE 1476 ---
Many aquarium guides recommend that a pound of live rock be used for each gallon of water in the aquarium, meaning that all but the smallest aquaria require a significant investment in rock.

--- SENTENCE 1477 ---
Live rock is very simple to produce.

--- SENTENCE 1478 ---
Suitable rocks are collected from around the local environment and are placed in shallow water near naturally occurring reefs (Haws et al. 2020).

--- SENTENCE 1479 ---
Volcanic rock that is porous and lightweight is best as the pores allow numerous sites for marine bacteria to colonize and lightweight rock is easier to collect and less expensive to transport.

--- SENTENCE 1480 ---
After a few months in the environment, the colonized rocks can be collected and sold to a commercial distributor.

--- SENTENCE 1481 ---
Care must be taken to ensure that live rock is not being obtained by damaging natural coral reefs (Haws et al. 2020), and some distributors will pay a premium for certified or sustainably sourced materials.

#### **Coral**

Corals are a popular addition to ornamental aquaria in many countries around the world.

--- SENTENCE 1482 ---
Coral is usually sold in the form of a "frag" or small fragment that can be placed in a home aquarium.

--- SENTENCE 1483 ---
Coral frags are produced by breaking or cutting a piece from another coral, and provided that the parent organism is large enough, this process can be repeated at intervals with no lasting harm.

--- SENTENCE 1484 ---
While the original parent coral must be collected from a natural reef, the goal is to avoid reliance on wild corals.

--- SENTENCE 1485 ---
While corals can be grown in outdoor areas through marine gardens, most corals do not grow fast enough under natural conditions to be profitable.

--- SENTENCE 1486 ---
Therefore, coral farms use high-end technology to provide optimized conditions that encourage rapid growth.

--- SENTENCE 1487 ---
Land based operations also protect from pollution, storm damage, theft, and other potential losses.

--- SENTENCE 1488 ---
A typical facility involves a number of shallow aquaria with excellent filtration and superior quality artificial lighting.

--- SENTENCE 1489 ---
Regular chemical testing of the water is conducted to ensure the proper conditions.

--- SENTENCE 1490 ---
Additional nutrients may also be added to improve the color and aesthetic quality of the corals.

--- SENTENCE 1491 ---
Some corals require supplemental feeding with cultured zooplankton, which must be raised in a separate tank with its own requirements.

--- SENTENCE 1492 ---
Coral farming can serve a dual purpose if some of the corals grown are used for restoration purposes.

--- SENTENCE 1493 ---
In fact, the driver behind many coral farming operations is to provide a sustainably sourced product.

--- SENTENCE 1494 ---
In the past, most corals were collected from the wild and the aquarium trade has been criticized for causing damage to vulnerable reefs.

--- SENTENCE 1495 ---
Although it requires additional resources and expertise, many facilities that grow and sell coral also breed ornamental fish and invertebrates.

--- SENTENCE 1496 ---
Because of the complexity of sourcing and shipping live marine animals, suppliers of aquarium goods prefer to purchase multiple product types from a single farm.

#### **Sponges**

Sponges, classified under the *phylum Porifera*, are exceedingly basic multicellular creatures.

--- SENTENCE 1497 ---
These organisms are sessile in nature, affixing themselves to diverse hard and soft surfaces.

--- SENTENCE 1498 ---
Exhibiting a wide array of shapes, colors, and sizes, sponges can range from a mere few millimeters to exceeding heights of 2 meters (Abel et al. 2019).

--- SENTENCE 1499 ---
They predominantly inhabit marine environments, occupying various depths; non-polluted coastal zones and tropical reefs are especially rich in this type of species.

--- SENTENCE 1500 ---
Many can be grown via mariculture in shallow depths of at least five feet deep at low-tide (MacMillan 1996).

--- SENTENCE 1501 ---
The demand for sponges has increased over time due to the demand in the areas such as domestic, cosmetic, biomedicine, pharmaceutical, pottery, art industry, filter, cleaning and industrial purposes, among other uses (Abel et al. 2019).

--- SENTENCE 1502 ---
Therefore wild sponge collection is becoming less common due to issues of sustainability.

--- SENTENCE 1503 ---
This issue provides an opportunity to transition from wild collection to mariculture of a variety of sponges.

--- SENTENCE 1504 ---
In addition, sponges are now being examined for their molecular properties in order to combat cancer (Mejia et al. 2013).

--- SENTENCE 1505 ---
One particular sponge in American Samoa (*Petrosia* sp.) was studied; some compounds were found to be broadly cytotoxic with limited selectivity for cancer cells, as they were moderately active against some human cancer cell lines as well as human fibroblast cells.

--- SENTENCE 1506 ---
Sponge mariculture is a global practice that, when carried out in the right locations, can prove to be an economical and highly productive approach.

--- SENTENCE 1507 ---
Sponges can be cultivated by utilizing fragments from existing organisms.

--- SENTENCE 1508 ---
The advantage of their filter-feeding behavior is that farmers do not need to incur costs for feeding, as highlighted in a report by thefishsite.org in 2023d.

--- SENTENCE 1509 ---
A cost-effective and easily accessible mariculture system can be established through suspended line culture.

--- SENTENCE 1510 ---
This method involves using horizontal lines anchored to the seabed, with sponges strung along these lines.

--- SENTENCE 1511 ---
This design facilitates convenient maintenance and harvesting processes, as outlined in works by MacMillan in 1996 and Abel et al. in 2019.

--- SENTENCE 1512 ---
Initiating the mariculture process is straightforward and careful site selection is a crucial factor.

--- SENTENCE 1513 ---
Sustainable harvesting of local sponges provides the initial seed stock.

--- SENTENCE 1514 ---
The larger sponges can be divided into smaller fragments, which are then allowed to grow until they reach the desired marketable size.

--- SENTENCE 1515 ---
This growth phase generally spans from approximately 15 to 18 months, extending up to three years.

--- SENTENCE 1516 ---
The chosen location should allow for uncomplicated access without the need for SCUBA gear.

--- SENTENCE 1517 ---
Additionally, it should be sufficiently deep to avoid boat propeller interference, while also accommodating tidal movements that ensure a constant supply of fresh water and nutrients.

--- SENTENCE 1518 ---
The site must be positioned away from freshwater stream outlets.

--- SENTENCE 1519 ---
It's imperative that the water's depth prevents the sponges from touching the seafloor and shields them from excessive heat during low tide ( MacMillan in 1996).

--- SENTENCE 1520 ---
Interestingly, reports from Marinecultures.org indicate that the establishment of sponge farms in Zanzibar has not adversely impacted natural coral or sponge populations.

--- SENTENCE 1521 ---
In some instances, it has even contributed positively to the enhancement of natural stocks (thefishsite.org in 2023c).

#### **Aquarium Fish**

Ornamental fish are another key component of the aquarium trade.

--- SENTENCE 1522 ---
Ornamental fish suppliers have been criticized for profiting from unsustainable practices in the collection of wild fish.

--- SENTENCE 1523 ---
A cheap and efficient method of collecting wild fish is to use chemicals such as rotenone to stun many fish on a reef and collect them as they float to the surface.

--- SENTENCE 1524 ---
This and similar practices result in high mortality rates among the fish and are especially damaging to invertebrates including coral.

--- SENTENCE 1525 ---
This has led to increased interest in tank bred fish that do not risk harming wild populations of environments.

--- SENTENCE 1526 ---
While many marine species important as food are too large to be practically bred in captivity, most ornamental fish are relatively small.

--- SENTENCE 1527 ---
Fish that are bred in captivity tend to adapt to home aquaria better than wild specimens and experience lower mortality rates.

--- SENTENCE 1528 ---
The requirements for raising ornamental fish for the aquarium trade are similar to those for corals, and it is recommended that any venture should aim to provide both products.

--- SENTENCE 1529 ---
A large number of indoor aquaria are necessary, and these must be equipped with high-end filtration.

--- SENTENCE 1530 ---
While some popular species such as clownfish are widely bred in aquaria, many tropical reef species require particular care and experimentation to induce spawning behavior.

--- SENTENCE 1531 ---
Rather than a single protocol, raising ornamental marine fish requires dedicated aquarists with a good understanding of multiple species.

--- SENTENCE 1532 ---
The facility will usually also need to breed a steady supply of zooplankton and other invertebrates as feed.

--- SENTENCE 1533 ---
As explained in the coral section, ornamental fish breeders usually improve their business if they diversify to provide a variety of products.

--- SENTENCE 1534 ---
Unlike corals, aquarium bred fish are typically not suitable for reintroduction to the wild and are not used in restoration.

#### **Octopus**

*Octopus cyane*a, also known as the big blue octopus or the day octopus is the main species harvested in American Samoa and important to local consumers via subsistence and commercial harvest.

--- SENTENCE 1535 ---
In general, productivity for this species is high (females lay between 150,000 and 700,000 eggs in a single clutch); however, little is known about survivability and reproductive timing (Raberinary and Benbow 2012).

--- SENTENCE 1536 ---
In addition, distribution and settling of the clutch is generally understood where larvae disperse to the water column for one to two months; dispersal is thought to be wide ranging with larvae traveling up to several hundred kilometers in ocean currents.

--- SENTENCE 1537 ---
Therefore, it is likely that spawning females contribute to the maintenance and replenishment of stocks at a regional level, not simply in the vicinity of the spawning locality.

--- SENTENCE 1538 ---
This complicated and poorly understood life cycle makes potential aquaculture of the species highly challenging and very technical.

--- SENTENCE 1539 ---
During our visits, people talked about harvesting octopus, but did not express interest in their mariculture.

--- SENTENCE 1540 ---
Octopus aquaculture from larvae reproduction to fully marketable sizes is not commercially viable at this time but the potential is being investigated worldwide (mainly Spain)

--- SENTENCE 1541 ---
since the demand for octopus has risen over the years.

--- SENTENCE 1542 ---
The main issue constraining development is difficulties in controlling the animals' reproduction and developing a sustainable diet.

--- SENTENCE 1543 ---
Captive breeding for larvae is not currently viable; therefore, brood stock is needed for outgrowth to a marketable size.

--- SENTENCE 1544 ---
Another challenge is the necessary food source; octopus need a highly nutritious diet (mainly crustaceans such as crabs and lobsters) for optimal growth.

--- SENTENCE 1545 ---
Harvesting or growing this food source can be expensive.

--- SENTENCE 1546 ---
Further research regarding proper lighting (type and intensity) needs to be investigated to optimize their growth on land.

--- SENTENCE 1547 ---
Finally, their captive environment needs to be spacious enough to prevent cannibalism (since they are solitary creatures) and secure enough to prevent escape.

#### **Sea Urchin**

The main species of urchin harvested in American Samoa are *Echinometra mathaei* or burrowing urchin.

--- SENTENCE 1549 ---
It is easily harvested in shallow waters around the islands and commonly eaten.

--- SENTENCE 1550 ---
Another species, but no longer commonly found, is *Tripneustes*.

# <span id="page-105-0"></span>**APPENDIX B: Freshwater Eel Aquaculture Information**

In this section, we discuss further information regarding aquaculture of freshwater eels.

--- SENTENCE 1576 ---
Due to the extensive knowledge Dr. Santos provided about freshwater eel aquaculture, we had far more information regarding this species than many others.

--- SENTENCE 1577 ---
Additionally, residents expressed a keen interest in rearing eels due to their abundance in the streams.

#### **Water Parameters**

Temperature: Ambient/warm (25–30°C); Cold during stocking of glass eels (18–20°C)

Salinity Freshwater: (0–0.5 ppt), pH: 7.5–8.5

Water Depth: 20–40 cm (nursery in tanks); 50–60cm (grow-out in tanks); 1.0–1.5 m (grow-out in ponds).

--- SENTENCE 1578 ---
There are three general main methods to grow eels.

--- SENTENCE 1579 ---
Extensive pond systems are the traditional form of eel culture in Europe (ponds of about 100-350 m²).

--- SENTENCE 1580 ---
A second method is a recirculating system that includes a system of square or circular tanks (25-100 m²), usually built of cement or fiberglass.

--- SENTENCE 1581 ---
The eels are stocked at a size of 50 g. Densities reach up to 100-150 kg/m².

--- SENTENCE 1582 ---
Extruded dry feed (1.5-3 mm) is fed automatically several times a day.

--- SENTENCE 1583 ---
Individual growth rates vary and grading every 6 weeks is necessary to reach a high overall growth performance (FAO 2023a).

--- SENTENCE 1584 ---
These two methods require continuous water exchange to remove impurities and fecal matter; they also require proper temperature ranges and control.

--- SENTENCE 1585 ---
As noted, these methods require 'grading' to separate larger eels to prevent cannibalism on the smaller eel brood stock (thefishsite.org 2023a), which can be labor intensive.

--- SENTENCE 1586 ---
Freshwater is an important factor that would need to be sourced from the deep-well pump groundwater or an adequate surface water supply; however, minimal water is required.

--- SENTENCE 1587 ---
In each culture tank, a water depth of 20-30 cm is maintained throughout the nursery stage (kuruko stage in nursery in tanks); 50–60cm (grow-out in tanks); 1.0–1.5 m (grow-out in ponds).

--- SENTENCE 1588 ---
Lastly, valliculture (another form of aquaculture) can be used in marine or brackish waters.

--- SENTENCE 1589 ---
Farmers use the seasonal migrations of the species to capture them returning from the sea into coastal, lagoonal or estuary zones to prevent them from returning to the sea (FAO 2023d).

--- SENTENCE 1590 ---
In Mediterranean systems, small eels (15-35 g) are stocked at the rate of 4-15 kg/ha.

--- SENTENCE 1591 ---
The elvers are mainly imported from France, Denmark, the Netherlands and Sweden (FAO 2023c).

--- SENTENCE 1592 ---
The success in the culture of freshwater eels lies in the proper siting, design of facilities and the use of appropriate support systems in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1593 ---
Glass eel collection should be done locally; therefore, collection sites would need to be identified.

--- SENTENCE 1594 ---
In addition, fishing gears such as fyke nets, scoop nets, fry bulldozer (traveling) and fine mesh nets, and other materials, such as light and oxygen tanks would be needed to collect them.

--- SENTENCE 1595 ---
A few people expressed interest in freshwater eel aquaculture on land for food security.

--- SENTENCE 1596 ---
American Samoa has a few places with already built concrete tanks, which can be used for freshwater eel aquaculture.

--- SENTENCE 1597 ---
However, canvas and fiber tanks can also be utilized.

--- SENTENCE 1598 ---
Concrete tanks can hold larger volumes of cultured eels.

--- SENTENCE 1599 ---
The shape of the tank has no effect on the growth and survival of cultured eels, although circular tanks are recommendable due to the eels constant swimming behavior.

--- SENTENCE 1600 ---
The tanks would require aeration.

--- SENTENCE 1601 ---
There are three kinds of aeration system designs that can be used in eel culture: airstone diffuser aerator, perforated pipe aerator, and paddle wheels.

--- SENTENCE 1602 ---
Airstone diffuser aerators consist of air tubes suspended above the tank, with air stones to introduce air bubbles into the water as well as keep the air tubes in place.

--- SENTENCE 1603 ---
Perforated pipe diffuser aerators are made out of perforated pipes set in the tank bottom.

--- SENTENCE 1604 ---
Most eel culture facilities employ airstone diffuser aerators.

--- SENTENCE 1605 ---
Grow-out culture facilities usually employ paddle wheels in concrete ponds.

--- SENTENCE 1606 ---
Adequate supply of dissolved oxygen is a vital requirement during culture, particularly with high stocking densities.

--- SENTENCE 1607 ---
The tanks also require water filtration and maintenance.

--- SENTENCE 1608 ---
Water from a deep well or from a yearround stream would be filtered prior to being pumped into the tank.

--- SENTENCE 1609 ---
Filtration typically uses sand, charcoal, corals, and pebbles.

--- SENTENCE 1610 ---
Water parameters include temperature, salinity, pH, and water depth.

--- SENTENCE 1611 ---
Freshwater eels are cultured in freshwater at 0 ppt, although during stocking, glass eels are cultured in 0.5 ppt for several days.

--- SENTENCE 1612 ---
Water temperature is usually at ambient temperature or at 25 to 30°C.

--- SENTENCE 1613 ---
However, during stocking, glass eels are cultured at 18 to 20°C for several days after stocking by placing crushed ice in the water to minimize stress caused by transport and stocking.

--- SENTENCE 1614 ---
This would require a substantial ice supply on island.

--- SENTENCE 1615 ---
The temperature is gradually increased after several days.

--- SENTENCE 1616 ---
The pH of the water is maintained at a range of 7.5 to 8.5.

--- SENTENCE 1617 ---
Due to eels' demersal behavior, the water level is kept low from 10 to 30 cm depending on the eels' size during nursery culture, although it can be increased up to 1 meter during grow-out culture.

--- SENTENCE 1618 ---
Water flow-through, recirculating and standing water (zero or minimal water exchange) are practiced.

--- SENTENCE 1619 ---
The flow-through system where water flows into the tank and flows out directly to the environment is the best practice, thus having a year-round stream could prove critical to success of raising eels in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 1620 ---
Facilities that use the recirculating system use sand, corals, fiber and biofoam as filtration media when water is recirculated and reused.

--- SENTENCE 1621 ---
Water exchange may occur once or twice daily.

--- SENTENCE 1622 ---
During water exchange, water is partially or completely drained.

--- SENTENCE 1623 ---
When completely drained, the tank bottom is cleaned manually or via a siphon.

--- SENTENCE 1624 ---
Eels are temporarily transferred to a separate container during total drainage.

--- SENTENCE 1625 ---
This will require an empty tank in which to hold the eels.

--- SENTENCE 1626 ---
Electricity for the tanks to run the aeration and filtration is necessary, thus communication with American Samoa Power Authority (ASPA) is vital for determining the best route for continuous power.

--- SENTENCE 1627 ---
This includes the option to have solar and/or generator backup power.

#### **Feeding Management**

Freshwater eels are carnivorous species that feed on a wide range of diets.

--- SENTENCE 1628 ---
They are generally nocturnal benthic feeders that feed on insect larvae, annelids, crustaceans, mussels and small fishes.

--- SENTENCE 1629 ---
Variety of feeds can be utilized during culture.

--- SENTENCE 1630 ---
Eels can be fed with commercial feeds, trash fish, or bloodworm *Tubifix sp.* or a combination.

--- SENTENCE 1631 ---
Commercial feeds used in eel culture come from local feed companies or from Southeast Asian countries, such as Japan, Korea, China and Taiwan, where eel culture technologies have already been developed.

--- SENTENCE 1632 ---
Crude protein content varies per country and brand.

--- SENTENCE 1633 ---
Feeds bought from Taiwan have a crude protein of 45%.

--- SENTENCE 1634 ---
Feeds from Japan have a higher crude protein content at 72-74% depending on the size of eels.

--- SENTENCE 1635 ---
Korean feeds have a crude protein content of 49-50%.

--- SENTENCE 1636 ---
On the other hand, local feeds have a protein content of 48-50%.

--- SENTENCE 1637 ---
Feeds are usually prepared by mixing commercial feeds with water and fish oil, which serves as

binder.

--- SENTENCE 1638 ---
Fish oil content is usually added at 3 to 7% per kg of feed depending on the size of the eels.

--- SENTENCE 1639 ---
Aside from fish oil, cassava powder can also be used as binder.

--- SENTENCE 1640 ---
Feeds are placed in porous plastic containers or containers covered with nets or wire mesh, with a wide range of mesh size depending on the size of eels.

--- SENTENCE 1641 ---
Feeding trays are suspended just below the water surface.

--- SENTENCE 1642 ---
Eels enter the feeding trays through the holes during feeding.

--- SENTENCE 1643 ---
The use of the bloodworm *Tubifix sp*.

--- SENTENCE 1644 ---
as initial live feed during the 1st few weeks of eel culture prior to feeding with commercial feeds is also recommended.

--- SENTENCE 1645 ---
Tubifix is usually bought from piggery farms.

--- SENTENCE 1646 ---
The use of the said live feed lasts for one to three weeks.

--- SENTENCE 1647 ---
Eels are fed with bloodworm daily or as much or as often as necessary or desired.

--- SENTENCE 1648 ---
Due to its origin, bloodworms must be washed thoroughly by placing them in holding tanks provided with running water and proper aeration.

--- SENTENCE 1649 ---
After the first few weeks of culture, commercial feeds are gradually introduced to the diet by mixing bloodworm and commercial feeds.

--- SENTENCE 1650 ---
However, it should be noted that the use of *Tubifix sp.* is not highly recommendable due to health concerns.

--- SENTENCE 1651 ---
The shrimp *Acetes sp*. can also be used as an initial live feed.

--- SENTENCE 1652 ---
Aside from commercial feed, they use trash fish or tuna waste as feed for eels.

--- SENTENCE 1653 ---
The usual species used are round scads *Decapterus sp.*

--- SENTENCE 1654 ---
(mackerel scad) bought from local markets.

--- SENTENCE 1655 ---
Feeds are prepared by grinding fresh or dried mackerel scad, and then mixing with commercial feeds and fish oil.

--- SENTENCE 1656 ---
Due to its nocturnal nature, it is recommended that eel culture must be done in a dark environment to enhance the appetite of eels.

--- SENTENCE 1657 ---
It is recommended that feeding should be in the late afternoon or at night.

#### **Disease Management**

Most of the mortalities in farms are caused by bacteria, fungi and parasites.

--- SENTENCE 1658 ---
Examples of such diseases include gill parasite infestation, fin rot and white spot (Ich).

--- SENTENCE 1659 ---
Mortalities could also be caused by improper water management.

--- SENTENCE 1660 ---
The change of feed type and brand may also lead to mortalities.

--- SENTENCE 1661 ---
Mortalities could also be attributed to unskilled workers and growers who use improper technology in culture.

--- SENTENCE 1662 ---
Disease occurrence is the leading factor for eel mortalities during culture.

--- SENTENCE 1663 ---
Due to this, disease management is important in eel culture facilities in order to attain high survival.

--- SENTENCE 1664 ---
Salt bath is the most practiced form of treatment and prevention in eel farms.

--- SENTENCE 1665 ---
Antibiotics and chemicals are only used during severe cases.

--- SENTENCE 1666 ---
It is important to segregate diseased eels in separate tanks during treatment.

#### **Harvesting**

Eels are cultured until they reach the legal size of 15 cm or 6 inches.

--- SENTENCE 1667 ---
By then, eels are ready for export.

--- SENTENCE 1668 ---
Culture period lasts for 5-6 months depending on the growth rate of the eels.

--- SENTENCE 1669 ---
For table size eels, it takes almost 10-12 months before 200 to 230 g is achieved.

--- SENTENCE 1670 ---
Full and partial harvests are both practiced.

--- SENTENCE 1671 ---
Partial harvest is done when several shooters achieve the target size faster than most of the cultured eels.

--- SENTENCE 1672 ---
Regular size grading is practiced sorting larger eels from smaller ones to segregate the shooters.

--- SENTENCE 1673 ---
This method is also done to avoid cannibalism and minimize competition of food among eels.

#### **Marketing**

The price of glass eels and juvenile eels (kuroko) are most often dictated by the buyer, although in several instances, negotiations are held.

--- SENTENCE 1675 ---
Prices of glass eels vary per season, depending on supply and demand.

--- SENTENCE 1676 ---
Although glass eel supply is stable all throughout the year, the species composition determines its price.

--- SENTENCE 1677 ---
After 5-6 months of culture, kuroko (*A. bicolor pacifica*) may be sold directly abroad or sold to other exporters.

--- SENTENCE 1678 ---
The market size for export is 6 to 7 inches, weighing approximately 6-7 g or at 150 pieces per kilogram.

--- SENTENCE 1679 ---
The buying price is USD \$1 per piece.

--- SENTENCE 1680 ---
The price of *A. marmorata* is 50% lower than *A. bicolor pacifica*.

--- SENTENCE 1681 ---
The major market for kuroko is Japan, followed by Taiwan and Korea.

--- SENTENCE 1682 ---
China is also a market, although on small volumes.

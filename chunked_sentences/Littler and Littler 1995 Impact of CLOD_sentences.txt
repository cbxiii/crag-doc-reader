## Impact of CLOD Pathogen on Pacific Coral Reefs

Mark M. Littler and Diane S. Littler

 A bacterial pathogen of coralline algae was initially observed during June 1993 and now occurs in South Pacific reefs that span a geographic range of at least 6000 kilometers.

--- SENTENCE 54 ---
The occurrence of the coralline algal pathogen at Great Astrolabe Reef sites (Fiji) increased from zero percent in 1992 to 100 percent in 1993, which indicates that the pathogen may be in an early stage of virulence and dispersal.

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
Because of the important role played by coralline algae in reef building, this pathogen, designated coralline lethal orange disease (CLOD), has the potential to greatly influence coral reef ecology and reef-building processes.

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
Barrier, fringing, and atoll reefs are com plex ecosystems that depend on calcareous coralline algae for the maintenance of wave-resistant fronts.

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
Crustose coralline al gae (an order of the Rhodophyta or red algal phylum) are plants that deposit a par ticularly hard and geologically resistant form of calcium carbonate (calcite).

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
These algae cement together much of the sand, dead coral, and debris to create a stable substrate.

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
Many have a prostrate-type growth form and look like red, pink, or purple cement covering large areas of the reef, whereas others form upright branched heads much like the corals.

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
Crustose coral line algae, particularly Porolithon onkodes in the Pacific and Porolithon pachydermum in the Atlantic, are the principal cementing agents that produce the structural integrity and resilience of the outer reef rim.

--- SENTENCE 61 ---
Coral line algae are important for the absorption of wave energy that would otherwise erode shoreward land masses and for the facilita tion of the development of most other shal low reef communities (1, 2).

--- SENTENCE 62 ---
No previously characterized diseases cause significant mortality of coralline al gae.

--- SENTENCE 63 ---
However, diseases resulting in bandlike tissue necrosis and colony death are known for western Atlantic (3) and Great Barrier Reef (4) reef-building corals.

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
Most ecological studies of algal pathogens have concentrat ed on freshwater phytoplankton or benthic diatoms; few data exist on the importance of pathogens in marine macroalgae and no pathogens are known for coralline algae.

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
Here we describe the growth habit of a bright orange bacterial pathogen that is le thal to coralline algae (termed coralline lethal orange disease, CLOD).

--- SENTENCE 67 ---
CLOD is similar to the coral banding diseases in that the pathogen occurs as a line or front that moves across the host and leaves complete ly dead skeletal carbonate behind.

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
Because of the critical role played by coralline algae in forming reef rims throughout the Indo- Pacific (2) and because reef-building coral line algae extend to much greater latitudes and depths than hermatypic corals (7), CLOD may influence reef ecology and reef building processes.

--- SENTENCE 69 ---

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
Isolated globules (Fig. 2B) placed on surfaces of healthy coralline thalli were also 100% infective (Table 4) and began to cause bleaching of the host within about 12 to 24 hours, confirming their role as propagules.

All 36 CLOD infections initiated from isolated propagules demonstrated rapid linear growth rates (Table 4), whereas none of the control patches became infected.

--- SENTENCE 108 ---
The maximum linear increase was 2.9 mm/day, with a mean  $\pm$  SE of 1.5  $\pm$  0.1 mm/day (n = 36).

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
The living CLOD bands reached widths of 1.8 cm and averaged 0.6  $\pm$  0.03 cm (n = 180) in width on the infected coralline algae.

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
Trace levels (that is, a single propagule per quadrat) of CLOD-infected coralline cover increased to 27.4% cover (significant at P < 0.05, Bonferroni test) during the first 16 days (Table 3) (13).

--- SENTENCE 111 ---
One year later, bleached coralline cover plus CLOD-infected area averaged only 0.7% cover in these same quadrats (significantly less, P < 0.05).

--- SENTENCE 112 ---
Concomitantly, there was a threefold decline in living coralline cover, a 10-fold increase in filamentous microalgae, and a doubling in frondose macroalgae (all three changes significant at P < 0.05, Table 3).

--- SENTENCE 114 ---

--- SENTENCE 115 ---

--- SENTENCE 151 ---
Limited thin regrowths of *P. onkodes* from small refuge holes in host thalli were observed in numerous cases after attacks of CLOD.

--- SENTENCE 152 ---
Only one other coralline algal escape mechanism with respect to the CLOD pathogen was apparent: very high wave energy habitats that may inhibit attachment of CLOD propagules.

--- SENTENCE 153 ---
The CLOD pathogen has not been previously reported, although it is neither easy to overlook nor rare.

--- SENTENCE 154 ---
This leads to the most likely hypotheses that (i) the disease has been around for decades or centuries but its virulence has recently evolved, (ii) it has been introduced into Pacific reef systems from some restricted, obscure location quite recently, (iii) it was formerly so rare or short-lived that no one noticed it, or (iv) it was always widespread but has gone unnoticed.

--- SENTENCE 155 ---
Given the extensive research on Pacific reefs and the fact that coralline hosts are so abundant and widespread, the fourth hypothesis is unlikely.

--- SENTENCE 156 ---
The first and second hypotheses combined warrant consideration in light of contemporary theory (17) and evidence (18), which states that natural selection acts on an infectious organism to maximize its fitness, as measured by the number of hosts infected.

--- SENTENCE 157 ---
Virulence, that is, the harming of a host, is of little consequence to the disease if the disease (for example, CLOD) can infect increased numbers of hosts (for example, corallines) in the process.

--- SENTENCE 158 ---
High virulence is therefore very likely to evolve (18) whenever a disease gains access to an abundance of new hosts.

--- SENTENCE 159 ---
Crustose coralline algae are among the most abundant and widespread space occupiers of photic zone marine hard substrata worldwide (19), and this pattern has persisted for millions of years (20).

The Neuron-Restrictive Silencer Factor (NRSF): A Coordinate Repressor of Multiple **Neuron-Specific Genes**

Christopher J. Schoenherr and David J. Anderson\*

The neuron-restrictive silencer factor (NRSF) binds a DNA sequence element, called the neuron-restrictive silencer element (NRSE), that represses neuronal gene transcription in nonneuronal cells.

--- SENTENCE 230 ---
Consensus NRSEs have been identified in 18 neuron-specific genes.

--- SENTENCE 231 ---
Complementary DNA clones encoding a functional fragment of NRSF were isolated and found to encode a novel protein containing eight noncanonical zinc fingers.

--- SENTENCE 232 ---
Expression of NRSF mRNA was detected in most nonneuronal tissues at several developmental stages.

--- SENTENCE 233 ---
In the nervous system, NRSF mRNA was detected in undifferentiated neuronal progenitors, but not in differentiated neurons.

--- SENTENCE 234 ---
NRSF represents the first example of a vertebrate silencer protein that potentially regulates a large battery of cell type-specific genes, and therefore may function as a master negative regulator of neurogenesis.

--- SENTENCE 235 ---
The molecular basis of vertebrate neurogenesis is not well understood.

--- SENTENCE 236 ---
To identify transcriptional regulators of neurogenesis we previously analyzed the transcriptional regulation of a neuron-specific gene, SCG10 (1).

--- SENTENCE 237 ---

--- SENTENCE 238 ---
This distal region satisfies the criteria for a silencer: a sequence analogous to an enhancer but with an opposite effect on transcription (4).

--- SENTENCE 239 ---

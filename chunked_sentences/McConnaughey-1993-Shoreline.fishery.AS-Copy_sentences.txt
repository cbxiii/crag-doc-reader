--- SENTENCE 13 ---
The Shoreline fishery of American Samoa in FY92

#### ABSTRACT

Due to the cultural and economic importance of the nearshore fish and invertebrate resources of American Samoa, The Department of Marine and Wildlife Resources instituted a program in 1990 to monitor the harvest in these nearshore waters.

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
This study uses comparable methodologies to a similar 1.5 year long study completed in 1979.

--- SENTENCE 15 ---
Although there has been a nearly 50% increase in the size of the human population on Tutuila since 1979, the overall fishing efforts within the study area have declined, even though catch rates in FY91 were comparable to 1979, and catch rates in FY92 increased almost 70%.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
The estimated effort in the study area in 1979 was 74,000 person hours, declining to 67,000 in FY91, and 41,000 in FY92.

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
Estimated island-wide catches for these years was 660,000 lbs, 440,000 lbs, and 334,000 lbs respectively.

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
The per capita inshore catch has also declined from 17.8 lbs/person in 1979 to 9.8 lbs/person in FY91 and 7.4 lbs/person in FY92.

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
The relative use of various types of fishing gear has changed markedly, probably in response to both changes in the socioeconomic structure of the island community, and to changes in the relative abundance of certain key species.

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
In 1979, all hook and line fishing methods accounted for 39% of the effort, raising to 72% in FY91, and dropping back to 23% in FY92.

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
The bamboo pole was the most popular hook and line method in 1979, though this gear type has been largely replaced with the rod and reel, and a simple hand line.

--- SENTENCE 22 ---
The use of handlines was not observed in 1979, though was the most used method in FY91.

--- SENTENCE 23 ---
Sales of domestically caught reef fish in the local fish markets has declined drastically in the last three years for which market survey data are available.

--- SENTENCE 24 ---
Much of this decline can be attributed to the availability of competitively priced fresh fish air freighted from neighboring island nations of Tonga and Western Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 25 ---
In 1990, 23% of reef fish sold in the local markets was imported.

--- SENTENCE 26 ---
In 1992, this percentage had risen to 78%.

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
The inshore reef fishery on Tutuila is estimated to account for about 7% of the total annual tonnage of fish, farm and livestock production in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 28 ---
Including imported foods, which account for 83% of the Samoan diet by value, the reef fisheries account for less than 2% of the total dollar value of foods consumed in American Samoa.

#### INTRODUCTION

Throughout their history, the people of American Samoa have relied on fish and shellfish food sources harvested on the reefs surrounding their islands.

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
Prior to western influence, fishing provided a substantial portion of the protein in the Samoan diet.

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
Although technological advances such as refrigeration, the availability of canned goods, and the gradual shift from a subsistence to a cash economy have created new options for meeting protein requirements, fishing remains an important part of the Samoan way of life.

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
The domestic fishery is comprised of two major components, the offshore commercial fishery and the shoreline subsistence fishery.

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
The offshore fishery, described in detail in Aita'oto et al (1991), and Craig et al (1993), has evolved considerably from historical times.

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
Outrigger canoes and sennit lines have been replaced with outboard driven catamarans and monofilament lines, through the effort of several fishery development programs.

--- SENTENCE 34 ---
However, marketing problems, resource depletion, and difficulties maintaining vessels have been all contributed to the fishery's failure to thrive (Itano 1991).

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
The present day offshore fishery supports both a commercial and a subsistence/recreational component, with approximately 70% of the total harvest being sold.

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
The shoreline fishery is primarily a subsistence fishery that targets fish and invertebrates from the fringing reef adjacent to the shoreline.

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
Unlike the offshore fishery in which participation is limited by the number of seaworthy boats, the shoreline fishery is highly accessible to the island's populace since most of the narrow, fringing reef can be reached on foot from shore.

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
Fishing takes place at all hours of the day and night by all sectors of the population.

--- SENTENCE 39 ---
The shoreline fishery is in a state of transition.

--- SENTENCE 40 ---
Traditionally, each village claimed ownership of their adjacent reefs and their associated fishing resources.

--- SENTENCE 41 ---
Permission for non-village residents to use these resources often had to be obtained from the local matai, or village chief.

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
Today, the main public highway follows the coastline all along the southern shore of Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
This road lays between the village structures and the beaches in most areas, allowing convenient public access to most of the village beaches.

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
In recent history, several villages have attempted to control through traffic along the highway, particularly during the evening "sa" or prayer hour.

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
However, villages have not been allowed to hinder through traffic.

--- SENTENCE 46 ---
Villages on the northern shore are more isolated, as they are serviced by branch roads from the main highway which terminate in the village, and thus there is no through traffic.

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
These villages maintain a much higher degree of control both in road access and in access to fishing rights on the reef.

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
Concerns regarding the current status of the shoreline fishery and frequent information requests regarding catch and effort data stimulated DMWR to implement a new monitoring program (Ponwith 1991).

## Recent Impacts to Coral Reefs.

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
Significant natural events have impacted the reef habitats in recent years.

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
Hurricanes have struck the island in 1979, 1990, and 1991, subjecting the reef environment to high winds and violent waves.

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
In addition, heavy rains and damage to the adjacent watersheds caused significant amounts of debris to wash down on the reefs.

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
Quantitative studies of these effects are lacking, though observations made after these storms shows that the corals suffered considerable damage in these events.

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
Observers stated that the reefs appeared to have been sandblasted.

--- SENTENCE 54 ---
Most of the table-top corals and branching corals were broken off and washed into rubble fields.

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
The massive encrusting corals were also significantly abraded.

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
The topography of the reefs was significantly altered by removing much of the three-dimensional relief.

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
In 1977-78, Tutuila suffered a massive crown-of-thorns (Acanthaster planci) infestation.

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
These starfish were observed to devastate the live corals, leaving huge areas of bleached, dead corals.

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
In response to a bounty program, nearly half a million star fish were removed from the reef and destroyed (Wass 1980).

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
The reef ecosystem has also been impacted as a result of the significant human population growth that has occurred over the last Rapid development and the years.

--- SENTENCE 61 ---
accompanying environmental degradation have affected the study area in many (1) coastal roads have been protected with heavily armored banks which encroached on the reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 62 ---
(2) land clearing for new construction, and new plantations on steep slopes have exacerbated the siltation problems which exist in a mountainous island environment.

--- SENTENCE 63 ---
(3) the amount of fish processed at the canneries has increased, which has increased the amount of waste the canneries dispose into Pago Pago Harbor.

--- SENTENCE 64 ---
A clear trend of increasing total phosphorous and total nitrogen levels in the inner harbor occurred over the period 1979 to 1987 (Chamberlin et al. 1989).

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
addition, low dissolved oxygen content due to high nutrient levels are suspected to be the cause of several fish kills in the inner harbor.

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
A recent toxicity study of the Pago Pago Harbor confirmed the presence of heavy metals, PCBs and pesticides in fish tissue samples taken from the inner harbor.

--- SENTENCE 67 ---
Lead concentrations as high as 7.9 ppm in fish muscle tissues and 73.8 ppm in fish liver tissues were found (AECOS 1991).

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
Health advisories were issued, recommending that inner harbor fish not be eaten and prompting health officials to test for lead levels in the blood of villagers who have eaten fish from the harbor.

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
The sale of fish caught in

the inner harbor area was eventually banned, although this ban has not been enforced rigidly.

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
Local stores say that they will not buy fish from the inner harbor, however several private fishermen still regularly sell fish from the harbor at an outdoor fish market in Fagatogo.

#### METHODS

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
The study design for this survey was developed by Bonnie Ponwith in 1990 (Ponwith 1991).

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
It's purpose was to repeat a previous survey done by Richard Wass in 1979, to document changes in this inshore fishery over the intervening 12 years, and to produce a data collection procedure which could be continued indefinitely into the future.

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
Due to personnel and logistical constraints, an island-wide survey was deemed not feasible (Wass 1980, Ponwith 1991).

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
The study, area selected was a 16-km stretch of shoreline, centered around Pago Pago Harbor (Figure 1).

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
It exhibits a range of reef health, from the outer villages such as Lauli'ituai which are impdcted primarily by fishing activities, to the heavily developed and polluted industrialized shore of inner Pago Pago Harbor.

!

--- SENTENCE 76 ---
Study area on Tutuila Island, American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
Individuals or groups fishing were sampled only if they had been fishing at least half an hour.

--- SENTENCE 78 ---
Parties that had no catch were recorded as such and included in the computation of CPUE.

--- SENTENCE 79 ---
Catch data included date, type of day (weekday or weekend-holiday), village, whether the trip was concluded or in progress, time of interview, fishing method used, number of hours fished at the time of the interview, number of gear units (rods, nets, spears, etc.), number of people in the fishing party.

--- SENTENCE 80 ---
The catch was weighed and counted by species groups whenever possible.

--- SENTENCE 81 ---
When the catch included a large number of small fish, the catch was weighed without counting.

--- SENTENCE 82 ---
The interviewers sampled only the portion of the catch which was kept, no estimate was made of the discarded portion, if any.

--- SENTENCE 83 ---
Fishermen often ate portions of the catch during their fishing trips.

--- SENTENCE 84 ---
No estimate was made of these fish caught but not available for inspection by the creel technician.

--- SENTENCE 85 ---
Fishermen on Tutuila seldomly headed or gutted their fish in the field, therefore fish weights reported here are in the round.

--- SENTENCE 86 ---
Methods of capture and preparation of shellfish varied, and technicians measured their catch as it appeared at the time of the interview, without any notations as to whether the recorded weight was of the entire animal, or just a portion there of.

--- SENTENCE 87 ---
As most of the animals were taken in the whole, shellfish weights presented here represent whole weights with two notable exceptions: 1) Sea Cucumbers where usually dissected and only the female gonads taken, leading to a severe underestimation of the catch of these species, and 2) Sea anenomies were often taken along with rocks they were attached to, leading to a serious over-estimation of the poundage of sea anenomies taken.

--- SENTENCE 88 ---
Data collection relied on locally hired technicians who had very limited backgrounds in biology or statistical procedures; however, they were familiar with the locally caught species of fish which they knew by their Samoan names.

--- SENTENCE 89 ---
For this reason, catch data were recorded primarily using the Samoan names.

--- SENTENCE 90 ---
This lead to some unfortunate combining of similar species in the data.

--- SENTENCE 91 ---
Sociological information was also collected during each interview.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
Participants were categorized by sex and age (14 or less, 15 or older).

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
Each fishing party was asked how much of the catch was to be sold or kept, and whether or not they were fishing adjacent to their home village.

# Palolo Data

Palolo, the coral worm (*Eunice viridis*), is an important species in the shoreline fishery and required an auxiliary sampling effort due to the brevity and magnitude of its appearance in the fishery.

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
Once a year at the beginning of the last lunar quarter of October or November, these burrowing annelids release egg- and sperm-filled body segments (epitokes) into the surrounding water.

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
The timing of this release usually occurs in a two night time span around the

last lunar quarter, (Kraemer 1902, Caspers 1984, Itano 1986, Itano and Buckely 1988).

--- SENTENCE 97 ---
There is a great deal of speculation and folklore associated with the exact timing of this event, and even an honor bestowed on the persons who could correctly predict the emergence of the worm.

--- SENTENCE 98 ---
Samoans, who consider the epitokes a delicacy, gather in large numbers around midnight on the predicted night to collect the epitokes from shoreline waters using scoop nets or long lengths of screen.

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
People typically wait on the beaches or in their cars, a few will venture out into the water every few minutes to see if the epitokes are present.

--- SENTENCE 100 ---
When epitokes are found, dozens to hundreds of people will rush into the waters for a brief pandemonium of fishing.

--- SENTENCE 101 ---
Palolo are harvested at various locations throughout the island, with the effort concentrated on several beaches which have been known to produce good harvests in the past.

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
Typically, a fishing party will consist of two to several people, some who hold lanterns and buckets, and others who scoop the epitokes from the water into the buckets.

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
A separate sampling effort was applied to the palolo fishery to accommodate its unique attributes.

--- SENTENCE 104 ---
A one-night survey was conducted in 1990 and 1991 (Ponwith 1991).

--- SENTENCE 105 ---
That survey only included the stretch of shoreline from Faga'alu to Nu'uuli which, attracted the greatest number of participants in the inshore survey study area.

--- SENTENCE 106 ---
In 1992, an expanded palolo survey was conducted to census all the likely palolo harvesting areas on the Southeast and Southwest shores of Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 107 ---
The palolo survey was also extended to include four nights in both October and November, centered on the last night of the lunar quarter.

--- SENTENCE 108 ---
This increased time was made to insure that the samplers did not miss the night of the palolo, and to obtain a more complete picture of this fishery.

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
Four crews were used to cover all areas.

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
Crew 1 surveyed from Tula to Aua, Crew 2 from the Rainmaker Hotel to Faganeanea, Crew 3 from Faganeanea to Coconut Point, Crew 4 surveyed the airport reef, and Crew 5 surveyed the Southwestern shore from Leone to Poloa.

--- SENTENCE 111 ---
Crews also sampled returning boat fishermen at the Fagatogo small boat harbor.

--- SENTENCE 112 ---
The harbor villages from Aua to the Rainmaker Hotel were not sampled as it was believed that no Palolo harvest would occur in this area.

--- SENTENCE 113 ---
Logistics and lack of personnel prevented us from deploying crews to the villages on the northern shore, even though anecdotal reports suggested that high densities of palolo had been seen in isolated spawning in past years.

--- SENTENCE 114 ---
Also, the survey of the southwestern shoreline was discontinued after two nights due to vehicle breakdowns, and that very few palolo fishermen were observed in those areas.

--- SENTENCE 115 ---
The palolo survey methods used in 1992 were similar to those described by Ponwith, except that both persons in the water and on the shore were counted, and the catch rate was based on a person/night basis rather than on a person/hour basis.

--- SENTENCE 116 ---
These changes were made for the following reasons:

- 1.

--- SENTENCE 117 ---
The peak fishing activity may last only 1/2 hour or so, making it impossible for limited crews to survey the study area obtaining counts of the number of people in the water.

--- SENTENCE 118 ---
It was also difficult to determine what proportion of the people observed waiting on the shorelines actually intended to fish.

--- SENTENCE 119 ---
If the worms did not appear on a particular night (as was the case), many of the would be fishermen would leave without actually fishing.

--- SENTENCE 120 ---
Also many people waiting on shore never intended to actually fish, but were rather there to accompany people who came to fish, supervise children, or just came to watch the show.
- 2.

--- SENTENCE 121 ---
Persons who come out at midnight in the anticipation of a palolo harvest could legitimately be considered participants in the fishery, whether their intent was to fish, or just to help or watch from the shore.

--- SENTENCE 122 ---
The cultural importance of the palolo fishery is likely greater than it's biological importance, and it is therefore important to document all participants in it.
-

--- SENTENCE 123 ---
An hourly catch rate makes little sense for this fishery, as a typical palolo fishing trip consists of a large amount of time spent waiting around on the beach watching for worms, and a short amount of time spent actively fishing.

--- SENTENCE 124 ---
To include the time spent waiting and searching for worms would lead to erroneous catch rates.

--- SENTENCE 125 ---
Consider the following hypothetical example: Year 1, a person comes to a beach and starts watching for worms at 12:00 PM.

--- SENTENCE 126 ---
The worms appear around 12:30 He catches 1 pound of epitokes, and leaves at 1:00 AM.

--- SENTENCE 127 ---
His hourly CPUE would be 1 pound per hour.

--- SENTENCE 128 ---
However in Year 2, the fisherman again starts searching for worms at 12:00 PM, but the worms do not appear until around 1:30 AM.

--- SENTENCE 129 ---
He again catches 1 pound of worms, and leaves at 2:00 AM.

--- SENTENCE 130 ---
His hourly CPUE would be 0.5 pounds/hour, a drop of 50% from year 1.

--- SENTENCE 131 ---
It seems more correct and more practical to consider the night as the unit of effort, and to report that the catch rate for both years 1 and 2 was 1 pound/night.

--- SENTENCE 132 ---
When the spawning was over, people would leave the areas in mass as quickly as possible, as it is the wee hours of the morning and they are wet, cold and tired.

--- SENTENCE 133 ---
Therefore interviews had to be conducted as rapidly as possible as people were leaving.

--- SENTENCE 134 ---
Extraneous questions (such as their age, when they started fishing or when they stopped) were eliminated in order to allow the rapid collection of the catch data.

--- SENTENCE 135 ---
We also found it difficult to interview unsuccessful fishermen as they wanted to leave as quickly as possible, and did not want to stop to discuss their failure with us.

# Participation Analysis

In a deviation from Ponwith (1992), fishing effort estimates are reported both in person-hours and in gear-hours.

--- SENTENCE 194 ---
This change is made for two reasons, 1) to allow for more direct comparisons to the 1979 study, which reported only person-hours, and 2) to present a more accurate picture of the human effort expended in this fishery.

--- SENTENCE 195 ---
Gear-hour computations are a better biological representation of this fishery, as it is common for a fishing party to include people who are not actively fishing.

--- SENTENCE 196 ---
For example, a fishing party may consist of two people one of whom fishes with a rod and reel while the other holds the catch.

--- SENTENCE 197 ---
The fishing power of that party is better defined by amount of gear they have, rather than the number of people participating.

--- SENTENCE 198 ---
Separate estimates for fishing effort were calculated for each effort stratum, which consisted of the following:

- 0)

--- SENTENCE 199 ---
Year (FY91 versus FY92)
- Time of day (Day-time versus Night time)
- 2) Day type (Week day versus week end or Holiday)
- 3) Fishing method
- 4) Village

--- SENTENCE 200 ---
The first step in estimating total effort was, for each stratum, to sum the total number of gear units observed and divide it by the total number of hours observed to produce a mean number of gear units per hour.

--- SENTENCE 201 ---
The second step was to multiply this value by the total number of hours possible for each stratum in each 1-year period.

--- SENTENCE 202 ---
# <u>Catch Analysis</u>

Catch estimation involved several steps.

--- SENTENCE 203 ---
First, a CPUE value for each catch that was sampled was calculated by dividing the total number of pounds caught by the total effort (where total effort equalled the product of number of gear units and the number of hours fished).

--- SENTENCE 204 ---
The interview (catch) database is organized in a hierarchical arrangement of strata, as follows:

# Level Strata

--- SENTENCE 205 ---
Vear, (User-selected range of dates for an analysis) Day or night fishing Day type (Week day versus Weekend day or holiday) Fishing technique, (Grouping of like methods) Fishing Method, (Rod and reel, hand-line, (etc.))

--- SENTENCE 206 ---
Habitat (Grouping of similar villages.

--- SENTENCE 207 ---
Village name

Total catch, (C), and total effort, (E) were calculated for each stratum and at each hierarchial level.

--- SENTENCE 208 ---
The ratio of these two numbers (C/E) was used to estimate the catch per unit effort, CPUE.

--- SENTENCE 209 ---
Expanded catches were generated by multiplying the CPUE for each stratum by the expanded effort estimate for each respective stratum.

--- SENTENCE 210 ---
In many cases there were not a sufficient number of interviews available in a given stratum at level 6 to compute a CPUE.

--- SENTENCE 211 ---
In these cases, the expansion program would look for a CPUE at level 5 in order to produce an expanded catch for a particular stratum.

--- SENTENCE 212 ---
If a level 5 CPUE was not available, then a level 4 CPUE would be used, (etc).

--- SENTENCE 213 ---
In this analysis, a minimum of two interviews would be required to complete the analysis.

--- SENTENCE 214 ---
For example, to estimate the catch by daytime, weekday, rod and reel fishermen fishing in the village of Onososopo, the program would first look for all interviews within the selected time period for day time rod and reel fishermen in Onososopo (Level 6).

--- SENTENCE 215 ---
If two interviews were not found, the program would then look at the level 5 CPUE, which would be all rod and reel fishermen fishing in the Aua area, which includes Onososopo.

--- SENTENCE 216 ---
If two interviews were not found there, then the a level 4 CPUE would be used, which would be all daytime, weekday, rod and reel fishermen within the entire study area.

--- SENTENCE 217 ---
Species composition estimates (by weight) were then calculated.

--- SENTENCE 218 ---
The proportion of each species in the sampled catch was multiplied by the total estimated catch to get the expanded species composition.

--- SENTENCE 219 ---
Fish weights are expressed as whole fish and shellfish weights include the shell.

--- SENTENCE 220 ---
# Palolo Analysis

Palolo data were analyzed independently from the rest of the data.

--- SENTENCE 221 ---
The procedure used in this study differs somewhat from prior

studies described by Ponwith, (1981).

--- SENTENCE 222 ---
Each fishing party interviewed was surveyed upon completion of their fishing trip.

--- SENTENCE 223 ---
The following data were collected: Total number of persons in the group including those who did not actually fish, and amount of catch for the group.

--- SENTENCE 224 ---
Palolo catch rates were then computed on a per person/night basis.

--- SENTENCE 225 ---
The total catch for a village area was estimated by multiplying the mean CPUE from the interview data by the maximum number of persons observed in that area for all the participation counts.

--- SENTENCE 226 ---
# Data limitations

Two data collection problems seriously affect this survey design: (1), logistics made it difficult to interview most reef fishermen who were away from the shore, and (2) it was often difficult to see nighttime fishermen, particularly spear divers.

--- SENTENCE 227 ---
The first problem made it difficult to obtain unbiased interviews of the fishermen's catch.

--- SENTENCE 228 ---
Most of the reef fishing occurred out on the reef flat, often as close to the reef face as possible.

--- SENTENCE 229 ---
Fishermen are often wading in knee deep water and are a couple hundred feet from shore.

--- SENTENCE 230 ---
Spear divers were usually swimming in deeper waters just past the reef face.

--- SENTENCE 231 ---
It was usually impractical and sometimes hazardous to walk out to these fishermen.

--- SENTENCE 232 ---
fishermen were generally interviewed only if they happened to be completing their fishing trip and seen returning to the shore by the data collector if he happened to be driving by at the right But persons fishing close to shore, or on docks were interviewed on a routine basis.

--- SENTENCE 233 ---
This problem adds an obvious bias to the survey results which is not corrected for in the data analysis.

--- SENTENCE 234 ---
This survey also suffers from low interview rates in several villages.

--- SENTENCE 235 ---
Often, interviews from other villages of persons fishing with the same gear type were used to compute an expanded

Poor visibility due to rain and darkness during evening shifts were the main factor affecting the accuracy of participation data.

--- SENTENCE 236 ---
During sampling shifts on nights surrounding the new moon period, the data collectors often relied on counts of flashlights since the fishermen, themselves, could not be seen.

--- SENTENCE 237 ---
If the lights were submerged and offshore, as was often the case for night divers, they could have been easily missed.

--- SENTENCE 238 ---
Rod and reel anglers who fish the edge of the reef often went for long periods of time without turning on a flashlight, making them difficult to see on dark nights.

--- SENTENCE 239 ---
Low estimates for nighttime effort would have a ripple affect on the rest of the estimation process.

--- SENTENCE 240 ---
They would result in low expanded catch and effort estimates for selected species, most notably for lobsters, alogo (bluestriped surgeonfish), squirrelfish and soldierfish.

--- SENTENCE 241 ---
Thus, catch and effort estimates for nighttime should be considered conservative, especially for the diving method.

#### RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

# Fishing Methods .

--- SENTENCE 242 ---
A variety of fishing methods were observed in use.

--- SENTENCE 243 ---
These were classified into seven gear types as follows:

Rod and Reel.

--- SENTENCE 244 ---
This gear type included the typical fiberglass casting rod and mechanical reel available in most sporting goods stores.

--- SENTENCE 245 ---
Baits included lures, live baits, feathers, and jigs.

--- SENTENCE 246 ---
Atule and Jacks were the most commonly targeted species, though other species were taken as well.

--- SENTENCE 247 ---
The hand line consisted of a length of line wrapped around a stick or partially crushed aluminum can.

--- SENTENCE 248 ---
Use of this gear type is not mentioned by Wass (1980) or by Hill (1978), but was the most often used gear type in FY91.

--- SENTENCE 249 ---
Fishing with hand lines is predominantly done off the docks in the Fagatogo area, but was also observed throughout the study area.

--- SENTENCE 250 ---
This gear type included any bamboo pole or stick with a piece of fishing line tied to it.

--- SENTENCE 251 ---
No reel was involved, which distinguishes it from the rod and reel gear type.

--- SENTENCE 252 ---
Gleaning is where people simply walk out onto the reef flats and catch or pick up whatever fish or invertebrates they may find.

--- SENTENCE 253 ---
Usually people will walk along the reef top at low tide during day light hours, probing cracks with a piece of wire for octopus, catching fish stranded in shallow tide pools, or simply picking up sea urchins and other invertebrates.

--- SENTENCE 254 ---
This is probably the most traditional fishery that people engage in, and was the most used method in the 1979 (Wass 1980).

--- SENTENCE 255 ---
Spear divers generally work the outer reef edge, though are frequently seen on the reef tops at high tide.

--- SENTENCE 256 ---
The equipment is simple, using swimmer's goggles or snorkel gear, and using spears of various designs to catch fish.

--- SENTENCE 257 ---
The spears vary from pole spears (Hawaiian Slings) to home made devices which resemble a bow and arrow.

--- SENTENCE 258 ---
Much of the spear diving is done at night using waterproof flashlights.

--- SENTENCE 259 ---
The fish are stunned and blinded by the lights, and are thus much easier to approach then they are during the day time.

--- SENTENCE 260 ---
The usage of scuba tanks was not observed within the study area, as this type of gear has not been adopted by most people due to its cost and lack of support on the island.

--- SENTENCE 261 ---
Most of the use of scuba gear at this time is by DMWR personal, or persons who learned the use of scuba equipment while living in Hawaii or the mainland, and may occasionally spear fish recreationally.

--- SENTENCE 262 ---
Throw nets or cast nets were used through out the study area.

--- SENTENCE 263 ---
In reef areas, they were usually used at low tide, often in conjunction with gleaning.

--- SENTENCE 264 ---
The gill nets observed in use in this study consisted of relatively short (100' - 200') lengths of monofilament gill nets.

--- SENTENCE 265 ---
Wass used the term "seines" to include:

"...

--- SENTENCE 266 ---
[monofilament] gill nets, throw nets, scoop nets and seines that were woven from cord from bark or coconut husks".

--- SENTENCE 267 ---
Thus Wass's usage of the term "seine" includes a variety of fishing techniques and gear which involved nets.

--- SENTENCE 268 ---
Wass considered "seines" as an active gear type where a net was pulled through the water, or the fish driven into the net, whereas he considered "gill nets" as a passive gear type which was allowed to catch fish more or less unattended.

--- SENTENCE 269 ---
As gill nets are generally used as in an "active" fishing method, Wass called them "seines" in his report, whereas they are called "gill nets" in this report.

--- SENTENCE 270 ---
The usage of the more traditional materials (nets woven from cord or bark or coconut husks) have not been observed since this study began in 1990.

--- SENTENCE 271 ---
The usage of gill nets is at times banned in some areas by the village councils.

--- SENTENCE 272 ---
This happened in Utulei and Faga'alu in FY91 during the atule run when there were complaints that too many fish were being caught by gill net fishermen, and not allowing the rod and reel fishermen their "fair share".

--- SENTENCE 273 ---
Enforcement of village rules such as this is not within the jurisdiction of the Department of Marine and Wildlife Resources.

--- SENTENCE 274 ---
Wass also describes several techniques that were not observed in this study.

--- SENTENCE 275 ---
Most of these involved using pens, weirs, or traps of some variety and large numbers of people to drive the fish into trap where they were harvested.

--- SENTENCE 276 ---
Wass (1980) states that many of the traditional fishing techniques required a group of people, and much of the enjoyment the Samoan's derived from fishing related to its social aspects.

--- SENTENCE 288 ---
The island-wide extrapolation should be considered only as a best guess for the following reasons: (1) Villages in the study area are not selected at random, but rather represent a string of villages in the more industrialized harbor area which could be conveniently surveyed.

--- SENTENCE 289 ---
Outlying villages may have considerably different fishing patterns.

--- SENTENCE 290 ---
(2) Catch per capita estimates were made by dividing the total catch by village by the village population.

--- SENTENCE 291 ---
A more accurate estimate would be to include only the catch by residents of the village.

--- SENTENCE 292 ---
(3) The highly populated Inner Harbor areas receive a reduced fishing effort due to the environmental degradation experience there, and due to health advisories which have cautioned residents against eating fish and shellfish taken from the harbor areas, thus observed catch per capita of 0.9 pounds/person is likely to be lower than that of other coastal villages.

--- SENTENCE 293 ---
(4) Villages in the Tafuna Plain and Aasufou regions where a large proportion of the island population lives, essentially have no access to reefs adjacent to their villages.

--- SENTENCE 294 ---
Their fishing efforts are undoubtedly different than that of the study area.

--- SENTENCE 295 ---
(5) The species composition of the catch is likely to be different between the study area and the outlying areas.

--- SENTENCE 296 ---
Fishing effort within the study area has dropped more than 45% since 1979 (Table 2), in spite of a nearly 50% increase in the human population during the same time period.

--- SENTENCE 297 ---
The cause of this drop cannot be attributed to a decline in the resource, as the catch per unit effort has been seen to increase during the same

time period.

--- SENTENCE 298 ---
Catches of individual species are seen to fluctuated widely from FY91 to FY92.

--- SENTENCE 299 ---
Comparing the pounds landed of each species group presented in Table 4 between FY91 and FY92, it can be seen that in almost all cases, the catch of a particular species will fluctuate by a factor of 2 to 10 in most cases (Table 4).

--- SENTENCE 300 ---
A number of factors affect the species composition observed in the catch, including natural fluctuations in species composition and abundance, and the variations in fishing methods.

--- SENTENCE 301 ---
Unfortunately, this study can not adequately monitor the variability in fishing methods, and so in unable to make definitive statements regarding fluctuations in the species composition or abundance, other than what was observed in the landings.

# Species analysis

This section presents a comparison of catches between FY91 and FY92 for eight of the dominant species groups that contributed to the inshore catch.

--- SENTENCE 534 ---
The atule (Selar crumenophthalmus) is thought to be migratory species which moves through the area once or twice a year.

--- SENTENCE 535 ---
The first run generally occurs in March to May, the second in October to November (Ponwith, 1992).

--- SENTENCE 536 ---
The strength and timing of the runs are highly variable.

--- SENTENCE 537 ---
They frequent the harbor areas, forming large schools which are caught by hook and line, gill nets, and throw nets (Table 5, Figure 2)

Participation and effort in the Tutila shoreline fishery appears to be driven largely by two seasonal species, atule and palolo.

--- SENTENCE 538 ---
Atule fishing dominated the fishing effort and catch in FY91 (Tables 4 and 5).

--- SENTENCE 539 ---
High catches of atule were obtained in the Fagatogo and Utulei areas in FY91.

--- SENTENCE 540 ---
However when atule catch rates fell after August of 1991, a general reduction in fishing effort followed, especially in the harbor areas where the atule primarily schooled.

--- SENTENCE 541 ---
There was an increase in gleaning and spear diving efforts in FY92 from FY91 (Table 2), probably as a result of people switching to these methods as there were no atule to be found after the second month of FY92 (Figure 2).

--- SENTENCE 542 ---
Very little biological information is known about this species in the waters of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 543 ---
Preliminary observations on the atule in the harbor area have shown these fish to be actively feeding (personal observation), however fish with mature gonads have not been observed, (Craig, personal communication).

--- SENTENCE 544 ---
Nothing is known concerning where these fish go when they are not in the harbor area.

--- SENTENCE 545 ---
As shown in Figure 2, these fish have been entirely absent from catches in the study area since August of 1991.

--- SENTENCE 546 ---
The cause of this absence is unknown.

--- SENTENCE 594 ---
Although the migratory atule are the dominant in terms of landings and effort in this fishery, of primary interest to fisheries managers is the abundance of the reefresident species, as they are presumed to be better indicators of the health of the reef ecosystem.

--- SENTENCE 595 ---
Unfortunately, it is difficult to factor the atule effort out of the database, as participation counts do not provide direct data about the species a fisherman is targeting (if indeed the fisherman is targeting a specific fish at all), nor does the interviewer ask the fisherman what they are fishing for.

--- SENTENCE 596 ---
However, as the atule are caught primarily in the Fagatogo and Utulei areas, and caught primarily by hook and line methods, it is possible to look at the CPUE in strata where atule did not dominate the effort and catch statistics.

--- SENTENCE 597 ---
For this analysis, all strata shown in Table 2 where atule contributed 50% or more to the catch were deleted.

--- SENTENCE 598 ---
All strata for the Nu'uuli area and the hand-line method were also deleted, as these strata were not recorded in the 1979 study, and so are not useful for this comparison.

--- SENTENCE 599 ---
What is left for comparison are the gleaning and spear diving strata, and three strata for bamboo pole and throw nets (Table 6, Figure 3).

--- SENTENCE 600 ---
A large drop in the "non-atule" effort is apparent, dropping about 51-78% between 1979 and the present (Table 6).

--- SENTENCE 601 ---
According to standard fisheries models, for a fishery that experiences a significant fishing mortality, a decline in effort should be accompanied by an increase in CPUE, as fish stocks recover from the fishing pressure.

--- SENTENCE 602 ---
However, the 1991 non-atule CPUE is virtually identical to the 1979 CPUE, suggesting that the CPUE is independent of fishing pressures at the levels of effort observed for this fishery.

--- SENTENCE 603 ---
From FY91 to FY92 the non-atule effort increased approximately 30% and at the same time the CPUE increased about 67%, again suggesting a lack of correlation between effort and CPUE at these levels of effort.

--- SENTENCE 604 ---
Whether the observed fluctuations in CPUE are a function of fishing pressure, or are a function of other factors is, unfortunately, almost impossible to say from this study.

--- SENTENCE 605 ---
It is reasonably assumed that the abundance and composition of the reef resident species are a function of the health of the coral reefs that provide the basis for this ecosystem.

--- SENTENCE 606 ---
As previously mentioned, the reefs on Tutuila have been assaulted by a crown-of-thorn invasions just prior to the 1979 study, and by hurricanes during the time of the present study.

--- SENTENCE 607 ---
At this time, the only fisheries data we have is from the reefs during and after times they were subjected to significant natural perturbations.

--- SENTENCE 608 ---
Interpretation of the present data is difficult without a longer time series to look at, which for experimental purposes should include times of a more stable reef environment, and large fluctuations in fishing effort.

--- SENTENCE 609 ---
<u>Faisua, Giant Clams, (Tridacniadae)</u>, were completely absent from surveyed catches in FY91 and FY92.

--- SENTENCE 610 ---
Fishing regulations prohibit the take of clams less than 6" across the longest part of the

shell, however allow an unlimited take of larger clams.

--- SENTENCE 611 ---
In 1979, 7935 pounds (in the shells) of bivalves of all species were harvested, mostly by gleaners, (Wass 1980).

--- SENTENCE 612 ---
Wass does not provide a breakdown by species for clams, however it is probable that the majority of the 1979 clam catch was tridacna.

--- SENTENCE 613 ---
In FY91, only and estimated 243 pounds of clams were harvested, non of which were tridacna (Table 4).

--- SENTENCE 614 ---
There were no recorded catches of clams of any species in FY92.

--- SENTENCE 615 ---
Wild tridacna clams are scarce within the study area and throughout the island, though anecdotal reports indicate that they were once much more abundant than they are today.

--- SENTENCE 616 ---
Overfishing and hurricane damage has probably reduced their numbers.

--- SENTENCE 617 ---
Catches of spiny lobster (Panulirus pencillatus) and slipper lobster (Parribacus caledonicus) are down considerably from their 1979 levels.

--- SENTENCE 618 ---
Wass (1980) estimated that 1580 pounds of all lobster species combined were harvested in 1979.

--- SENTENCE 619 ---
Only 378 and 301 pounds of lobster were taken in FY91 and FY92, respectively (Table 4).

--- SENTENCE 620 ---
Lobster are taken almost entirely by night time spear divers and gleaners.

Fe'e, Octopus.

--- SENTENCE 674 ---
Octopus are a favored species, taken almost exclusively by gleaners and spear divers, and were the second most abundant species taken by fishermen for FY91 and FY92 combined.

--- SENTENCE 675 ---
Species identification is not known.

--- SENTENCE 676 ---
Catches of octopus tripled from FY91 to FY92, particularly for fishermen gleaning on the reef tops (Table 7, Figure 4).

--- SENTENCE 677 ---
Gleaners caught nearly 14 times more octopus in FY92 than the did in FY91, even though there was only a modest 24% increase in gleaning effort.

--- SENTENCE 678 ---
The octopus CPUE for gleaners increased by a factor of 12 between FY91 and FY92, from 0.20 pounds/hour to 2.47 pounds/ gear hour.

--- SENTENCE 679 ---
The CPUE for spear divers however decreased 27% over the same time period, dropping from 0.95 pounds octopus/hour to 0.60 pounds/gear hour (Table 7, Figure 4).

--- SENTENCE 680 ---
Reasons for this discrepancy between gleaning and spear diving CPUE are unclear.

--- SENTENCE 681 ---
During much of FY92 a small group of Tongan fishermen were observed gleaning for octopus on a sometimes daily basis.

--- SENTENCE 682 ---
Depending on tide, current, and surf conditions, they would typically begin a fishing trip in Usa'aiga (Matu'u area) and end in Faga'alu where they would sell their catch on the roadside.

--- SENTENCE 683 ---
Octopus fishermen usually walk on the reef top during very low tides, or swim at other times, poking metal rods into crevices to flush out their prey.

Jacks, lupo, lupota, malauli, ulua, sapo'anae (Carangidae).

--- SENTENCE 724 ---
There are 13 genera and 25 species of carangids present in the waters of American Samoa, most of which are not known by specific Samoan names, but rather distinguished by size classes (Wass 1984).

--- SENTENCE 725 ---
Jacks are taken by all fishing methods in all of the study areas, with the three hook and line methods accounting for the majority of the catch (Table 8).

--- SENTENCE 726 ---
Certain larger jack species are known to follow the atule, and fishermen will sometimes target these fish by baiting their hooks with live atule.

--- SENTENCE 727 ---
The total pounds landed dropped 72% from FY91 to FY92, however the number of fish increased 23% (Table 4).

--- SENTENCE 728 ---
The jack CPUE for rod and reel fishermen dropped considerably from 0.7 pounds/hour to 0.3 pounds/hour, while the spear diving CPUE for jacks increased from 0.04 pounds/hour to 0.3 pounds/hour (Table 8, Figure 5).

--- SENTENCE 729 ---
Due to the large number of different species within this category, little can be said about what these changes mean.

--- SENTENCE 730 ---
The average weight of jacks was 0.7 pounds/fish in FY91, dropping to 0.2 pounds/fish pounds in FY92.

--- SENTENCE 731 ---
The likely explanation for this observation is that the species composition in the catch has changed, with fewer larger fish caught in FY92 than in FY91.

Jack (Carangidae) catch by area (top), and Jack CPUÉ for Rod and Reel and Spear fishing methods (bottom).

--- SENTENCE 764 ---
Pone and Palagi (Small and large Brown Surgeonfish).

--- SENTENCE 765 ---
Samoans commonly refer to any small brown surgeonfish as "pone", and the larger surgeonfish as palagi (Wass 1984).

--- SENTENCE 766 ---
However these terms are also commonly applied to many dull-colored or less abundant surgeonfish or unicornfish for which a specific name is not known.

--- SENTENCE 767 ---
This choice of terminology makes it difficult to provide accurate statistics on many of the acanthurds by species.

--- SENTENCE 768 ---
The Pone and Palagi categories do not include some of the distinctively colored, more common or more popular such as the alogo (bluebanded surgeonfish), manini (convict tang), kolama (achilles tang), mamo (sergeant major), and pe'ape'a (moorish idol).

--- SENTENCE 769 ---
In the harbor area, most of the "palagi" caught were of one species, the yellowfin surgeonfish, Acanthurus xanthopterus.

--- SENTENCE 770 ---
Catches of palagi, dropped dramatically between FY91 and FY92 (Table 9, Figure 6).

--- SENTENCE 771 ---
This is an important reef species, targeted both by hook and line fishermen and spear divers, and often sold in the market place and fish stores around the island.

--- SENTENCE 772 ---
Reasons for the drop in catches are unknown.

--- SENTENCE 773 ---
The drop may reflect a decline in abundance of this species, although it could simply reflect a lack of targeting on this species, since palagi were usually caught in the harbor area where the sale of fish has been banned due to pollution, (see also "Recent impacts to coral reefs" on page 3).

--- SENTENCE 774 ---
Hook and line fishermen often target palagi by using a clump of rice on a hook, then snagging the fish as they come to eat the bait.

--- SENTENCE 775 ---
Although this fishing method differs from other hook and line methods, this choice of baits used by fishermen is not recorded in this survey, making extraction of this fishing method from the database difficult.

--- SENTENCE 776 ---
Anecdotal evidence suggest that spear diving can deplete the Palagi from an area.

--- SENTENCE 777 ---
While collecting fish samples for heavy metal analysis, two department divers speared 14 A. xanthopterus off the reef in Utulei.

--- SENTENCE 778 ---
When this dive was repeated a week later, only one palagi was seen.

--- SENTENCE 779 ---
Catches of pone have increased in all areas, especially Nu'uuli, for gleaners and spear fishermen (Figure 6).

--- SENTENCE 780 ---
Catches of pone (top) and palagi (bottom) for FY91 and FY92.

--- SENTENCE 782 ---
"Pone" refers to smaller fish and "palagi" refers to larger fish.

Mullet, anae, fuafua, poi, moi (Muqilidae).

--- SENTENCE 852 ---
Seven species of mullet are reported in Samoan waters, of these Liza melinoptera (giantscale mullet), Valamugil seheli (bluespot mullet), and V. engili (Engel's mullet) predominate in inshore catches.

--- SENTENCE 853 ---
The general name for mullet is "anae" which is usually applied to fishes measuring 20-40cm; other names are "fuafua", usually 12-15cm, and "poi" (5-8cm), and "moi" (< 5cm).

--- SENTENCE 854 ---
Mullet are a popular food fish in Samoa and the third most abundant fish caught during the FY91-FY92 study period, and represented 5% of the non-atule catch (Table 4).

--- SENTENCE 855 ---
Catches and catch rates for mullet have been seen to drop considerably from FY91 to FY92 (Table 11, and Figure 8).

--- SENTENCE 856 ---
Mullets are most often caught by throw net and gill net fishermen.

--- SENTENCE 857 ---
Fishing pressure by gill net and throw net methods has also declined over the study period, with the exception of gill net fishing in the Aua area where a four fold increase was observed (Table 3).

--- SENTENCE 858 ---
Mullet were of prime interest to DMWR staff, as samples of these fish from the inner harbor area were found to have the highest concentrations of lead in their tissues of all species examined (AECOS 1991).

--- SENTENCE 859 ---
DMWR staff repeatedly sampled the inner harbor area from July through October of 1992, in an attempt to catch fish (particularly mullet) for further heavy metal analysis.

--- SENTENCE 860 ---
Juvenile mullet ( < 15cm were found in abundance, however only one large mullet (37cm) was captured in spite of repeated efforts.

--- SENTENCE 861 ---
Fishermen in the harbor interviewed during this time period also reported that they had not seen or caught any large mullet.

## <u>Palolo</u>

--- SENTENCE 929 ---
Although very brief, the palolo fishery represents a significant portion of the total inshore fishing effort.

--- SENTENCE 930 ---
If we assume the average palolo fishing trip lasts three hours, then the 2818 person-trips observed in the inshore study area in FY93 (Table 13), represent approximately 8400 person hours.

--- SENTENCE 931 ---
This figure represents approximately 17% of the total fishing effort estimated to have occurred in the study area in FY92 (Table 2), however the palolo catch represents only 2.5% of the non-atule catch (Table 4).

--- SENTENCE 932 ---
Boat fishermen had much higher catch rates than did shoreline fishermen (Figure 10).

--- SENTENCE 933 ---
This is because the palolo epitokes will swim toward the surface, but otherwise are poor swimmers and by and large drift with the currents.

--- SENTENCE 934 ---
The general flow of water on the reef is for waves to push water up onto the reefs tops along the reef face.

--- SENTENCE 935 ---
This water then flows parallel to the beach until it reaches a channel or "ava" where it can flow out.

--- SENTENCE 936 ---
Floating debris, including the epitokes, are floated along and concentrated on the reef top until they reach the channels and are flushed out.

--- SENTENCE 937 ---
Boats will anchor in these channels, and so are able to filter a great deal more epitoke rich water with less effort than are the shore fishermen.

--- SENTENCE 938 ---
Palolo worms are found in the greatest abundance in the surf zone and in the shallower waters immediately off shore of the reef face.

# RECOMMENDATIONS

Monitoring Program.

--- SENTENCE 1167 ---
This survey does provide reasonably reliable data on shoreline fishing efforts on Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 1168 ---
However, catch/effort surveys such as this are able to produce only a crude indicator of stock abundance, particularly for a shoreline fishery as complex and species diverse as this one.

--- SENTENCE 1169 ---
Its value for estimating species abundance and total fishing mortality is extremely limited without independent data to estimate these parameters.

--- SENTENCE 1170 ---
More accurate and reliable data for both management and scientific purposes has to come from other sources.

--- SENTENCE 1171 ---
If the primary purpose of this study is to provide baseline biological data, then it is recommended that this study not be continued.

--- SENTENCE 1172 ---
While this project provides reasonably reliable data on fishing effort, the catch data collected are insufficient, biased, and unreliable for a variety of reasons.

--- SENTENCE 1173 ---
Biological inferences based on this data such as CPUE and catch composition are highly questionable.

--- SENTENCE 1174 ---
Options for this project include 1) discontinuing altogether, 2) continue, however with emphasis on the fisheries cultural and economic importance, rather then as of a biological study.

--- SENTENCE 1175 ---
If this project is continued, it is recommended that:

1.

--- SENTENCE 1176 ---
Inadequate interview data severely hampers the accuracy of the data analysis.

--- SENTENCE 1177 ---
Interview rates simply must be increased to get a valid sample size.

--- SENTENCE 1178 ---
Two years of continuous effort by both principal investigators to increase catch data collection has not yielded the desired results.

--- SENTENCE 1179 ---
Deployment of additional crews whose primary purpose would be to collect interviews would help correct the studies defeciencies.

--- SENTENCE 1180 ---
This should not bias the expansions of the effort data as expansions are done on each strata separately.

--- SENTENCE 1181 ---
It is unlikely that the catch estimates will be significantly biased by this either because it is unlikely that weekend fishermen experience significantly different CPUE's than week day fishermen.

--- SENTENCE 1182 ---
The nature of the fishery and the design of this survey produces a bias and under sampling in the sampling of catch data, particularly for spear diver fishermen.

--- SENTENCE 1183 ---
The fishing effort is probably also underestimated due to the fact that it is often difficult to see and therefore count persons who are often skin diving at night and at some distance from the shore line.

--- SENTENCE 1184 ---
Biases result as the sampling of these spear diver fishermen includes only those who are easily sampled due to their closeness to the shore, or who happen to be seen exiting the water when the creel technician is in the area.

--- SENTENCE 1185 ---
Island-wide expansion factors need to be validated.

--- SENTENCE 1186 ---
Currently the island-wide catch estimates are estimated by multiplying the study area per capita catch by the total island population.

--- SENTENCE 1187 ---
This method assumes that the study area's catch rates and fishing patterns resemble those of the rest of the island, an assumption which may be inaccurate for the reasons listed in the fishery profile section, (page 16).

--- SENTENCE 1188 ---
Information on recreational and other uses of the inshore reef areas should be collected.

--- SENTENCE 1189 ---
A large number of people are often seen swimming, bathing, surfing, and mining sand, who are not counted in the normal participation counts as they are not fishing.

--- SENTENCE 1190 ---
Obtaining estimates of non-fishing related uses of the beaches and reefs will provide a more complete picture of the uses of these resources.

--- SENTENCE 1191 ---
In February 1993, data forms were modified, and technicians were instructed to start collecting this information.

--- SENTENCE 1192 ---
This entailed only a nominal increase in survey efforts.

--- SENTENCE 1193 ---
Data collectors record the numbers of persons seen swimming, surfing and sand mining on their participation data sheets during the normal participation counts.

--- SENTENCE 1194 ---
They also make visual estimates of the quantity of sand removed from the beaches without actually interviewing the participants.

--- SENTENCE 1195 ---
The data analysis programs used for generating this report were highly obtuse, cryptic, and lacking in reasonable documentation.

--- SENTENCE 1196 ---
Fortunately, they are in the process of being rewritten.

--- SENTENCE 1197 ---
A possible major logic was identified in the current program, when corrected will probably change much of the "expanded" catch and CPUE data shown in this report.

--- SENTENCE 1198 ---
(Effort data shown here is probably correct, however).

--- SENTENCE 1199 ---
The operation of the analysis programs needs to be streamlined, and adequate documentation needs to be prepared.

--- SENTENCE 1200 ---
The catch estimation program used in this analysis needs to be modified to insure that data swamping does not occur in the annual estimates in cases where brief pulse fisheries produce large amounts of both catch and effort compared to the rest of the year.

--- SENTENCE 1201 ---
This is very likely a problem with the Atule in the FY92 data set (see Figure 2).

--- SENTENCE 1202 ---
In FY92, there was high catch and effort for Atule for the first two months of the year, followed by no Atule catches for the rest of the year.

--- SENTENCE 1203 ---
The current program expands data on an annual basis, and this brief pulse is averaged into the data for the entire year.

--- SENTENCE 1204 ---
Basically, the expansion program implicitly assumes that catch rates are constant throughout the time period selected for analysis.

--- SENTENCE 1205 ---
The correction for this would be to add a "season" stratum in the catch expansions hierarchy for selecting a CPUE to use for expanding a particular effort.

--- SENTENCE 1206 ---
Management Plan: The following issues were identified by Ponwith (1991) as items which would need to be addressed in a management plan.

--- SENTENCE 1207 ---
To date, these issues have not been addressed:

- 1) Overall management objectives for the fishery.
- Objectives and options for fishery monitoring.

--- SENTENCE 1208 ---
- 3) Policies on issues such as commercial versus recreational/subsistence use, stock rebuilding, and resource use in protected areas.

--- SENTENCE 1209 ---
A plan for implementing additional regulations should they become necessary.
- 5) Background information on key species taken in the inshore fishery.

--- SENTENCE 1210 ---
The following issue was also identified by Ponwith, and has been partially answered by this study, and continued monitoring of this fishery would provide additional detail:

--- SENTENCE 1211 ---
1) Identification of key species taken in the inshore fishery.

--- SENTENCE 1212 ---
<u>Future research</u>: Little quantitative data exists describing the factors affecting the abundance of various key species.

--- SENTENCE 1213 ---
Managers would benefit from more basic biological information including the following:

- 1) Atule.

--- SENTENCE 1214 ---
Basic biological information on the migratory Atule.

--- SENTENCE 1215 ---
Where do they go, what do they do when they're not in Pago Pago Harbor?

--- SENTENCE 1216 ---
What stocks of fish are being fished on in Samoa?

--- SENTENCE 1217 ---
Are the seasonal runs we see part of a much larger stock?

--- SENTENCE 1218 ---
Or is this a small local stock of fish which may be endangered by the intense pressure put on it by the Atule fishermen in Pago Pago Harbor?
- Size and age of maturity studies.

--- SENTENCE 1219 ---
Size at maturity and the size composition of the harvest for groupers, snappers and parrot fish would be valuable, considering the small size at which they are currently harvested.
- 3) <u>Studies of virgin populations</u>.

--- SENTENCE 1220 ---
Comparative studies of species composition and size structure in areas of different exploitation rates would provide data on the effects of fishing pressure on the community structure.

--- SENTENCE 1221 ---
Few areas on Tutuila offer good "virgin stock" controls to compare to the heavily used areas.

--- SENTENCE 1222 ---
The Fogagogo area, and the Tula are two possibilities, although topographical and environmental variables would confound the comparison between "virgin" and heavily fished areas.

--- SENTENCE 1223 ---
Migrations and offshore movements.

--- SENTENCE 1224 ---
Tagging fish species which are caught by both the shoreline and offshore fisheries, such as some of the groupers and snappers, to study movements between the areas exploited by the two fisheries would be of interest.

--- SENTENCE 1225 ---
For example, are the reef habitats replenished by fish from offshore when the stocks are fished down?

--- SENTENCE 1226 ---
Exchange of reef fish over large distances.

--- SENTENCE 1227 ---
Knowing the amount of interchange between stocks of fish between the various islands would provide managers with a valuable tool for assessing the effects of over fishing or an environmental catastrophe which may deplete a local stock.

--- SENTENCE 1228 ---
Most of the reef-resident species observed in American Samoa have a planktonic larval stage, suggesting that brood stocks for these species could reside at considerable distances from the areas that the adults are observed.

--- SENTENCE 1229 ---
A study of the assessing the degree of genetic relatedness between reef fish species among the various island groups could shed some insights on this question.

--- SENTENCE 1230 ---
A genetic study on Yellowfin Tuna in various locations in the Western Pacific region have shown a high degree of relatedness among the presumed stocks of fish.

--- SENTENCE 1231 ---
Indications are that these fish are highly migratory, and localized depletions could potentially be replenished from other populations in a relatively short period of time, (assuming that the Pacific wide yellowfin stocks remained healthy).

--- SENTENCE 1232 ---
Larval distribution studies, and genetic studies assessing the degree of relatedness between various Pacific island stocks could help answer this questions.

- 7) Impacts of spear divers using SCUBA.

--- SENTENCE 1233 ---
Spear diver fishermen observed in the study area use snorkel gear almost exclusively, often using only swimmers goggles and no fins.

--- SENTENCE 1234 ---
Their efficiency and ability to target deeper fish would increase dramatically should they switch to scuba gear.

--- SENTENCE 1235 ---
Managers need to monitor this potential gear change, as breading stocks of parrot fish, lobster, bluebanded surgeonfish, and many others are highly vulnerable to night spear divers using scuba.

--- SENTENCE 1236 ---
The department has denied aquarium permits to several recent applicants, on the grounds that the coral reefs in the area have been damaged and are in need of a recovery period.

--- SENTENCE 1237 ---
However, the department is currently lacking scientific data to support the contention that an aquarium fishery would be detrimental to the reefs, or to the fish populations.

--- SENTENCE 1238 ---
As species targeted by the aquarium trade would be different in the most part than those taken by subsistence and commercial fishermen, an aquarium fishery would probably not compete or interfere with the more traditional reef fisheries.

--- SENTENCE 1239 ---
An aquarium fishery also has the potential to provide a much higher value fishery than traditional fisheries.

--- SENTENCE 1240 ---
As an example, a 2 ounce butterfly fish can sell from \$2 to \$50 depending on the species, whereas the same fish is worth only about \$0.22 assuming an average price of \$1.73 for reef fish in 1992 applied to the small aquarium fish.

--- SENTENCE 1241 ---
An aquarium fishery would probably have low environmental impacts as well, assuming fishermen were restricted to only those methods that are currently legally allowed.

--- SENTENCE 1242 ---
The department should initiate a study to substantiate it's contention that an aquarium fishery would adversely impact the reefs resources.

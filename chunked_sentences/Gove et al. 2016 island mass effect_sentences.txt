# Near-island biological hotspots in barren ocean basins

Jamison M. Gove<sup>1</sup> , Margaret A. McManus2, Anna B. Neuheimer2, Jeffrey J. Polovina1 , Jeffrey C. Drazen2, Craig R. Smith2, Mark A. Merrifield2,3, Alan M. Friedlander4,5, Julia S. Ehses3,6, Charles W. Young3,6, Amanda K. Dillon3,6 & Gareth J. Williams7,8

Phytoplankton production drives marine ecosystem trophic-structure and global fisheries yields.

--- SENTENCE 2 ---
Phytoplankton biomass is particularly influential near coral reef islands and atolls that span the oligotrophic tropical oceans.

--- SENTENCE 3 ---
The paradoxical enhancement in phytoplankton near an island-reef ecosystem—Island Mass Effect (IME)—was first documented 60 years ago, yet much remains unknown about the prevalence and drivers of this ecologically important phenomenon.

--- SENTENCE 4 ---
Here we provide the first basin-scale investigation of IME.

--- SENTENCE 5 ---
We show that IME is a near-ubiquitous feature among a majority (91%) of coral reef ecosystems surveyed, creating near-island 'hotspots' of phytoplankton biomass throughout the upper water column.

--- SENTENCE 6 ---
Variations in IME strength are governed by geomorphic type (atoll vs island), bathymetric slope, reef area and local human impacts (for example, human-derived nutrient input).

--- SENTENCE 7 ---
These ocean oases increase nearshore phytoplankton biomass by up to 86% over oceanic conditions, providing basal energetic resources to higher trophic levels that support subsistencebased human populations.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
<span id="page-1-0"></span>hytoplankton production is an essential source of energy in the marine environment<sup>1</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
The extent and availability of phytoplankton biomass drives the trophic-structure of entire marine ecosystems<sup>2</sup>, dictating the distribution and production of the world's fisheries<sup>3</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
The ecological impacts of enhanced phytoplankton biomass are especially acute near tropical coral reef islands and atolls as these ecosystems predominantly reside in nutrient impoverished waters that lack new production<sup>4</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
For example, across the central and western Pacific, islands and atolls exposed to elevated levels of nearshore phytoplankton support higher fish biomass and a greater abundance of reef-building organisms than those found in more oligotrophic waters<sup>5,6</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
Hence, mechanisms that act to promote nearshore phytoplankton biomass are critical for coral reef ecosystem development and persistence<sup>5</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
The increase in phytoplankton biomass proximate to island-reef ecosystems—'Island Mass Effect' (IME)—was first documented over a half century ago<sup>7</sup> (Fig. 1).

--- SENTENCE 22 ---
Much of our current knowledge of the IME, however, stems from studies in a small number of locations in geographically confined areas<sup>8–10</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 23 ---
Thus, whether or not the IME is a pervasive phenomenon across broad gradients in oceanic conditions has historically remained unknown.

--- SENTENCE 24 ---
Furthermore, the relative influence of natural vs anthropogenic drivers of variations in the magnitude of the IME has remained a mystery until now.

--- SENTENCE 25 ---
Here we present a basin-scale investigation of the 60-year-old IME hypothesis.

--- SENTENCE 26 ---
Using 35 coral reef islands and atolls spanning 43° of latitude and 60° of longitude (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 1) that cross multiple gradients in oceanic forcing <sup>11</sup>,

geophysical attributes<sup>11</sup>, reef-community composition<sup>5,12</sup> and local human impacts<sup>12</sup>, we quantify the prevalence of the IME across the tropical Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
We use long-term satellite-derived observations of chlorophyll-*a* (a proxy for phytoplankton biomass) to show the IME is a near-ubiquitous feature and identify key biogeophysical drivers of variations in the magnitude of the IME among Pacific island- and atoll-reef ecosystems.

--- SENTENCE 28 ---
We also incorporate ship-based surveys at 29 of these locations to confirm that the nearshore enhancement in chlorophyll-*a* occurs over the full euphotic depth range.

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
Finally, we show that the IME increases the nearshore standing stock of phytoplankton biomass by up to 85.6% over background oceanic conditions, providing basal energy sources for higher trophic levels across an otherwise barren ocean landscape.

--- SENTENCE 30 ---

Nearshore phytoplankton enhancement.

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
We found that 91% (n=32) of island- and atoll-reef ecosystems displayed localized nearshore enhancement in long-term chlorophyll-a associated with the IME.

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
Localized increases in phytoplankton biomass near island- and atoll-reef ecosystems—Island Mass Effect—may be the result of several causative mechanisms that enhance nearshore nutrient concentrations, including coral reef ecosystem processes, such as nitrogen fixation or decomposition, and animal waste products, such as reef-associated fishes; current-bathymetric interactions that can drive vertical transport of water masses via upwelling, downstream mixing and eddies, and internal waves; island-associated inputs, such as submarine groundwater discharge and outflow from rivers, which can mobilize and transport sediment and other terrigenous material laden with nutrients; the flushing and associated outflow of lagoonal waters from atoll environments; human-derived runoff of agricultural production, urban development and wastewater input.

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
Enhanced nearshore phytoplankton can influence food-web dynamics and elicit a biological response in higher trophic groups, for example: horizontal and vertical migration patterns in squids, fishes and other micronekton (collectively referred to as the 'mesopelagic boundary layer community') that move nearshore at night to feed on increased food resources; inshore migration of pelagic predators, such as tuna, to feed on the island-associated micronekton community; greater reef fish biomass and increased cover of calcifying benthic organisms in coral reef ecosystems.

--- SENTENCE 48 ---

--- SENTENCE 49 ---

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
Bathymetric influences on ocean currents can force vertical

transport of subsurface nutrient-rich waters that fuel nearshore productivity (Fig. 1).

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
For example, vertical transport can be driven by current impingement that uplifts isotherms on the upstream side of an island4,16 or through turbulent mixing, lee eddy and wake effects on the downstream side17-19.

--- SENTENCE 54 ---
Internal waves, generated from tidal currents interacting with underlying bathymetry, can also drive vertical perturbations in the background stratification that deliver cooler, nutrient-rich waters to the near-surface<sup>20</sup> resulting in increased nearshore phytoplankton biomass<sup>21</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
The shoreward propagation of internal waves is directly related to bathymetric slope<sup>22</sup>; internal waves more readily reach shallower waters and fuel phytoplankton production where the underlying slope is more gradual.

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
In contrast, internal waves are reflected offshore at steeper sloped locations.

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
Across our study region, we found the IME to be particularly pronounced at locations within the Hawaiian Archipelago, a region characterized by islands and atolls with gradual sloping bathymetry (Supplementary Table 1) and highly active internal wave generation<sup>23</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 96 ---

--- SENTENCE 98 ---
While in situ surveys provided direct observations of nearshore phytoplankton enhancements in the upper water column, they also demonstrated clear spatiotemporal variability exists in the IME.

--- SENTENCE 99 ---

--- SENTENCE 100 ---

--- SENTENCE 101 ---

--- SENTENCE 102 ---

--- SENTENCE 103 ---

--- SENTENCE 104 ---
Nevertheless, our in situ observations provide important verification that the observed surface phytoplankton biomass enhancements are indeed reflective of subsurface phytoplankton gradients near Pacific island- and atoll-reef ecosystems.

**Total increase in marine food resources.

--- SENTENCE 106 ---
We examined the ecological implications of the IME by quantifying the total increase in standing stock of phytoplankton, and thus the increase in basal resources available to higher trophic levels, driven by the presence of each island- and atoll-reef ecosystem.

--- SENTENCE 107 ---
The IME resulted in a long-term averaged (10 years) combined increase in nearshore phytoplankton biomass of 703.6% across our study locations (n = 28; see Methods section).

--- SENTENCE 108 ---
This represented a total increase of 17.21 metric tonnes over the background oceanic standing stock of phytoplankton.

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
On a per island basis, we saw a range of 0.2–85.6% increase in total phytoplankton biomass (Fig. 5).

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
The Hawaiian Archipelago harboured the top nine reef

ecosystems with the greatest long-term increases in total phytoplankton biomass relative to background oceanic conditions (range; 29.9-85.6%).

--- SENTENCE 111 ---
Other locations exhibited more modest enhancements in phytoplankton biomass.

--- SENTENCE 112 ---
The equatorial islands of Howland, Baker and Jarvis, for example, showed a combined long-term enhancement in phytoplankton biomass of only 2.6% over background oceanic conditions (Fig. 5).

--- SENTENCE 113 ---
This result was unexpected considering in situ observations of strong localized upwelling<sup>16</sup>, high cover of reef-building organisms<sup>5</sup> and high planktivorous and predatory fish biomass<sup>12</sup> at these locations.

--- SENTENCE 114 ---
However, these islands are uniquely situated at the western edge of the equatorial cold tongue; a geographic area in the Pacific that experiences consistent trade wind-driven equatorial upwelling and high chlorophyll-a concentrations (Fig. 2).

--- SENTENCE 115 ---
Therefore, a more muted biological response associated with the IME was observed at these island-reef ecosystems owing to the already elevated background phytoplankton levels.

# **Discussion**

The consequences of increased phytoplankton biomass span multiple trophic groups within coral reef island and atoll marine food-webs.

--- SENTENCE 117 ---
Calcium carbonate-forming benthic organisms, namely hard (scleractinian) corals and crustose coralline algae, show increased abundance across the central and western Pacific Ocean when exposed to increased phytoplankton biomass<sup>5</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 118 ---
Similarly, the biomass of planktivorous and piscivorous fishes and baseline estimates of Pacific reef sharks are far greater at locations with greater mean phytoplankton biomass<sup>6,36</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 119 ---
In addition, a distinct community of squids, fishes and other deepwater associated micronekton are found in high densities near island-reef ecosystems relative to offshore waters<sup>37</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 120 ---
This community—the mesopelagic boundary layer community exhibits strong diel migration in association with subsurface island topography, transiting long distances (>5 km) towards shore at night and peaking in abundance and density in waters with increased phytoplankton biomass<sup>38</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 121 ---
Pelagic predators, such as dolphins<sup>39</sup> and tuna<sup>40</sup> cue in on the shoreward migration of organisms, exploiting the island-associated micronekton community as a food resource (Fig. 1).

--- SENTENCE 122 ---
Moreover, inter-island migratory patterns of marine apex predators, such as tiger sharks (Galeocerdo cuvier), appear to be driven by variations in phytoplankton biomass, presumably owing to net energetic gain associated with bottom-up driven increases in prev abundance<sup>41</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 123 ---
Phytoplankton biomass and the underlying drivers that enhance it may not always act to bolster ecological communities.

--- SENTENCE 124 ---
For example, sewage pollution can increase nutrient levels that drive entire regime shifts in marine ecosystems<

--- SENTENCE 125 ---
sup>42</sup> while the associated phytoplankton enhancement can drive coastal eutrophication and toxic algal blooms, resulting in mass mortalities of fishes, marine mammals and seabirds<sup>28</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 126 ---
In addition, cold ocean temperatures in chronic upwelling environments may limit coral growth and suppress reefbuilding processes that are important for coral reef persistence 43.

--- SENTENCE 127 ---
Similarly, upwelled nutrients can significantly exceed background concentrations and enhance fleshy (non reef-building) algal growth<sup>20</sup>, potentially disrupting benthic competitive interactions and reshaping coral reef communities.

--- SENTENCE 128 ---
Because ecological communities can exhibit threshold-type responses, or tipping points, to variations in human<sup>44</sup> and natural physical drivers<sup>45</sup>, similar relationships potentially exist between island- and atoll-reef ecosystems and the processes that enhance nearshore phytoplankton biomass.

--- SENTENCE 129 ---
Future research is needed, however, to properly identify and better understand the existence of non-linearities in marine ecosystems associated with the IME.

--- SENTENCE 130 ---
Global shifts in biogeochemical cycling and ocean mixing associated with climate change are projected to decrease nutrient availability and primary production in the tropics and subtropics<sup>46</sup>, impacting fisheries and in turn compromising human food supplies<sup>47</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 131 ---
However, state-of-the art climate models used in these projections do not resolve complex biophysical interactions, such as the IME, that occur at the island-reef ecosystem scale.

--- SENTENCE 132 ---
The projected strengthening of the Pacific equatorial undercurrent 48, for example, may increase vertical transport of nutrient-rich waters that drive phytoplankton biomass enhancements near equatorial island- and atoll-reef ecosystems despite projected regional declines in marine primary production.

--- SENTENCE 133 ---
The number of island- and atoll-reef ecosystems that stand to benefit from future equatorial undercurrent strengthening is but a fraction of reef ecosystems in the Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 134 ---
Nevertheless, these systems represent potentially important refugia for coral reef ecosystems and the food-webs they support in a rapidly changing climate.

--- SENTENCE 135 ---
Biogeophysical drivers of the IME may also serve to bolster coral reef ecosystem resiliency to future thermal stress events and associated coral bleaching.

--- SENTENCE 136 ---
The delivery of particle-laden deep ocean waters via upwelling and internal waves, for example, could provide important energetic subsidies<sup>49</sup> and a thermal reprieve<sup>50</sup> for corals during prolonged periods of anomalously warm temperatures.

--- SENTENCE 137 ---
Our basin-scale investigation of the IME demonstrates that nearshore phytoplankton enhancement is a long-term, near-ubiquitous feature among Pacific coral reef islands and atolls.

--- SENTENCE 138 ---
Moreover, we found the magnitude of nearshore phytoplankton enhancement differed among island- and atoll-reef ecosystems owing to variations in key biogeophysical drivers, namely geomorphic type (atoll vs island), bathymetric slope, reef area and factors associated with the presence of local human populations.

--- SENTENCE 139 ---
Individual coral reef island and atolls were capable of increasing the nearshore standing stock of phytoplankton biomass by up to 86% over background oceanic conditions, forming biological hotspots across an otherwise barren ocean landscape.

--- SENTENCE 140 ---
Ecosystem services vital to human populations, such as fisheries production and coastal protection, are intrinsically linked to the nearshore phytoplankton enhancement associated with the IME.

--- SENTENCE 141 ---
Such ecosystem-scale biophysical phenomenon must therefore be incorporated into future modelling efforts if we are to accurately predict the trajectory of marine ecosystems and the millions of people they support in this era of rapid change.

#### Methods

Quantifying chlorophyll-a enhancements.

--- SENTENCE 143 ---
Spatial gradients in chlorophyll-a were quantified following Gove et al. 11.

--- SENTENCE 144 ---
In brief, long-term mean (July 2002 to June 2012) chlorophyll-a was calculated from 8-day, 0.0417° Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) data.

--- SENTENCE 145 ---
Eight sectors of ~3.27 km width (0.0295°; ½ the diagonal of a satellite pixel) that were perpendicular to the 30 m isobath were quantified at each location (for example, Supplementary Fig.1).

--- SENTENCE 146 ---
Sectors extended  $\sim 3.27-6.54 \,\mathrm{km}$  to  $\sim 26.16-29.43 \,\mathrm{km}$  (0.0295-0.0590° to  $0.2360-0.2655^{\circ}$ ) offshore from the 30 m isobath.

--- SENTENCE 147 ---
Data within  $\sim 3.27$  km of the 30 m isobath were removed to avoid optically shallow waters and errors induced by terrigenous input, re-suspended material or bottom substrate properties<sup>51</sup> (Supplementary Fig. 2).

--- SENTENCE 148 ---
Where island or atoll proximity resulted in sectors from different locations containing common data pixels, pixels were identified and removed before pixel averaging to avoid potential biases associated with the IME signal from one location influencing another's.

--- SENTENCE 149 ---
However, when proximity resulted in a large proportion of pixels shared between locations (>50%), bathymetry was combined and sectors recalculated for the larger formed island-complex.

--- SENTENCE 150 ---
Specifically, Molokai, Maui, Lanai and Kahoolawe were combined to form MAUI NUI; Saipan, Tinian and Agujian were combined to form SAI; Ofu, Olosega and Tau were combined to form MANUA (Fig.

--- SENTENCE 151 ---
2 and Supplementary Table 1).

--- SENTENCE 152 ---
We found the relationship between long-term chlorophyll-a and distance to shore was best described by a power function at all locations (for example, Supplementary Fig.1b).

--- SENTENCE 153 ---
Given that a power function is equivalent to a linear fit on log-log transformed data, seamless transition can be made between nonlinear and linear fits and associated analyses.

--- SENTENCE 154 ---
To test for differences in the relationship between long-term chlorophyll-a and distance to shore between locations, an analysis of covariance was performed on log-log transformed data.

--- SENTENCE 155 ---
Two tests were performed; one test that included all islands and atolls (n = 35) and another that only included locations that showed a significant increase in chlorophyll-a with decrease distance to shore (n = 28; P < 0.05).

--- SENTENCE 156 ---
We incorporated a series of biogeophysical parameters that are potential drivers of increased phytoplankton biomass near oceanic island- and atoll-reef ecosystems.

--- SENTENCE 157 ---
The following were quantified for each location: latitude, geomorphic type (atoll vs island), reef area, land area, bathymetric slope, elevation, human population status (unpopulated vs populated) and the long-term mean and standard deviation for: sea-surface temperature, precipitation and ocean currents (Supplementary Table 2).

--- SENTENCE 158 ---
Latitude (°) represented the centre point of each location.

--- SENTENCE 159 ---
Geomorphic type was either 'atoll' or 'island'.

--- SENTENCE 160 ---
Reef area (km²) was calculated from the shore-line to the 30-m isobath and land area (km²) was calculated for all emergent land.

--- SENTENCE 161 ---
Bathymetric slope (°) was derived from bathymetric grids in ArcGIS v10.1 using the Spatial Analyst 'slope' function, calculated between 30–300 m depth and then averaged across the entire location.

--- SENTENCE 162 ---
A detailed description of these factors (latitude, geomorphic type, reef area and land area) can be obtained in Gove *et al.*<sup>11</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 177 ---
<span id="page-6-0"></span>Statistical analysis and model selection.

--- SENTENCE 178 ---
Our research hypothesis was that the IME varied with island type (atoll vs island), reef area, land area, bathymetric slope, elevation, population status (populated vs unpopulated), SST (mean and standard deviation), precipitation (mean and standard deviation) and current speed (mean and standard deviation).

--- SENTENCE 179 ---
We used slope ('b') from the regression output of log-log transformed data (chlorophyll-a vs distance to shore) to represent the IME (that is, the response variable), enabling a standardized comparison in chlorophyll-a gradients among study locations.

--- SENTENCE 180 ---
This hypothesis was tested by initially fitting a GLM.

--- SENTENCE 181 ---
While also assuming linear relationships among response and predictor variables (that is, like traditional linear regression), GLMs relax the requirement of a normal error distribution, 'generalizing' the model to other distributions (within the exponential family) by relating the response and predictor variables through a 'link' function.

--- SENTENCE 182 ---
Only locations with significant negative relationships (that is, increased chlorophyll-a with decreased distance to shore) were used in the model (n = 28; P < 0.05).

--- SENTENCE 183 ---
Initial examination of response vs predictor(s) appeared to show a combination of linear and non-linear relationships were possible.

--- SENTENCE 184 ---
A GLM was used as the starting model and the assumption of linearity (that is, use of GLM) was tested (as were all other assumptions).

--- SENTENCE 185 ---
An error distribution of gamma was assumed (requiring a positive transformation of slope values before model input) and a log link was used.

--- SENTENCE 186 ---
Collinearity among predictors was tested both by calculating Pearson's correlation values and variance inflation factors (VIF, for multicollinearity; for example<sup>53</sup> via car package<sup>54</sup>).

--- SENTENCE 187 ---
Predictors with the highest VIF value were inspected with respect to their linear correlation with other predictors (via Pearson's correlation), as well as the mechanistic underpinnings of the research hypothesis.

--- SENTENCE 188 ---
Following removal of the predictor representing the most concern (for example, highest collinearity), the model was refit and remaining predictors were assessed until all VIFs were <3 ref.

--- SENTENCE 189 ---
Following this iterative process, elevation, latitude, SST (mean and standard deviation) and the standard deviations of current speed and precipitation were removed.

--- SENTENCE 190 ---
Remaining predictors were geomorphic type (atoll vs island), reef area, land area, bathymetric slope, population status (populated vs unpopulated), mean precipitation and mean current speed.

--- SENTENCE 191 ---
Model variants representing all possible combinations of these predictors (main effects, see interactions below) were tested and the models were ranked according to Akaike Information Criteria corrected (AICc) for small sample size.

--- SENTENCE 192 ---
Two candidate models were chosen based on ∆AICc≤2 (Supplementary Table 3).

--- SENTENCE 193 ---
On the basis of these results (Supplementary Table 3), models representing all possible combinations of main effects and two-way interactions were tested with geomorphic type, reef area, bathymetric slope, population status and mean current speed as predictors (MuMIn package<sup>55</sup>).

--- SENTENCE 194 ---
The interaction between geomorphic type and population status was removed as no atolls were populated.

--- SENTENCE 195 ---
Two models were chosen based on ΔAICc≤2 (Supplementary Table 3).

--- SENTENCE 196 ---
The highest AICc weight occurred for the simpler model and further analysis indicated mean current speed did not significantly improve model explanatory power (based on analysis of deviance via  $\chi^2$ -test, P = 0.219).

--- SENTENCE 197 ---
In contrast, the interaction term between geomorphic type (island) and reef area was significant (based on analysis of deviance via  $\chi^2$ - test, P = 0.001), increasing the overall explained deviance of the model from 78 to 85%.

--- SENTENCE 198 ---
The resulting best-specified model was:

(2) abs(b) ∼ Geomorphic Type + Reef Area + Bathymetric Slope + Population Status + Reef Area:Geomorphic Type + (1|Region)

There was no significant difference found when comparing the model with and without this random effect (based on analysis of deviance via  $\chi^2$ - test, P = 0.5) and the fixed effects model was chosen (model 1).

--- SENTENCE 218 ---
The total long-term mean (July 2002 to June 2012) in nearshore standing stock phytoplankton biomass enhanced by each island and atoll over background oceanic phytoplankton was calculated using remotely sensed observations.

--- SENTENCE 219 ---
We first calculated the long-term mean in depth-integrated chlorophylla ( $\Sigma$ Chl; mg m<sup>-2</sup>) by multiplying the long-term mean in chlorophyll-a(see above for details) by the long-term mean in the depth of light penetration for each satellite pixel.

--- SENTENCE 220 ---
Depth of light penetration, or the depth at which the satellite can 'see' into the water column, was calculated by taking the reciprocal of the long-term mean in 8 day, 0.0417° MODIS k490 ref.

--- SENTENCE 221 ---
All long-term mean ΣChl values within each sector (see Supplementary Fig.1) were then averaged for each island and atoll.

--- SENTENCE 222 ---
The relationship between  $\Sigma$ Chl and distance to shore was then quantified by applying a linear fit on log-log transformed data.

--- SENTENCE 223 ---
Only locations with significant (P < 0.05) negative relationships (that is, increased  $\Sigma$ Chl with decreased distance to shore) were used (n = 28; same locations used in modelling efforts).

--- SENTENCE 224 ---
Using the furthest sector from each location (sector 8, for example see Supplementary Fig.1) to represent offshore, oceanic conditions in phytoplankton, we calculated the change ( $\Delta$ ) in  $\Sigma$ Chl with each subsequent, more proximate sector.

--- SENTENCE 225 ---
We then multiplied  $\Delta\Sigma$ Chl for each sector by the respective sector area (m<sup>2</sup>; the change in latitude was accounted for in longitude to distance conversions for all locations) to calculate the phytoplankton enhanced (kg and percentage) for each sector.

--- SENTENCE 226 ---
Summing over all sectors (1-7) enabled a quantitative estimate of the long-term phytoplankton biomass enhanced by the presence of each island and atoll in our study.

--- SENTENCE 227 ---
Vertical chlorophyll-a profiles were obtained using a profiling fluorometer during 37 ship-based surveys of 29 individual islands and atolls in our study region.

--- SENTENCE 228 ---
Surveys consisted of horizontal transects, starting  $\sim 2-4\,\mathrm{km}$  from shore and extending 20–30 km offshore in one or more cardinal directions over 1–3 days.

--- SENTENCE 229 ---

--- SENTENCE 230 ---

#### **Background**

Degradation of nearshore habitats is a serious problem in some areas of American Samoa, such as in Pago Pago Harbor on Tutuila Island, and is a smaller but chronic problem in other areas.

--- SENTENCE 4 ---
Sedimentation, pollution, nutrient enrichment from surface runoff or groundwater, and trampling are the major factors causing the changes (Peshut and Brooks, 2005).

--- SENTENCE 5 ---
The outer islands of Ofu and Olosega (Manu'a Islands; Fig. 1) have no perennial streams and a small and declining human population (approximately 500 residents), yet there is an interesting contrast between relatively pristine lagoon habitats not far from comparatively degraded lagoon habitats.

Analysis of nutrients in lagoon waters is not the most effective method for detecting nutrient influx because of the rapid removal and recycling of nutrients from seawater by plankton and macroalgae (also known as seaweeds; e.g., Atkinson, 1988).

--- SENTENCE 24 ---
Unlike some marine organisms and plants, macroalgae are known to incorporate nutrients directly from the water column and store excess nutrients in their tissues, providing an integrated record of nutrient influx (Fong and others, 1994).

--- SENTENCE 25 ---
Thus, macroalgae may better provide a history of nutrient influx to a site compared to other organisms.

--- SENTENCE 26 ---
For example, cyanobacteria can directly fix nitrogen (Howarth and others, 1988); rooted plants such as sea grasses primarily take up nutrients from sediments and interstitial water and not directly from the water column (Paling and McComb, 1994:

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
Erftenmeijer and

Middleburg, 1995); and coral reef organisms incorporate nutrients from symbionts or ingested plants and/or other organisms (Mills and others, 2004).

--- SENTENCE 28 ---
To investigate the relative differences in nutrient influx to each of the lagoons, we analyzed tissue from the two most common species of macroalgae for nitrogen content.

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
To determine the source of nutrients to each of the lagoons, we examined the nitrogen isotope content of the macroalgae found on the reef flats in front of both villages (Olosega and Ofu, Fig. 1) and compared those sites with the relatively pristine lagoon/reef flat in front of To'aga (Fig. 1).

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
Biological processes usually result in fractionation of the isotopes of elements (such as nitrogen and carbon) incorporated by organisms and plants (Minagawa and Wada, 1984).

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
The ratio of <sup>15</sup>N to <sup>14</sup>N {more specifically the  $\delta^{15}N$  (in %) =  $[(^{15}N)^{14}N \text{ sample}]/(^{15}N)^{14}N$  atmospheric nitrogen) – 1] x 1000} in tissue has been reported to be effective in distinguishing between sources of nitrogen (e.g., atmospheric deposition, fertilizer, nitrogen fixation, and animals or plants; Peterson and Fry, 1987) and trophic level (the higher the trophic level, the greater the  $\delta^{15}$ N; e.g., Minagawa and Wada, 1984).

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
Atmospheric deposition and fertilizers have relatively low  $\delta^{15}$ N values (-12 to +5% and -3 to +3%, respectively; Kreitler and Browning, 1983; Russell and others, 1998).

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
$\delta^{15}$ N values of +5 to +22 ‰ are reported to indicate that nitrogen from animals may be present, with highest values (+10 to +22%); Kreitler and Browning, 1983; Aravena and others, 1993) associated with septic and wastewater systems.

--- SENTENCE 34 ---
Distinguishing between multiple animal and human sources is not as well defined.

#### Methods

Study sites

This study was conducted at Ofu and Olosega islands (in the Manu'a island group), which are located 96 km ENE of Tutuila, American Samoa (Fig. 1).

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
Three study sites were selected: two reef flats off Ofu Island (one seaward of Ofu Village and the other at a "pristine" site with no human settlement, To'aga); and a reef flat off Olosega Island seaward of Olosega Village (Fig. 1).

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
Sampling transects ran from shore to the reef-crest at each of the three locations and were sited to avoid proximity to openings in the reef and associated strong currents (Fig. 1).

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
The width of the reef flat at low tide determined the transect length at a site (Table 1).

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
Ofu Village and reef flat (hereafter "Ofu") are located on the western shore of Ofu Island (Fig. 1).

--- SENTENCE 39 ---
Ofu Village lagoon is influenced by open-ocean swells from the north, west, and south and has two openings in the reef ("avas").

--- SENTENCE 40 ---
Two ephemeral streams empty into Ofu Village lagoon.

--- SENTENCE 41 ---
Approximately 250 people live in Ofu Village and use cesspools for sewage disposal.

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
Soils are sandy with relatively low organic-carbon content.

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
To'aga is on the southeastern shore of Ofu Island (Fig. 1).

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
To'aga is most influenced by swells from the east, south and southwest and has one small pass in the reef and a channel on the eastern end of the island.

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
To'aga has an undeveloped watershed with no streams; a garbage dump is sited on land approximately 1.3 km northeast (and downcurrent) of the To'aga site.

--- SENTENCE 56 ---

--- SENTENCE 57 ---

--- SENTENCE 59 ---

--- SENTENCE 60 ---

--- SENTENCE 61 ---
Ocean waters continually flush the reef flats even during low tide, when lower volumes of water break over the reef-crest, resulting in less forceful currents.

#### *Field sampling*

Five replicate samples of the two most common species of macroalgae (*Halimeda*  sp. and *Dictyosphaeria versluysii*) were collected at each of five stations located on each transect: 3 m from shore; at distances ¼, ½, and ¾ of the transect length, and at the shoreward edge of the reef-crest (Table 1).

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
No samples were collected from forereef sites due to the lack of macroalgae at those stations.

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
Whole algal organisms (without rhizoids or holdfasts) were collected by hand using disposable gloves, cleaned of detritus and invertebrates, and immediately placed in individual Ziploc bags with seawater.

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
All macroalgae and water samples were collected during periods of low tide between 24 May and 4 June 2006.

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
A water sample for salinity testing was collected concurrently with each seaweed sample.

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
Additional water samples were collected at each of the sites and from freshwater wells at Olosega and Ofu villages for nutrient analysis [nitrate, ammonia, dissolved inorganic nitrogen (DIN) and phosphate].

#### Laboratory

More than 200 macroalgae samples were further cleaned of sand and fouling organisms in the lab, rinsed with fresh water, placed in aluminum weighing boats, and dried at 60°C until constant weight.

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
Individual samples were ground into a fine powder.

--- SENTENCE 78 ---
transferred to scintillation vials and placed in a desiccator.

--- SENTENCE 79 ---
A 5 to 9-mg subsample of each powdered *Halimeda* sp. sample was transferred into a tin capsule, weighed and sealed for  $\delta^{15}$ N analysis.

--- SENTENCE 80 ---
Because single D. versluysii samples did not provide the minimum weight needed for stable-isotope analysis, five replicates per station were pooled, mixed thoroughly, and three 5 to 9-mg subsamples were transferred into tin capsules, weighed, and sealed for  $\delta^{15}$ N analysis.

--- SENTENCE 81 ---
The limited number of green filamentous and blue-green algae samples was pooled by species and site, mixed thoroughly, transferred to tin capsules, weighed, and sealed for  $\delta^{15}$ N and for  $\delta^{13}$ C analyses.

--- SENTENCE 82 ---
Replicate *Dictyosphaeria* samples from each station were subsampled, subsamples pooled, weighed in tin capsules, and sealed for  $\delta^{13}$ C analysis.

--- SENTENCE 83 ---
Replicate Halimeda sp. samples from each station were subsampled, subsamples pooled, placed in scintillation vials, a few drops of concentrated HCl added, and placed in a desiccator overnight.

--- SENTENCE 84 ---
Inorganic C in the samples was released as CO<sub>2</sub>, leaving the organic C. Treated samples were dried at 60°C to remove HCl, 1to 4 mg of sample weighed in tin capsules, and capsules sealed for  $\delta^{13}$ C analysis.

--- SENTENCE 85 ---
Macroalgae samples were analyzed for  $\delta^{15}$ N and  $\delta^{13}$ C at the University of California at Davis Stable Isotope laboratory specializing in isotope-mass ratio spectrometry.

--- SENTENCE 86 ---
Nutrient concentrations in water samples (NO<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup>+NO<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup>, PO<sub>4</sub><sup>3-</sup>, NH<sub>4</sub><sup>+</sup>) were analyzed by colorimetric techniques (Lachat QuickChem 8000 autoanalyzer, Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution, Nutrient Analytical Facility).

--- SENTENCE 87 ---
Nitrate and nitrite were not separately quantified, and in this report their sum is referred to as "NO<sub>3</sub>"."

--- SENTENCE 88 ---
Total dissolved nitrogen (TDN) was measured using persulfate digestion (D'Elia and others, 1977).

--- SENTENCE 89 ---
Dissolved organic nitrogen (DON) concentration was calculated as DON = TDN -  $NO_3^-$  -  $NH_4^+$ .

--- SENTENCE 90 ---
Salinity of water samples was determined using a refractometer.

--- SENTENCE 91 ---
ANOVA statistical analysis was used to test for differences in  $\delta^{15}$ N values of *Halimeda* sp. and *Dictyosphaeria versluysii* among stations and sites.

#### Results

Overall, macroalgal cover was noticeably low at all lagoon sites (Fig. 5), with *Halimeda* sp. and *Dictyosphaeria versluysii* the predominant species and *Chlorodesmis fastigata* a distant third.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
Living coral cover was lowest on the Olosega (6.47%) and Ofu (10.05%) transects and highest at To'aga (22.81%; Fig. 5).

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
A greater number of coral species occurred directly on the To'aga transect (10) than the Ofu and Olosega transects (6 species each; Table 2).

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
The  $\delta^{15}N$  values of the individual macroalgae samples ranged from -0.4 to +5.5% (Table 3), indicating sites that seem to be most influenced by oceanic and/or atmospheric and not anthropogenic sources.

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
Salinity measurements did not show a significant freshwater influence at any sites or stations sampled (Fig. 6).

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
However, salinity was slightly lower, but not significantly so, in samples from the station nearest to shore in Ofu lagoon, most likely due to a freshwater stream that emptied into Ofu lagoon (Fig. 1).

#### *Halimeda* sp.

--- SENTENCE 128 ---
There was a significant difference in *Halimeda* sp.  $\delta^{15}$ N values among sites (p=0.0004), with To'aga (4.45 ± 0.48) greater than Ofu (3.47± 0.41) and Olosega (3.61± 0.38; Table 4).

--- SENTENCE 129 ---
There was no significant difference in  $\delta^{15}$ N among stations at Ofu (p = 0.0598); and a significant difference among stations at Olosega (p=0.022) and To'aga (p = 0.014; Table 4), with values generally increasing from shore to the reef-crest (Fig. 7).

--- SENTENCE 130 ---
#### Chlorodesmis fastigata

The green filamentous alga, *Chlorodesmis fastigata*, contained a relatively higher percent of nitrogen (mean =  $3.97\pm0.53$ ) than *Dictyosphaeria versluysii* ( $0.88\pm0.17$ ), which exceeded that of *Halimeda* sp. ( $0.62\pm0.15$ ; Table 3).

--- SENTENCE 131 ---
Nutrient data for the three macroalgae species could not be compared statistically because of fewer and unequal numbers of samples of *C. fastigata* from the three sites.

--- SENTENCE 132 ---
Replicate samples of *C. fastigata* could be collected only at To'aga because the species was rarely observed at Ofu and infrequently seen at Olosega.

--- SENTENCE 133 ---
Thus, the number of samples per site varied greatly (1-25).

--- SENTENCE 134 ---
Carbon-to-nitrogen ratios in all species of macroalgae (no replicate C samples) were much lower than typical Redfield ratios reported from the open ocean (C:N = 6.625; Table 3).

#### Water samples

Nitrate, ammonia, and DIN levels were low in lagoon-water samples from all sites and not considered high for well/groundwater samples (Table 5).

--- SENTENCE 135 ---
Phosphate levels in well water were higher in Olosega than Ofu samples.

--- SENTENCE 136 ---
Similarly, phosphate concentrations in lagoon-water samples were highest from Olosega (Table 5).

--- SENTENCE 137 ---
High silica concentrations in groundwater were most likely from the volcanic rock that forms the islands.

#### Discussion

The nitrogen content (percent total nitrogen, concentration of the heavier nitrogen isotope  $^{15}$ N, and  $\delta^{15}$ N values) of macroalgae varies by species (due to physiology and biochemical processes), light regime, temperature, and availability of nitrogen and <sup>15</sup>N content of the water (e.g., Heikoop and others, 1998; Cohen and Fong, 2006).

--- SENTENCE 155 ---
Values of  $\delta^{15}$ N in this study (Table 3) were close to or lower than those reported for oligotrophic suspended particulate matter (4-5%; Waser and others, 2000), pelagic and coastal marine seaweeds (5-8%; Minagawa and Wada, 1984), and euphotic layer DIN (7-10%; Minagawa and Wada, 1986).

--- SENTENCE 156 ---
The low  $\delta^{15}$ N values of the macroalgae, the low nutrient concentrations of lagoon waters, the lack of correlation between  $\delta^{15}N$  values and distance from shore, and the low concentrations of nutrients in well-water samples indicate that the major sources of nutrients to the three Ofu-Olosega lagoons at the time of this study were most likely oceanic/atmospheric, and not animal/anthropogenic in origin.

--- SENTENCE 157 ---
In contrast to this study, Umezawa and others (2002) found an inverse linear or curvilinear decrease in  $\delta^{15}$ N values of macroalgae with distance from shore in the Ryuku Islands, Japan.

--- SENTENCE 158 ---
This pattern was attributed to multiple sources of nitrogen, including terrestrial and human.

--- SENTENCE 159 ---
Cohen and Fong (2006) reported  $\delta^{15}N$  in macroalgae decreased with distance from the head of estuaries in California during the dry season, due to a greater  $\delta^{15}N$  content of DIN from the watershed than oceanic sources.

--- SENTENCE 160 ---
Similarly, the  $\delta^{15}N$  in rooted macroalgae (not free-floating) was found to increase significantly with increased influx of sewagenitrogen to estuaries in the northeastern U.S. (Cole and others., 2005).

--- SENTENCE 161 ---
In the lagoons of Ofu and Olosega islands, high volumes of oceanic waters and strong currents flush the lagoons daily and would be expected to dilute nutrient input from land rapidly.

--- SENTENCE 162 ---
Under high-flow regimes, oligotrophic waters are able to provide sufficient nutrients for algal growth, but storage of nutrients in algal tissues is limited due to both low residence time and low nutrient concentrations of the water (Larned and Atkinson, 1997).

--- SENTENCE 163 ---
When high concentrations of nutrients are available, from episodic pulses to sustained high concentrations, some macroalgae store nutrients in their tissues (Wheeler and North, 1980; Lapointe and Duke, 1984; Fong and others, 1994).

--- SENTENCE 164 ---
However, Fong and others (2003) found there is not a direct correlation between nutrient concentrations in water and macroalgae tissue.

--- SENTENCE 165 ---
The nutrient content of tropical marine algae affects the organism's response to N and P influx, be it growth or storage.

--- SENTENCE 166 ---
Thus, the history of available nutrients combined with the biochemistry of the macroalgae species drive the responses observed in the field (Fong and others, 2003).

--- SENTENCE 167 ---
Whereas some macroalgae incorporate nutrients only from the water column, others can access nutrients in sediments, from adjacent heterotrophs (such as the scleractinian corals to which Dictyosphaeria versluysii attaches; Larned and Stimson, 1996), or, in the case of some cyanobacteria, from fixation of nitrogen (Howarth and others, 1988).

--- SENTENCE 168 ---
Halimeda and Dictyosphaeria (Division Chlorophyta) are rhizophytic algae and can access nutrients from the water as well as from sediment via holdfasts (Littler and Littler, 1990).

--- SENTENCE 169 ---
Larned and Stimson (1996) found that D. cavernosa, closely related to D. versluysii, has low N storage capacity and needs a continual supply of DIN.

--- SENTENCE 170 ---
The close attachment of D. cavernosa (and presumably D. versluysii) to the substrate creates space between the alga and live coral wherein ammonium concentrations are two-fold, and nitrate+nitrite

concentrations are 10-fold greater than that of the water column (Larned and Stimson, 1996).

--- SENTENCE 171 ---
The lack of stored nitrogen (%) in *Dictyosphaeria* was evident in the data from this study (Table 3).

--- SENTENCE 172 ---
The [molar](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mole_%28unit%29) C:N ratio in most phytoplankton is 106:16 (or 6.6; Redfield and others, 1963) when nutrients are not limiting.

--- SENTENCE 173 ---
In tropical epilithic macroalgae in Atlantic oligotrophic waters that are N limited, the C:N ratio is reported to range from 14-28 and to vary by species (Lapointe and others, 1992).

--- SENTENCE 174 ---
The C:N and %N data in this study may indicate that *Dictyosphaeria* and *Halimeda* are more N limited than *Chlorodesmis* at all sites (Table 3).

--- SENTENCE 175 ---
Alternatively, the observed difference between species could be a due to: a higher structural C content in *Dictyosphaeria* and *Halimeda;* and/or, the enhanced ability of *Chlorodesmis* to assimilate nitrogen from the environment or to store nitrogen than *Dictyosphaeria* or *Halimeda.*

--- SENTENCE 176 ---
The C:N ratio was consistently, but not significantly, higher at the To'aga site for the three macroalgae species, which could be due to a lower supply of DIN at the site.

--- SENTENCE 177 ---
Ofu and Olosega well-water nutrient levels from this study (Table 5) were within the range of American Samoa EPA values for Oct 2001, Jan and Dec 2003 samples (< 0.2 - 8.7 µM Ofu; <0.2 - 43 µM Olosega).

--- SENTENCE 178 ---
Other than relatively high phosphate levels in the well-water samples, well-water nutrient levels were within normal groundwater concentrations, at the time of this study.

--- SENTENCE 179 ---
Other sources of nutrients to the lagoons include episodic intense rainfall events that could flush nutrients from the upland watersheds and from reservoirs (such as cesspools) in the sandy soil into the lagoons.

--- SENTENCE 180 ---
Hurricanes produce high seas and battering waves that can inundate the coast for tens of meters inshore and wash nutrients from the sandy soil into the lagoons.

--- SENTENCE 181 ---
Nearshore marine communities adjacent to villages with cesspools, pig farms, and other domestic animals would be expected to receive a concentrated pulse of nutrients that could be quickly utilized by filamentous algae that can rapidly take up nutrients from the water and grow quickly.

--- SENTENCE 182 ---
If lagoon substrate was exposed by removal of sand or turf algae, as often occurs during storms, an opportunistic filamentous alga could colonize, grow rapidly, and produce a "bloom".

--- SENTENCE 183 ---
Hurricanes and other strong storms produce oceanic upwelling, where deep, cooler, nutrient-rich waters are brought to the surface (Babin and others, 2004).

--- SENTENCE 184 ---
Storm-induced upwelling in conjunction with tidal pumping and surface runoff from land could provide a considerable source of nutrients to the nearshore waters.

--- SENTENCE 185 ---
All of these mechanisms could fuel algal blooms nearshore.

--- SENTENCE 186 ---
During a hurricane damage survey conducted by the National Park of American Samoa nine days after Hurricane Olaf, a filamentous alga bloom was observed in Olosega lagoon (P. Craig.; Fig.

--- SENTENCE 187 ---
Yet no macroalgae blooms were observed off To'aga or on the north side of Ofu at that time.

--- SENTENCE 188 ---
It is probable that an influx of nutrients flushed from the adjacent village via intense rainfall, tidal pumping, and inundating storm waves stimulated the bloom.

--- SENTENCE 189 ---
However, data from this study, conducted 18 months later, do not support or refute this speculation.

--- SENTENCE 190 ---
To determine the specific drivers for this system, nutrient influx from land and from upwelling, as well as the role of space availability will need to be examined.

--- SENTENCE 191 ---
Identifying the drivers of macroalgae blooms is often not straightforward.

--- SENTENCE 192 ---
For example, a large-scale macroalgae bloom has been reported on coral reefs over a wide geographic area in the northern Great Barrier Reef and 15 to 20 km from known anthropogenic sources (Burgess, 2006).

--- SENTENCE 193 ---
Is this the result of nutrient influx, loss of herbivores/changes in trophic structure, a predator outbreak that opens substrate to

colonization, changes in physiology that alter algae-coral dynamics, or a combination of factors?

--- SENTENCE 194 ---
Are nutrient sources atmospheric, oceanic, from groundwater, rivers, or point sources?

--- SENTENCE 195 ---
Our understanding of the major factors driving macroalgal blooms on coral reefs is far from complete.

--- SENTENCE 196 ---
We suggest that the lagoons in front of Ofu and Olosega Villages may have less live coral and fewer species than the lagoon at To'aga because:

- reef-walking for gleaning and fishing occurs more frequently in front of the villages, causing chronic physical damage to the coral;
- episodic, heavy rains periodically flush nutrients from the land and cesspool contents into the lagoon, resulting in filamentous algae blooms that compete for space and/or periodically smother live coral;
- the relatively shallower lagoons fronting the villages may never have supported as abundant and diverse a coral growth as is found in To'aga.

--- SENTENCE 197 ---
We consider this unlikely because numerous shallow areas of the lagoon on the southeast of Ofu Island contain numerous species of coral and much of the substrate is covered by live coral.

--- SENTENCE 198 ---
Related questions that arise from the study:

- Does the higher N content of *Chlorodesmis* make it a preferred food for herbivores, and therefore less abundant on reefs than the carbonate-rich *Halimeda* and tough-membraned *Dictyosphaeria*?

--- SENTENCE 199 ---
The same question can be asked of the blue-green alga.

--- SENTENCE 200 ---
- Is there a difference in herbivore (specifically macroalgae-feeding fish species) abundance among the three lagoons?

--- SENTENCE 201 ---
If so, would this predispose some areas to algal blooms under conditions of high nutrient influx?

### **Conclusions**

 From this study, Ofu and Olosega lagoons seem to have good water quality, with nitrogen influx of predominantly oceanic/atmospheric origin.

--- SENTENCE 202 ---
At the time of this study, anthropogenic sources of nutrients were not detected in macroalgae in the lagoons.

--- SENTENCE 203 ---
The major factors driving the differences among the three lagoons (live coral cover, macroalgae cover, and number of coral species) remain unproven.

--- SENTENCE 204 ---
Data from this study provide the baseline needed to compare against data from future episodic events, to determine the nitrogen sources fueling algal blooms in the lagoons of Ofu and Olosega.

# Dept. of Marine and Wildlife Resources biological report series no. 6 CHARACTERIZATION OF INSHORE SAMOAN FISH COMMUNITIES WASS

# I. Introduction.

--- SENTENCE 2 ---
American Samoa's burgeoning population and economic growth have given rise to increased fishing effort (Wass, in press) and a multiplication of shoreline development projects.

--- SENTENCE 3 ---
Both have a generally adverse effect on the marine environment resulting in declining catches and reduced production of fishery resources.

--- SENTENCE 4 ---
Historically, the Samoans were very much aware of the need to conserve and protect their marine resources.

--- SENTENCE 5 ---
Conservation practices included reef tenure and a complex system of taboos reserving certain species and sizes of fish for the chiefs and restricting effort to certain seasons and locations.

--- SENTENCE 6 ---
The acquisition of Western culture and its attendent legal system, however, has caused the disappearence of many of the traditional management methods in the more populated and developed areas.

--- SENTENCE 7 ---
Modern fisheries management requires the resurrection and reinforcement of selected traditional practices and the blending of these with bethods and regulations based on comprehensive resource inventories and scientific study.

--- SENTENCE 8 ---
The present investigation was designed to aid these management efforts through definition of the basic inchore habitats around Tutuila Island, American Samoa and a determin ation of the composition and magnitude of the associated fish resources.

--- SENTENCE 9 ---
Tutuila is located in the tropical South Pacific at 14° south latitude and 171° west longitude at the midpoint of the Samoan archipelago.

--- SENTENCE 10 ---
It is a high island with a rugged basaltic terrain and a land area of about 140 km².

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
The temperature range is 20-33° C. and annual precipitation averages about 450 cm.

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
The southern coast of Tutuila is bordered by a more or less continuous fringing reef flat varying in width from about 50 to 900 m. Much of this flat is exposed at low kide though there are natural depressions as deep as a few meters and dredged areas as deep as six meters.

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
An irregularly sloping reef sterrace averaging about 40 m. in width extends seaward of the reef crest on the outer edge of the reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
The reef front at the seaward margin of the

terrace slopes steeply and often vertically from four to ten meters at the upper edge to a gently sloping sand and rebble bottom at 20 to 40 m.

The southern coast is indented by four prominent bays offering dissimilar habitats.

--- SENTENCE 15 ---
Pago Pago Bay is the largest and is bordered by the most populous and developed area of the island.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
Though most of the bay is over 60 m. deep, sewaye outfalls, effluent from tuna canneries, bilgewater and waste from the moored vessels and shoreline runoff contribute toward a decline in water quality near the back of the bay.

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
Pala Lagoon is a shallow, mangrove-fringed bay averaging about two meters in depth.

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
Tidal flow across a reef flat and through a narrow channel accounts for its limited circulation.

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
Larsen and Fagatele Bays for a third type of bay habitat.

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
They have wide mouths and are exposed to wind and current.

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
Both are volcanic craters with steeply sloping sides above and below the ocean surface and are over 200 m. deep.

--- SENTENCE 22 ---
There is little human habitation or development along their shores and almost no fresh water runoff so water quality is gM/g/ high.

--- SENTENCE 23 ---
A submerged barrier reef comprised of Taema and Nafanua Banks and intervening reefs parallels much of the southern coast.

--- SENTENCE 24 ---
It is separated from Tutuila by a distance of two to three kilometers and a depth of 80 to 100 m. The reefs rise to within 15 to 20 m. of the surface and slope off on either side to a sand and rugble bottom at 30 to 40 m.

Reef flats are a less conspicuous feature of the northern coastline of Tutuila, being limited primarily to the inner margins of numerous bays and coves.

--- SENTENCE 25 ---
Steeply sloping basaltic terrain characterizes the exposed portions of shoreline.

--- SENTENCE 26 ---
A submerged reef terrace directly abuts the rocky shore in most areas and extends seaward for 10 to 50 m. before plunging to a sand and rubble bottom at 20 to 40 m.

Existing studies of Samoan fishes consist largele of taxonomic investigations and portions of environmental assessments.

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
Jordan and Seale (1906) conducted the first comprehensive survey of Samoan

ichthyofauna.

--- SENTENCE 28 ---
Their <u>Fishes of Samoa</u> lists 475 species for the archipelago.

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
Schultz (1943) lists 441 Samoan fishes and includes keys to aid in the ridentification.

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
The taxonomy of these and other investigators is updated and 967 species are recorded in a recent publication by the author (Wass, MS).

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
Speci s names used in the present study are derived from that paper.

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
Most of the literature concerned with the ecology and community composition of Samoan fishes is contained within environmental assments conducted prior to the construction of docks, sewage outfalls, boat harbors, shoreline protection revetments, etc.

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
For the most part the data are purely descriptive lacking analysis and interpretation.

--- SENTENCE 34 ---
These publications also have the disadvantage of unavailability in most libraries.

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
Living Marine Resources, Inc. (1974a, 1974b) and Dames and Moore (1975) include descriptions of fish communities within Pago Pago Bay.

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
Randall and Devaney (1974), Helfrich and collaborators (1975), FHA and Dept. of Public Works (1976) and Devaney and Suzumoto (1977) present data summarizing observations of fishes in various areas around Tutuila and the neighboring islands of An Aunu'u, Ofu and Closega.

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
The American Samoa Coral Reef Inventory (1981) describes the habitats and associated biological communities for most of the inshore areas of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
It has much wider geographic coverage than the other studies but, again, analysis is lacking beyond a subjective ranking of species abundance/and little attempt is made to relate the influence of ecological factors to the observed distribution.

--- SENTENCE 39 ---
Investigators in other areas of the tropical Pacific have examined these relationships in considerable detail.

--- SENTENCE 40 ---
The Hawaiian fauna has probably received the most attention beginning with a pioneering study by Brock (1954) who was the first to combine the use of SCUBA and visual fish censuses.

--- SENTENCE 41 ---
Gosline (1965) discussed vertical zonation of Hawaiian fishes and related it primarily to wave action.

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
Hobson (1974) characterized the fish communities

associated with five habitats in Kona and related composition to environmental parameters as part of his detailed study of feeding relationships.

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
Fish community composition is characterized for major habitat types and related to environmental factors such as water movement, bottom configuration, substrate composition, depth, nutrient levels, predation, etc. for the Marshall Islands by Hiatt and Strasburg (1960), for the Great Barrier Reef by Talbot and Goldman (1972), for Fanning Island by Chave and Eckert (1974) and for a lagoon in Guam by Jones and Chase (1975).

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
Each of these studies implies that presence or absence of a species can be predicted if its ecological requirements are known.

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
Smith (1977, 1978) has reached the same conslusion from his investigations of Carribean fish communities.

--- SENTENCE 46 ---
A different viewpoint is expressed by Sale (1975, 1977, 1978a, 1978b) and Sale and Dybdahl (1975) who have found that the presence or absence of one or more of a group of damselfishes or certain other small reef fishes with similar ecological requirements is the result of random factors including chance colonization.

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
Brock, et. al.(1979) conclude that the "lottery hypothesis" may account for species distributions on a small areal scale and over a short timespan, but that a relatively deterministic pattern emerges if a larger unough area is considered and that the fishes are a persistent and predictable entity within a specific habitat.

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
Study locations were chosen to represent all of the common inshore habitats around Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
At each of 57 sites, the fishes were enumerated by a technique patterned after that of Jones and Chase (1975).

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
A weighted transect line 100 m. long and divided into five equal sections was laid on the bottom in a more or less straight line.

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
When transecting reef flat habitats which are subject to varying degrees of wave action depending upon depth and distance from the reef crest, the line was laid perpendicular to the shoreline with the seaward end as near the reef crest as

surf conditions allowed.

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
The two-year study period enabled a choice of days when wave action was least for conducting surveys.

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
For most reef flat stations it was, therefore, possible to position the seaward end of the transect at the reef crest where wave action is normally high.

--- SENTENCE 54 ---
Reef flat width was one of the primary factors governing the selection of transect sites.

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
Relatively narrow reefs were generally chosen to enable the transect to span most or all of the distance between the reef crest and the shoreline.

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
For sites where the reef was much wider than 100 m., the transect was situated across the slightly deeper mid-reef zone where fish biomass and diversity are greatest because of the shelter afforded by coral and reef rock.

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
At study sites beyond the breakers and in deeper water, the transect line was generally laid parallel to the depth contour at 4 to 15 m. where the gently slaping reef terrace merges with the steeply sloping reef front.

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
This interface harbors the richest coral growth, has the greatest structural complexity and borders deep water.

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
These factors all serve to increase fish diversity and biomass.

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
A few transects were also sited along shallow basalt shorelines and along the deep base of reef fronts as well as across and around coral pinnacles in Pago Pago Bay.

--- SENTENCE 61 ---
Transects were positioned near natural or man-made landmarks such as large rocks, channels or concrete structures.

--- SENTENCE 62 ---
Exact locations and depth were carefully noted to facilitate relocation because no permanent markers were used.

--- SENTENCE 63 ---
Approximately

Approximately 15 minutes after the line was laid, a single observer equipped with SCUBA swam the transect.

--- SENTENCE 64 ---
All fishes observed within one meter on either side of the line and two meters above it were identified to species, counted and their total lengths estimated to the nearest inch.

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
If the transect was on a reef flat, the data were kept separate for each 20-meter section to distinguish species preferences for different amounts of wave action, depths and types of substrate.

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
At the deeper stations where environmental conditions were relatively uniform, the data were not sectioned.

--- SENTENCE 67 ---
Holes and cracks in the reef within the transect corridor were inspected for nocturnal and secretive fishes and the substrate was examined for cryptic species.

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
There is no doubt, now-problem and problem and problem and problem and the substrate many of these fishes were undetected resulting in an underestimate of their abundance.

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
Being wary of divers, larger and more transient fishes tend to depart the transect corridor at the approach of the observer so they, too, are under-censused by this procedure.

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
Even clearly visible fishes that have no tendency to hide or flee the approach of a diver are subject to inaccurate counts because of their diversity and numbers and because of their constant motion in and out of the transect corridor.

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
In spite of these shortcomings, the visual census technique is considered a valuable tool for studying reef fish populations and is widely used.

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
It is of greatest value for making relative comparisons between fish communities at different times or locations rather than as a quantitative method for assessing the precise composition of a particular community.

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
All censuses were conducted by the author to reduce variability due to observer bias.

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
Twenty to thirty minutes were required to enumerate the fishes on the transect.

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
Data were recorded on a tape recorder in an underwater housing with the picrophone inserted within the mouthpiece of a regulator.

--- SENTENCE 76 ---

B. Data Analysis

# 1. Dendrographs.

--- SENTENCE 84 ---
No attempt was made to discriminate fish communities or to define habitat types before the surveys were initiated.

--- SENTENCE 85 ---
Upon their completion, a dendrograph analysis (McCammon, 1968; McCammon and Wenniger, 1970) was used to objectively group stations with similar fish communities.

--- SENTENCE 86 ---
Environmental characteristics common to the stations within a group could then be used to define the habitat.

--- SENTENCE 87 ---
The dendrograph is a tree-like diagram depicting mutual relationships between and within groups of stations.

--- SENTENCE 88 ---
The distance between any two adjacent stations on the vertical axis is proportional to their dissimilarity.

--- SENTENCE 89 ---
The spacing between the left margin of the dendrograph and the vertical lines connecting stations or groups of stations is inversely proportional to the degree of similarity within the grouping.

--- SENTENCE 90 ---
Close spacing and connecting lines near the left margin are, thus, indicative of a closely related group of stations.

### 2.

--- SENTENCE 91 ---
The dendrograph requires a similarity measure between all pairs of stations as the basic unit of data.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
Two coefficients of similarity were computed for each pair in the present study.

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
Jaccard's

coefficient of similarity (Jaccard 1901, 1908) is based on the presence or absence of species at one or both stations of the pair.

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
It can be defined as the percentage of species common to both stations and is formulated as:

$$S.j = \frac{c}{a+b+c} \times 100$$

where a is the number of species unique to station A, b is the number of species unique to station B and c is the number of species common to both stations.

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
The Bray-Curtis coefficient of similarity (Bray and Curtis, 1957) includes information on the number of individuals of each species.

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
It is derived from the quantitative modification of Sorensen's coefficient (Sorensen, 1948) as first applied by Motyka, et.

--- SENTENCE 97 ---
al. (1950):

$$S_{m} = \frac{2w}{A + B} \times 100$$

where A is the number of individuals of all species at station A,
B is the number of individuals of all species at station B and w
is the sum of the lesser of the counts for station A or B for those
species occurring at both stations.

--- SENTENCE 98 ---
Bray and Curtis adjusted the
values of A and B to 100 and computed percent Values for each
species count.

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
The above formula, thus, reduces to:

$$S_{bc} = \frac{2w}{100 + 100} \times 100 = w$$

where w is the sum of the lesser of the percent values for station A or B for those species occurring at both stations.

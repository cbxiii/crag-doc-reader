#### **ABSTRACT**

A study of the lobster population in American Samoa was undertaken between October 2002 and 2003.

--- SENTENCE 97 ---
Panulirus penicillatus is the main species speared by night near the outer slope by free divers while diving for finfish.

--- SENTENCE 98 ---
Total landings expanded from market survey were estimated to be 576 kg (1271 lb) of spiny lobsters sold per year (without taking subsistence and recreational catches into account).

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
Sex ratio was in favor of males.

--- SENTENCE 100 ---
This difference in sex ratio could be due to the fishing technique which not includes the highest part of the reef edge and the beginning of the reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 101 ---
This could also explain why commercial catches rarely include slipper lobsters (Parribacus caledonicus and some P. antarcticus) whereas they are relatively abundant in the field.

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
With the aid of data from market surveys undertaken in 1999 (i.e. before the ban of scuba fishing), we established that free diving was the most efficient technique.

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
Comparison between markets surveys done in 1999 and 2002-2003, showed an increasing of Catch Per Unit Effort in terms of weight but not in number of lobsters caught, and a decrease in the mean length.

--- SENTENCE 104 ---
Size at sexual maturity, i.e. size when 50 % of females are ovigerous, was estimated to be around 7 cm CL (2.76 in) for Panulirus penicillatus whereas the smallest berried females measured 5.25 cm CL (2.07 in).

--- SENTENCE 105 ---
Relationship between fecundity (number of eggs) and carapace length (CL) is provided.

--- SENTENCE 106 ---
Although it was unlawful since 1995 to catch berried females and spiny lobsters smaller than 7.94 cm (3 1/8 in) of CL, they are often found in the markets.

--- SENTENCE 107 ---
Values from market survey were used to estimate the total mortality.

--- SENTENCE 108 ---
The discontinuation of the field survey following an accident did not allow estimation of local growth parameters and natural mortality which were taken from literature.

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
Results of fishing mortality and Yield per Recruit (YPR) were in accordance with other lobster fisheries in the Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
YPR would slightly increase if length at recruitment was at the legal minimum size.

--- SENTENCE 111 ---
The main recommendation provided is the ban of spearfishing for the commercial fishery.

--- SENTENCE 112 ---
This regulation will allow fishermen to i) verify if lobsters are berried or undersized without injuring them, ii) to catch more slipper lobsters which are easily marketable, and iii) to sell high quality product in markets.

#### 1.

--- SENTENCE 113 ---
INTRODUCTION

The Samoans as well as all the communities of the South Pacific Islands have traditionally relied on the coral reef environment.

--- SENTENCE 114 ---
Fishing was an important activity if not essential, sea foods being a substantial part of the Samoan protein diet.

--- SENTENCE 115 ---
Culturally, fishing is also an important part of the Samoan way of life, but this importance has decreased with increasing Western influence (such as refrigeration and canning), implying a gradual shift from subsistence to a cash economy.

--- SENTENCE 116 ---
Lobsters are more expensive sea-foods than fishes, but are often present in important meals such as wedding, funerals, Christmas, or New Years day.

--- SENTENCE 117 ---
Formerly, lobsters were provided at the level of the village/family, whereas nowadays, they are mainly bought at the market, caught by professional/regular fishermen.

--- SENTENCE 118 ---
The main purpose of this study was to obtain biological parameters from the lobster population in American Samoa, and to assess the level of the lobster fishery in order to provide, if necessary, recommendations for the long-term management of this resource.

--- SENTENCE 119 ---
As the biology of spiny lobsters and slipper lobsters is relatively complicated, I chose to begin this report with an up-date of our knowledge on the two main species present in American Samoa shallow-waters.

--- SENTENCE 120 ---
Following this, I present the survey work.

--- SENTENCE 121 ---
STUDY SITE

The US territory of American Samoa covers a surface of 197 km² (76.1 square miles).

--- SENTENCE 122 ---
It includes five volcanic islands (Tutuila, Aunu'u, Ofu, Olosega, and Ta'u)

--- SENTENCE 123 ---
(Figure 1) and 2 remote atolls (Rose - *Lat*: 14.53° S *Long*: 175.87° W - and Swain - *Lat*: 11.06° S *Long*: 171.08° W).

--- SENTENCE 124 ---
The main city – Pago Pago – is situated in a deep well-protected harbor in Tutuila I sland.

--- SENTENCE 125 ---
Most people (96 %) for a total of more than 60,000 in 2002 (Census, DOC) live on Tutuila Island.

--- SENTENCE 126 ---
Because of its "young" age (1.5 millions years), Tutuila Island is only surrounded by a narrow fringing reef of coralline algae and live coral (Pitcher, 1993) of 55 km long (see characteristics in Wass, 1980a, Craig, 2000).

--- SENTENCE 127 ---
This allows fishermen to reach the reef edge more or less easily by walking at low tide through the reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 128 ---
During the dry season (May to November) moderate southeast trade winds predominate; the period from June to August has exhibited the driest and coolest conditions.

--- SENTENCE 129 ---
Weakened variable winds occurring during the wet season cause the high temperatures and heavy rains which persist from January to March.

--- SENTENCE 130 ---
Average annual rainfall measured at the Pago Pago International Airport weather station is 3,175 mm (125 inches).

--- SENTENCE 131 ---
However, rainfall varies greatly with elevation and aspect.

--- SENTENCE 132 ---
Pago Pago Harbor rainfall is estimated at 7,600 mm (300 inches).

--- SENTENCE 133 ---
Except the harbor and sheltered bays, the entire south coast of Tutuila receives offshore swells generated by the trade winds, which breaks on the reef as heavy surf.

--- SENTENCE 134 ---
There are several Marine Protected Areas in Tutuila Island, under different statutes.

--- SENTENCE 135 ---
The National Park of American Samoa (NPSA, <a href="http://www.nps.gov/npsa/">http://www.nps.gov/npsa/</a>) is situated in the North East of the Island.

--- SENTENCE 136 ---
Commercial fishing is prohibited in the marine part of the Park.

--- SENTENCE 137 ---
There is a Marine Sanctuary in Fagatele Bay situated in the South-East of Tutuila (<a href="http://www.fbnms.nos.noaa.gov/">http://www.fbnms.nos.noaa.gov/</a>).

--- SENTENCE 138 ---
Sanctuary regulations prohibit taking invertebrates found in the bay.

--- SENTENCE 139 ---
There are several MPAs set up, and managed by villages.

#### 3.2.1.

--- SENTENCE 148 ---
Habitat

All these species are nocturnal, deeply hidden in caves and corals by day.

--- SENTENCE 149 ---
P. versicolor (Figure 3), the painted spiny lobster, is very rare.

--- SENTENCE 150 ---
This species lives in protected coral environments (garden corals) which are mainly present in lagoons (George, 1972), and sometimes on the outer slope.

!

--- SENTENCE 151 ---
Figure 3: Geographic distribution of Panulirus versicolor (Holthuis, 1985)

The most common species is *P. penicillatus*, the pronghorn spiny lobster (Figure 4).

--- SENTENCE 152 ---
Amongst Palinuridae, this species has the widest distribution, from East Africa, the Red Sea, through Indian and Pacific oceans, including Hawaii and even to the west coast of Mexico (Johnson, 1971; Holthuis, 1991).

--- SENTENCE 153 ---
It lives only in clear water, strong current and surf conditions (George, 1969; Prescott, 1977, 1980a, 1988) at a range depth from 1 to 5 m (3 to 16 feet).

--- SENTENCE 154 ---
This species is highly cryptic/lucifugous by day, deeply hidden in coral crevices.

--- SENTENCE 155 ---
It forages by night between the beginning of the reef flat and the spurs and grooves (McDonald, 1971; Richer de Forges & Laboute, 1995; Coutures & Chauvet, 2003).

--- SENTENCE 156 ---
'The adaptation of robust pereiopods characteristic of P. penicillatus e nables this species to forage in strong surge and probably provides the selective advantage that allowed this species to penetrate and successfully occupy this habitat, which may serve as a refuge from predation" (MacDonald, 1988).

!

--- SENTENCE 157 ---
Figure 4: Geographic distribution of *Panulirus penicillatus* (Holthuis, 1985)

--- SENTENCE 158 ---
Parribacus antarcticus (Figure 5), the sculptured or Chinese mitten lobster, lives in rocky and coral dens, at depth less than 10 m.

--- SENTENCE 159 ---
In coral reefs, it is present on the entire reef (outer slope, reef flat, inner slope if present), and is probably gregarious (Holthuis, 1985).

--- SENTENCE 160 ---
(Figure 5), the Caledonian mitten lobster (called also 'butterfly lobster', *papata* in Samoan), lives in the same area as *P. penicillatus* (George, 1972).

--- SENTENCE 161 ---
Its geographic distribution includes Australia, New Caledonia, Vanuatu, Fiji and the Samoa Archipelago (Holthuis, 1985).

--- SENTENCE 162 ---
By day *P. caledonicus* is gregarious; individuals live in crevices often beneath coral patches, generally hanging upside down, under the domes of dens (Coutures, 2000a; Coutures & Chauvet, 2003).

--- SENTENCE 163 ---
By night, they forage individually, mainly on the beginning of the reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 164 ---
This species can sometimes be abundant (Coutures & Chauvet, 2003).

--- SENTENCE 165 ---
These two species are distinguishable by the number of teeth on the side of the basal segment of the antenna: 6 for *P. antarcticus* and 7 for *P. caledonicus* (Holthuis, 1985).

#### 3.2.2.

--- SENTENCE 167 ---
Diet

Scyllarids and palinurids are commonly considered to be opportunistic omnivorous i.e. they are able to feed on a wide range of food (Phillips et al., 1980).

--- SENTENCE 168 ---
Chambers and Nunes (1975) indicated that the diet of *P. penicillatus* in American Samoa was "primarily, if not solely, algae".

--- SENTENCE 169 ---
However, other studies showed that this species also feeds on Annelids Polychaetes, Mollusks (Gastropods and Bivalve), Crustaceans, and Echinoderms (George, 1972; Prescott in Pitcher, 1993).

--- SENTENCE 170 ---
Whereas only a few studies have dealt with the slipper lobster diet, they showed similar patterns to that for spiny lobsters (Lau, 1987; Spanier, 1987; Coutures, pers.

--- SENTENCE 171 ---
The main fact, observed in all studies, is that theses animals are able to select their food according to availability and density.

--- SENTENCE 172 ---
Thus, individuals of the same species but which are living in different areas can have different diets implying important differences in growth rates (Morgan, 1980; McKoy & Esterman, 1981; Edgar, 1990).

#### 3.2.3.

--- SENTENCE 173 ---
Growth and mortality

Lobsters, as other Crustaceans, increase in size by molting, while the growth of tissues (and thus the weight) is continuous.

--- SENTENCE 174 ---
The growth rate decreases with age, and the inter-molt duration increases.

--- SENTENCE 175 ---
During the molt, most of the calcified components are replaced.

--- SENTENCE 176 ---
Molts may not be isometric:

- 1).

--- SENTENCE 177 ---
Larvae are released with only 3 pairs of pereiopods (walking legs), the two other pairs appearing and increasing in size progressively with following molts.
- 2).

--- SENTENCE 178 ---
Broken appendages (legs or antenna, partly or totally) are regenerated at the following molt but the full regeneration must take several molts.
- 3).

--- SENTENCE 179 ---
Metamorphosis from the larvae into the post-larvae occurs in a single molt, considered to be one of the deepest transformations within Decapods (Gurney, 1936).
- 4).

--- SENTENCE 180 ---
When juveniles reach sexual maturity, a molt will generate the sexual characteristics: pleopod (swimmerets) biramous for females (the 2<sup>nd</sup> part, cross-

shaped will include setae to bind and maintain eggs), uniramous for males; within females, the fifth pair of legs terminates by a small claw (subchelae used during reproduction).

--- SENTENCE 181 ---
And last, there is sexual dimorphism, with males reaching larger sizes than females.

--- SENTENCE 182 ---
For *P. penicillatus*, this dimorphism is easily visible, males having larger carapace, and longer third pair of legs (to facilitate the mating).

--- SENTENCE 183 ---
The largest males can weight 3 kg (6.5 Lb) for 16 cm (6 ¼ in) of Cephalothoracic (or Carapace) Length (CL: measured from the basis of the supraorbital spines to the end of the cephalothorax) (Richer de Forges & Laboute, 1995).

--- SENTENCE 184 ---
The most commonly used growth model for lobsters is that of von Bertalanffy (1938) (Morgan, 1980):  $CL_t = CL_{\infty}$ .

--- SENTENCE 185 ---
(1 -  $e^{-K(t-to)}$ ) where  $CL_{\infty}$  is the asymptotic length, K the growth rate, and  $t_0$  the time when length should theoretically be zero.

--- SENTENCE 186 ---
The von Bertalanffy growth curve (VBGC) parameters may be established from growth data obtained with mark and recapture studies [for *P. penicillatus*: Ebert & Ford (1986) in Marshall Islands], length-frequency analysis methods (e.g. Bhattacharya; Cassie; ELEFAN, program; Wetherall; for *P. penicillatus*: Munro (1988) in Tonga, Arellano (1989) in Philippines, and Coutures & Chauvet (2002) in New Caledonia], and recently by measuring the lipofuscin rate, this pigment accumulating with age (Crossland *et al.*, 1987;

--- SENTENCE 187 ---
Sheehy *et al.*, 1998).

--- SENTENCE 188 ---
The total mortality coefficient - Z- may be established from VBGC parameters using Beverton & Holt formula (1956):

$$Z = K \cdot \frac{CL_{\infty} - \overline{CL}}{\overline{CL} - CL'}$$

where  $\overline{CL}$  is the mean Carapace Length in the catches, and CL' "some length for which all fish of that length and longer are under full exploitation" (Beverton & Holt, 1956).

--- SENTENCE 189 ---
Z, the total mortality coefficient, is the sum of F the fishing mortality, and M the natural mortality.

--- SENTENCE 190 ---
In order to obtain F, we must estimate M by analyzing data from a reserve without fishing (and then, Z = M).

#### 3.2.4.

--- SENTENCE 191 ---
Reproduction

For a review, see Nakamura, 1994; Kittaka & MacDiarmid, 1994.

--- SENTENCE 192 ---
Reproduction varies within the Palinuroidea (Berry, 1970; Prescott, 1988).

--- SENTENCE 193 ---
For Crustaceans, the reproductive season is determined by photoperiod and luminosity (Lipcius & Cobb, 1994).

--- SENTENCE 194 ---
As a result, lobsters from temperate and sub-tropical waters have only one spawning period (Chubb, 1994; Kittaka & MacDiarmid, 1994)

--- SENTENCE 195 ---
whereas numerous tropical species are able to spawn nearly year-round, and some individuals are able to spawn several times in the same year (Berry, 1971; Moore & MacFarlane, 1984; Juinio, 1987).

--- SENTENCE 196 ---
In Hawaii, 40 % of the *P. penicillatus* females are berried (with eggs under the abdomen)

--- SENTENCE 197 ---
(McDonald, 1971, 1979) at any time, and some females spawn 2 or 3 times a year (Juinio, 1987; Plaut, 1993).

--- SENTENCE 198 ---
Fertilization is external (Lyons, 1970; Morgan, 1980); the male deposits a spermatophore, via paired penile projections at the base of the fifth walking legs, onto the female sternum.

--- SENTENCE 199 ---
This spermatophoric mass becomes black and is butterfly-shaped for spiny lobsters and covers the two first pairs of pleopods in *Parribacus* spp.

--- SENTENCE 200 ---
The female is then called « tarred ».

--- SENTENCE 201 ---
After a few days, the female spawns several thousands of oocytes from the paired gonopores situated at the base of the third walking legs, into a 'chamber' formed by curving the abdomen under the sternum.

--- SENTENCE 202 ---
The female scrapes the spermatophore with special chelae (claws or pincher) on the dactyl (last segment) of the fifth walking legs, to release the sperm which fertilizes the oocytes during the transit (Berry, 1970; Lipcius & Herrnkind, 1987; Pitcher, 1993).

--- SENTENCE 203 ---
The fertilized eggs adhere to the ovigerous setae of the internal part of the biramous pleopods (Berry, 1970) (see Appendix I).

--- SENTENCE 204 ---
In the literature, the smallest *P. penicillatus* berried females measured from 4.08 cm of CL (1.6 inches) in Philippines (Juinio, 1987) to 6.20 cm (2.45 inches) in the Marshall Islands (Ebert & Ford, 1986).

--- SENTENCE 205 ---
No information on any *Parribacus* spp.

--- SENTENCE 206 ---
reproduction is available in the literature.

--- SENTENCE 207 ---
After 1 to 9 weeks of incubation [20 to 35 days for *P. penicillatus* (Juinio, 1987; Plaut, 1993)], larvae are released in the field (George, 1958; Chittleborough, 1974, 1976; Nair *et al.*, 1981; Pitcher, 1993).

--- SENTENCE 208 ---
Among spiny lobsters, hatching always occurs in an area allowing the larvae to quickly drift offshore (Phillips & Sastry, 1980; Coutures,

2000b), which implies that sometimes there is migration from coastal to oceanic areas (Moore & MacFarlane, 1984; MacFarlane & Moore, 1986; Coutures, 2000b).

--- SENTENCE 209 ---
The larvae of Palinuroidea (spiny lobster or Palinuridae, slipper lobster or Scyllaridae, and Synaxidae or coral lobsters) is called phyllosoma (from Greek "phyllo" leaf, and "soma" body), a flat, transparent, leaf-like larva, which corresponds to the Crustacean zoea stage (Figure 6).

--- SENTENCE 210 ---
Larval/planktonic phase

The outstanding feature in the life history of the Palinuroidea is the exceptional duration of their larval phase, which varies among spiny lobsters from 6 to 12 months according to the species (Phillips & Sastry, 1980; Booth & Phillips, 1994) and even 24 months for Jasus e dwardsii (Lesser, 1978), a species o ccurring in New Zealand and South Australia, and from 1 to 9 months for slipper lobsters (Sims, 1965; Robertson, 1968a,b, 1969a,b; Takahashi & Saisho, 1978; Ito & Lucas, 1990;

--- SENTENCE 211 ---
Marinovic et al., 1994; Mikami & Greenwood, 1997).

--- SENTENCE 212 ---
During the larval phase, phyllosomata can travel great distances, e.g. Johnson (1974) caught one Panulirus penicillatus phyllosoma in the Pacific Ocean 3,700 km from the Galapagos Archipelago, which was the closest hatching site.

--- SENTENCE 213 ---
Mechanisms allowing phyllosomata to come back to the coast have been shown only for a few species, especially for Panulirus cygnus in the southwest of Australia (see Pearce & Phillips, 1980; Phillips et al., 1991; Pearce et al., 1992).

--- SENTENCE 214 ---
However, for species with a wide geographic distribution such as P. penicillatus and P. parribacus, the larval dispersion implies that recruits can come from adults living at great distances (Lyons, 1980, 1981; Menzies & Kerrigan, 1980).

--- SENTENCE 215 ---
Oceanic currents and gyres seem important in the transport, and the dispersion, and thus, in the "oceanic routes" used by phyllosomata (Yeung & McGowan, 1991; Booth & Phillips, 1994).

--- SENTENCE 216 ---
During their larval development, phyllosomata increase in size and general shape through numerous molts.

--- SENTENCE 217 ---
For the species studied, systematicians have described 7 to 12 larval stages which characterize the growth by appearance of anatomic features (e.g.: Stage I, 3 pairs of pereiopods, stage II: bud of the fourth one), and characteristic measurements (Phillips & Sastry, 1980).

--- SENTENCE 218 ---
The different larval stages of *P. penicillatus* have been described by Johnson (1968) and Michel (1969, 1971).

--- SENTENCE 219 ---
The duration of the larval species has been estimated to be 7 - 8 months for this species (Johnson, 1971).

--- SENTENCE 220 ---
For *P. caledonicus*, stage I is the only stage to have been described so far with certainty (Coutures, 2001b).

--- SENTENCE 221 ---
It must be noted that in the Pacific, *Parribacus* species form a complex including a widely distributed species – *P. antarcticus* – and 5 allopatric species (without overlap of their adult distributions).

--- SENTENCE 222 ---
Larvae of this complex are very similar (Coutures, 2001b), and sometimes impossible to separate (Prasad & Tampi, 1960; Michel, 1971; Berry, 1974).

--- SENTENCE 223 ---
Furthermore, some giant larvae – from 6.5 to 8 cm (2.55 to 3.14 inches) of total length, without pereiopods - have been caught in the Pacific, Indian, and Atlantic Oceans, that would correspond to the late-stage of *P. antarcticus* (Johnson, 1951; Robertson, 1968c).

--- SENTENCE 224 ---
When phyllosomata have reached the last larval stage, have enough body reserves, and may be after stimulation from a coastal signal (Booth, 1986; Phillips & McWilliam, 1986a; Booth & Phillips, 1994; McWilliam & Phillips, 1997), they metamorphose into post-larvae called nisto for slipper lobsters and puerulus for spiny lobsters (Lyons, 1970).

--- SENTENCE 225 ---
Post-larval/pelagic phase

Puerulus and nistos differ from juveniles by the absence of calcification and lack of pigmentation, except for the eyes and a few streaks and points on the antennas (Phillips & Sastry, 1980)

--- SENTENCE 226 ---
The carapace is smoother, pleopods bear long setae (Phillips & Penrose, 1985), the body is longer, antennas are longer (only for spiny lobsters), and sometimes they terminate in a spatula (Serfling & Ford, 1975; Briones-Fourzan & McWilliam, 1997) (Fig. 5).

--- SENTENCE 227 ---
According to numerous authors (Serfling & Ford, 1975; Sweat, 1968; Witham et al., 1968; Phillips, 1972; Phillips & Olsen, 1975; Serfling & Ford, 1975; Phillips et al, 1978; Lyons, 1980; Calinski & Lyons, 1983; McDonald, 1986; Hayakawa et al., 1990; Coutures, 2000a, 2001a; Coutures & Chauvet, 2002), the behavior of pueruli and nistos would be to actively swim to benthic coastal habitats (with the aid of their pleopods) before settling onto the benthos.

--- SENTENCE 228 ---
For example, the puerulus of

Panulirus cygnus can swim from 20 to 60 km (12.5 to 37.5 miles) (Phillips & Penrose, 1985), in 5 to 15 days (Lemmens, 1994).

--- SENTENCE 229 ---
The post-larvae begins to be colored as soon as it settles on the bottom.

--- SENTENCE 230 ---
The first molt occurs 8 to 10 days after settlement (Phillips & Sastry, 1980).

--- SENTENCE 231 ---
Although it is not the case for all species, *Panulirus penicillatus* and *Parribacus caledonicus* settle in the same habitat as the adults (McDonald, 1971; Nunes & Chambers, 1975; Pitcher, 1992, 1993; Coutures, 2000a; Coutures *et al.*, 2002), i.e. just in front of the breakers.

--- SENTENCE 232 ---
The puerulus of *Panulirus penicillatus* has been described by Michel (1971): it measures about 1 cm (0.4 inch) of CL.

--- SENTENCE 233 ---
The nisto of *Parribacus caledonicus* has not been described yet but it is likely to be similar to congeners which reach sizes varying from 5 to 5.82 cm (2-2.3 inches) (Rathbun, 1903;

--- SENTENCE 234 ---
Coutures *et al.*, 2002).

#### 3.5.

--- SENTENCE 236 ---
Rearing/Aquaculture/farming

Only a few species have been reared from the egg to the adult.

--- SENTENCE 237 ---
For spiny lobsters, only temperate species have been successfully reared (Kittaka 1988;

--- SENTENCE 238 ---
Kittaka & Ikegami, 1988; Kittaka et al., 1988; Kittaka & Kimura, 1989; Kittaka, 1994a; Kittaka et al., 1997), and only two small slipper lobsters (Robertson, 1968b; Ito & Lucas, 1990).

--- SENTENCE 239 ---
Survival rates are always very low, and large scale aquaculture is not economically viable (Kittaka, 1994b; Kittaka & Booth, 1994), but studies are continuing (Kittaka, 1997; Kittaka et al., 2001; Moss et al., 2001).

--- SENTENCE 240 ---
Another method consists of catching pueruli and then growing juveniles in tanks (Booth & Kittaka, 1994).

--- SENTENCE 241 ---
Pueruli are taken by collectors along many shorelines in larval recruitment studies, sometimes in high numbers (for a review, see Phillips & Booth, 1994).

--- SENTENCE 242 ---
Sea-farming has begun to take place in several areas, such as South-East Asia, Europe, New Zealand, Australia (Booth & Kittaka, 1994).

--- SENTENCE 243 ---
Some trial collecting in coral reef environments did not successfully catch any lobsters (Coutures, 2000a).

--- SENTENCE 244 ---
P. penicillatus and P. caledonicus settle under the breakers, i.e. in a rough environment where setting collectors is impossible.

--- SENTENCE 245 ---
Some individuals of *P. penicillatus* and *Parribacus* spp. have been caught with crest nets in French Polynesia, and Wallis (Dufour, pers.

--- SENTENCE 246 ---
com.; Coutures et al., 2002).

--- SENTENCE 247 ---
A net set on the reefflat in the region of the breaking waves acts as a passive sampler, which filters water coming from the ocean (Dufour, 1992).

--- SENTENCE 248 ---
This efficient technique is used to catch ornamental fish and invertebrates for the aquarium trade (Aquafish Technology, <a href="http://www.aqua-fish.com/">http://www.aqua-fish.com/</a>).

--- SENTENCE 249 ---
Unfortunately, the two main species present in American Samoa settle in front of where the waves break, i.e. before reaching the sampling site.

--- SENTENCE 250 ---
In French Polynesia, and in Wallis, the largest catches of lobsters were obtained at times of strong swell, which could be explained by this.

--- SENTENCE 251 ---
This technique would be efficient for species which settle in coastal areas, or in lagoons, as such as P. versicolor.

#### 3.6.

--- SENTENCE 254 ---
Fishing techniques and abundance

Spiny lobsters support some of the largest commercial fisheries in the world, while concurrently sustaining small-scale artisanal fisheries on remote islands or where they are in low abundance (Lipcius & Cobb, 1994).

--- SENTENCE 255 ---
There are only a few techniques used to capture spiny lobsters in the region.

--- SENTENCE 256 ---
Most are caught by hand or by spear, some are taken incidentally in tangle nets, and a few are trapped (Munro, 1994).

--- SENTENCE 257 ---
P. penicillatus is fished throughout its geographic distribution, and provides the main landings in the Central Pacific (George, 1974; Munro, 1988; Prescott, 1988).

--- SENTENCE 258 ---
This species must be trapped in some places (McDonald, 1971; Prescott, 1988) but yields are weak except in the Solomon Islands where they reached 15 kg (33 Lb) per trap per night (George, 1972).

--- SENTENCE 259 ---
George (1972) also reported that in 1965, tests with rectangular traps (1.2 x 0.9 x 0.9 m - 4 x 3 x 3 feet) made of 7.6 x 10.2 cm (3 x 4 inches) welded metal mesh and baited tuna heads produced catches of about 15 large P. penicillatus per haul in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 260 ---
The experiment was terminated when the traps were stolen.

--- SENTENCE 261 ---
No information was given on the depth at which the traps were set.

--- SENTENCE 262 ---
These results are doubtful and I will discuss this anecdotical report of a high catch in the discussion.

--- SENTENCE 263 ---
Tangle nets are efficient in the Philippines out of Monsoon season (Juinio & Gomez, 1986).

--- SENTENCE 264 ---
Everywhere else, *P. penicillatus* is fished by hand by day or by night.

--- SENTENCE 265 ---
Fishermen walk onto the barrier reef at low tide.

--- SENTENCE 266 ---
By day, fishermen look for animals in their den, and by night they catch the animals which are foraging (George, 1972; Prescott, 1988; Pitcher, 1993).

--- SENTENCE 267 ---
By walking or by free diving, they catch lobsters on the edge of the outer slope using waterproof flashlights (in the past with coconut flare-torches) (Pitcher, 1993).

--- SENTENCE 268 ---
In American Samoa, George (1972) indicated that *P. penicillatus* was fished by teams of about 4 people diving and spearing at night on the reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 269 ---
Catches were usually around 10 lobsters per night, but could reached 30.

--- SENTENCE 270 ---
As they live mostly in the same area, all these techniques also allow catching *Parribacus caledonicus*.

--- SENTENCE 271 ---
In the South-western part of New Caledonia, fishing on foot during the day produces the best yields (Coutures & Chauvet, 2003).

--- SENTENCE 272 ---
Fishing is highly dependant on the weather, becoming impossible during rough sea periods.

--- SENTENCE 273 ---
As a result, areas that are particularly exposed, such as the narrow reef zone situated just under the

breakers, are rarely exploited (Juinio & Gomez, 1986; Coutures, 2000a; Coutures & Chauvet, 2003).

--- SENTENCE 274 ---
The abundance of *P. penicillatus* has been studied by Ebert & Ford (1986) in the Marshall Islands where the population is estimated to be a maximum of 126 lobsters per linear km (0.62 miles) of barrier reef.

--- SENTENCE 275 ---
Same values (111 to 128 lobsters per km) have reported in the Solomon Archipelago by Prescott (1988).

--- SENTENCE 276 ---
In American Samoa, there are several fishing regulations concerning lobsters.

--- SENTENCE 277 ---
It is unlawful:

- to use spears or snagging devices for the collection of slipper lobster (g. *Parribacus*);
- to take, possess, sell or offer for sell any egg-bearing spiny lobster;
- to take any spiny lobster that measures less than 3 ½ inches (7.94 cm) in carapace length.

--- SENTENCE 278 ---
Furthermore, scuba fishing was officially banned in April 2002.

#### 4.

--- SENTENCE 279 ---
STUDY OF THE AMERICAN SAMOAN LOBSTER POPULATION

Management measures to reduce inshore fishing pressure have been introduced recently, e.g. the SCUBA fishing ban in 2002.

--- SENTENCE 280 ---
However, there is little data on current inshore fishing activity, and we need to determine inshore fishery trends, determine the level and nature (e.g. recreational or commercial) of fishing activity, identify target species and determine the effectiveness of current management regimes.

--- SENTENCE 281 ---
The overall objective is to acquire information to support the development of an inshore/coral reef management plan.

--- SENTENCE 282 ---
The study of the lobster fishery is part of this plan.

--- SENTENCE 283 ---
I divided the study in two parts, one made in the field to determine biological and population characteristics (growth, density, behavior, fecundity, habitat, natural and fishing mortalities etc), and one called landings survey which consisted of a study of the commercial fishery through market surveys, and by checking imports from other islands.

--- SENTENCE 284 ---
Sampling protocols, including market and field surveys had been approved by Dr. Chris Evans, former Chief Biologist at DMWR, who wrote the initial project proposal.

#### 4.1.1.1.

--- SENTENCE 287 ---
Sampling protocol

Field surveys began in mid-October 2002.

--- SENTENCE 288 ---
They consisted in two night dives per week.

--- SENTENCE 289 ---
Fifteen sites around Tutuila Island (Figure 7) were to be sampled; each site had to be sampled every 3 months.

--- SENTENCE 290 ---
During each night, the site was selected according to the weather.

--- SENTENCE 291 ---
On Tutuila Island, there are only two boat launching ramps, one at the end of Pago Pago harbor, and the other in Fagasa in the middle of the northern coast.

--- SENTENCE 292 ---
As a result, the two extreme ends of the island, west and east, were sampled only during very good weather.

--- SENTENCE 293 ---
Each night consisted to two free-dives of 60 to 90 minutes according to the site.

--- SENTENCE 294 ---
Diving conditions were estimated upon arrival on a new site during the day, and the journey/run along the reef evaluated.

--- SENTENCE 295 ---
Coordinates were taken with a GPS.

--- SENTENCE 296 ---
At dusk, two or three free divers followed the reef edge between spurs and groves with excursions onto the reef flat if the breakers allowed it.

--- SENTENCE 297 ---
We tried to catch <u>all lobsters</u> seen by hand, without consideration about regulations (berried females, limited size).

!

--- SENTENCE 298 ---
Lobster survey locations around Tutuila (produced by Will White)

--- SENTENCE 299 ---
At the end of each dive, lobsters were brought back on board.

--- SENTENCE 300 ---
We noted the species, the sex, noting whether females were tarred or berried if necessary, and the number of appendages (antennas or walking legs) broken.

--- SENTENCE 301 ---
Then each animal was weighted (+/-10 g) with spring scales, and the CL was measured with a slider caliper to the nearest mm along the mid-dorsal line from the anterior end and between the post-orbital spines to the posterior edge.

--- SENTENCE 302 ---
All animals other than berried females were tagged with a Floy spaghetti tag inserted dorsally between the cephalothorax and the abdomen.

--- SENTENCE 303 ---
Each tag was numbered and included the DMWR phone number (Figure 8).

--- SENTENCE 304 ---
Animals were then released on the reef edge.

--- SENTENCE 305 ---
Berried females were kept to study fecundity at the laboratory, except in Marine Reserves (Fagatele Bay and National Park) where our permit did not allow bringing animals back.

Catch composition and CPUE

The catch composition in terms of species, sex-ratio, average length, and Catch per Unit Effort (weight or number of individuals fished), were estimated in order to compare our values with the data obtained from the commercial fishery.

Sex-ratio was compared to the 1:1 expected frequency with a  $\chi^2$  test (Sokal & Rohlf, 1995).

Estimation of the population size

The capture/tagging/recapture technique was set up to estimate the population at the scale of each site, and with the usual assumptions (no lost tags or death, no emigrants, no immigrants).

--- SENTENCE 310 ---
These tags remain readable through several molts.

--- SENTENCE 311 ---
We expected to catch less and less untagged individuals at each trip<sup>2</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 312 ---
Densities at each site would be estimated per reef km, and thus, data expanded to the Tutuila coastline.

#### 4.1.1.4.

--- SENTENCE 313 ---
Fecundity and size at sexual maturity

The fecundity, estimated by the number of eggs as a function of the size, is a fundamental fact for determining a species' reproductive potential.

--- SENTENCE 314 ---
These data are useful to manage this high-value resource.

--- SENTENCE 315 ---
When berried females were caught in the field, they were brought back to the laboratory.

--- SENTENCE 316 ---
Each egg mass with pleopod attached was removed while the lobster was fresh and was fixed in a 10 % formalin and seawater solution.

--- SENTENCE 317 ---
A waterproof label indicating the species, the CL, the date and the site was placed in the jar.

--- SENTENCE 318 ---
After hardening for about 30 days, eggs were excised from pleopods and dried to a constant weight (60°C/140°F during 3 days).

--- SENTENCE 319 ---
The dried eggs were then weighed to the nearest mg.

--- SENTENCE 320 ---
Three replicate 0.1 g samples were taken from each mass of dried, rubbed between the fingers to separate the eggs and remove any attached setal material, and counting manually.

--- SENTENCE 321 ---
Egg breakage was negligible.

--- SENTENCE 322 ---
The average of the three samples was used to calculate the total number of eggs for each respective female.

--- SENTENCE 323 ---
Drying, weighing, and counting were done at the American Samoa Community College in the Soil Science Laboratory run by Dr. Don Vargo.

--- SENTENCE 324 ---
Berried females were sometimes seized by the DMWR Enforcement staff, and they have been included in the sampling.

--- SENTENCE 325 ---
Size at sexual maturity was estimated as the size at which 50 % of the females are berried (ovigerous).

--- SENTENCE 326 ---
In order to obtain values of growth, a public notice (for seafood markets) was made to advertise fishermen who caught tagged lobster in the field (Appendix II & III)

#### 4.1.2.

--- SENTENCE 327 ---
Study of the commercial fishery

Market surveys began in early October 2002.

--- SENTENCE 328 ---
They consisted of two samplings per week and later four a week in markets which sell seafoods all around Tutuila Island.

--- SENTENCE 329 ---
Furthermore, import surveys were carried out.

--- SENTENCE 330 ---
Landings from Manu'a Islands and "Western" Samoa were checked from the port and the airport, both twice a month.

--- SENTENCE 331 ---
Market surveys were done early in the morning, for two reasons.

--- SENTENCE 332 ---
First, the fishermen bring in their catches in the morning, and the data collectors can meet and interview them in order to obtain information about the fishing party: fishing site, number of divers, duration etc (Figure 9).

--- SENTENCE 333 ---
Secondly, lobster measurement is a long process and data collectors must not impede their work, so this is avoided by doing the market survey early.

All lobsters were measured, weighed, species and sex were noted, as well as the female reproductive state, and if animals had been speared or not.

--- SENTENCE 335 ---
Raw data including different variables from previous market surveys have been analyzed to compare with the new data in order to show if the pattern of the lobster catches by commercial fishermen has changed between the two periods.

--- SENTENCE 336 ---
These data (56 fishing teams interviewed; N = 465 lobsters sampled), covering the period between June and November 1999, included in addition to weight and length: the technique used (free or scuba-diving), the species, the sex and the number of divers involved in the fishing outing.

--- SENTENCE 340 ---
The Beverton and Holt's Relative Yield-per-recruit model (Sparre & Venema, 1989) was carried out for assessing the effect of minimum size regulations on the fishery.

--- SENTENCE 341 ---
This 'relative yield per recruit model' developed by Beverton and Holt (1966) is based on length, whose measurement is relatively accurate.

--- SENTENCE 342 ---
This model describes "the state of the stock when the fishing pattern has been the same for such a long time that all fish alive have been exposed to it since they recruited" (Sparre & Venema, 1989).

--- SENTENCE 343 ---
(Y/R)' can be calculated for given input values of M/K,  $CL_{\infty}$  and  $CL_{c}$  (the length at recruitment) for values of E ranging from 0 to 1, corresponding to F values from 0 to  $CL_{\infty}$ .

$$(Y/R)' = E * U^{M/K} *

--- SENTENCE 344 ---
\left[ 1 - \frac{3U}{1+m} + \frac{3U^2}{1+2m} - \frac{3U^3}{1+3m} \right]$$

$$m = \frac{K}{Z}$$

$$U = 1 - \frac{CL_c}{CL_{\infty}}$$
 the fraction of growth to be completed after entry into the exploited phase 
$$E = \frac{F}{Z}$$
 the exploitation rate of the fraction of deaths caused by fishing

"The plot of (Y/R)' against E gives a curve with a maximum value,  $E_{MSY}$  (Maximum Sustainable Yield), for a given value of  $CL_c$ .

--- SENTENCE 345 ---
Thus, when  $CL_c$ , F and M/K are known for a certain fishery the actual exploitation rate can be compared with the  $E_{MSY}$  level and management measures be proposed as necessary" (Sparre & Venema, 1989).

--- SENTENCE 495 ---
Catch Composition

The 3 sampling sets (field survey 2002, market survey 1999, and market survey 2002-2003) show different patterns in terms of species composition and sex-ratio.

--- SENTENCE 496 ---
The artisanal fishery targets mainly spiny lobsters, other crustaceans being caught opportunistically.

--- SENTENCE 497 ---
Results of interviews with fishermen do not show a tendency to keep slipper lobsters or crabs for self-consumption.

--- SENTENCE 498 ---
They catch and sell all crustaceans seen in the fishing area but few fishermen bring a bag into the field to catch slipper lobsters and crabs without spearing them.

--- SENTENCE 499 ---
It may be noted that the purchase and selling prices (mean \$3.95, and \$5.05 per lb, respectively) are equal for all the lobsters, implying that fishermen do not hesitate to sell all their catches.

--- SENTENCE 500 ---
The difference between our field sampling and the commercial catches is certainly due to two factors.

--- SENTENCE 501 ---
Firstly, the fishing effort is focused near the spurs and grooves where fishermen target reef fishes and spiny lobsters.

--- SENTENCE 502 ---
During the field survey, we swam in a zigzag fashion, coming from the spurs to the beginning of the reef flat near the breakers.

--- SENTENCE 503 ---
Thus, we covered part of the habitat of the 2 species.

--- SENTENCE 504 ---
Secondly, the spear fishing technique associated with the system to carry the catches on a line limit the catch of slipper lobsters (which cannot be speared by law) and crabs.

--- SENTENCE 505 ---
In the same way, the sex-ratio is strongly in favor of males in the 2 market samples, and weakly for females in the field survey (with smaller number of samples).

--- SENTENCE 506 ---
Zann (1984) in Tonga and deBruin (1962) in Sri Lanka found the sex ratio of *P. penicillatus* favored males, whereas it was the reverse in the Solomon (Prescott, 1988) and in the Marshall (Ebert & Ford, 1986).

--- SENTENCE 507 ---
Ebert & Ford (1986) indicated that females came further up the reef flat than the males, and hence were more likely to be captured by walking on the reef flat, as they did.

--- SENTENCE 508 ---
MacDonald (1979) indicated that more males of *P. penicillatus* were caught in Hawaii, maybe because traps were set in deeper areas.

--- SENTENCE 509 ---
This behavior would explain the difference shown in our results, the fishermen staying in spurs and groves, whereas we also searched the reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 510 ---
Another or complementary explanation has been proposed by Shacklee (1983) who showed for *Panulirus* 

marginatus (a species endemic to Hawaii) a natural non-equal sex ratio from the eggs to the adult population.

--- SENTENCE 511 ---
For *Parribacus parribacus*, our catches included more males than females perhaps for the same reason.

--- SENTENCE 512 ---
In New Caledonia (Coutures, pers.

--- SENTENCE 513 ---
and in Tonga (Prescott, 1988), catches of this species made by walking at low tide on the reef flat showed a sex ratio in favor of females indicated a slight difference in the habitat of the sexes.

--- SENTENCE 514 ---
As a function of the number of days sampled at markets during the survey period versus the total week days per year, we estimated to 576 kg or 1271 lb (mean value) to be the total landings of lobsters in Tutuila between November 2002 and October 2003.

--- SENTENCE 515 ---
This value should be used with caution, since the expansion ignored the recreation/subsistence fishery, and the number of good weather days.

--- SENTENCE 516 ---
For a comparison, c atches in American S amoa was estimated to be less than 1 metric ton (2,200 lb) in 1985 and 1986 (FAO, 1987).

Average length and CPUE

Our results show a decrease in the average length between the two market surveys.

--- SENTENCE 544 ---
This difference seems to have two origins.

--- SENTENCE 545 ---
- Market surveys carried out in 1999 included data from scuba and free diving.

--- SENTENCE 546 ---
Comparisons of the average length per sex for the two fishing techniques show a significant difference in favor of scuba diving for both sex (respectively for male and female: p<0.001 and p<0.01).

--- SENTENCE 547 ---
Scuba diving allowed catching larger animals than free diving, because fishermen explored much better the deep part of their habitat, and also they were able to spend more time spearing and extracting animals deeply hidden in a den.
- Larger animals are easier to catch than small ones.

--- SENTENCE 548 ---
The scuba ban has been official only since April 2002 which is not long enough to allow a notable increasing length of the population.

--- SENTENCE 549 ---
If we pool all the data, the average length appears slightly smaller than in other places in the Pacific (see Prescott, 1988).

--- SENTENCE 550 ---
There is perhaps a depletion of the large animals due to increasing catches in the fishing area.

--- SENTENCE 551 ---
Tutuila is a small island, and fishable areas are not numerous.

--- SENTENCE 552 ---
In these sites, large animals tend to be removed by fishing.

--- SENTENCE 553 ---
Prescott (1988) and Coutures (2000a) observed the largest specimen of *P. penicillatus* in the highest latitudes of the species distribution.

--- SENTENCE 554 ---
However, the structure of the coral reef itself plays a role.

--- SENTENCE 555 ---
Larger animals need a large den to hide during the day, and the larger the animal, the larger the den must be.

--- SENTENCE 556 ---
There is only a fringing reef around Tutuila Island, and dens could be rarer than in areas where reefs are older and larger.

--- SENTENCE 557 ---
For example, Alega bay is an unofficial MPA, fished only once or twice a year.

During the field survey, we caught 5 spiny lobsters and saw 26 others but none measured more than 10 cm of CL.

--- SENTENCE 561 ---

- 1).

--- SENTENCE 562 ---
fishermen have become more and more professional and enhanced their efficiency, which is an important factor in CPUE analysis (Coutures & Chauvet, 2003);
- 2).

--- SENTENCE 563 ---
the ban on scuba diving implies that divers must stay in shallower water, so they are closer to the breakers, which corresponds to the main habitat of *P. penicillatus*.
- 3).

--- SENTENCE 564 ---
fishermen spend a longer part of the fishing trip targeting lobsters because the demand increased, or because of decreasing of other targeted species such as parrotfishes (Page, 1998).

--- SENTENCE 565 ---
Point 2 explains also why the ban of scuba diving has had few impacts on the lobster fishery.

--- SENTENCE 566 ---

--- SENTENCE 571 ---
Growth, mortality and Relative Yield per Recruit model

By using values of K (growth coefficient),  $CL_{\infty}$  (asymptotic Carapace Length),  $t_0$  (time at CL = 0) and M (natural mortality) found in the literature, the von Bertalanffy Growth Curve (VBGC) and the Yield-Per-Recruit (YPR) model fit our data well.

--- SENTENCE 572 ---
However, the size at recruitment and the mean CL are in concordance with literature and accurate enough to be confident of our results.

--- SENTENCE 573 ---
Although this model (Beverton & Holt's YPR) has been carried out in several lobster fisheries in the Pacific (Pitcher, 1993), the methodology of length-frequency analysis is based on several assumptions (Sparre & Venema, 1988) reproduced as following (with our comments in italics):

- 1.

--- SENTENCE 574 ---
Recruitment is constant, yet not specified

It can be assumed that the recruitment is constant if a size-limit is applied

- 2.

--- SENTENCE 575 ---
All fish of a cohort are hatched on the same date

There are no cohorts in this population, this species reproduces year-round, and we presume year- round recruitment

- 3.

--- SENTENCE 576 ---
Recruitment and selection are "knife-edge"

As in point 1, a lobster reaching the size-limit becomes 'catchable' by everyone

- 4.

--- SENTENCE 577 ---
The fishing and natural mortalities are constant from the moment of entry to the exploited phase

The ecological factors may be considered as constant in the P. penicillatus habitat, but large animals, mainly the old males, are obviously more vulnerable, M is certainly higher, by the lack of big dens available in the reef which implies an increasing predation (sharks, octopus), and large lobsters are more easily seen and speared than small ones

5.

--- SENTENCE 578 ---
There is a complete mixing within the stock

The large distribution of this species allows assuming this assumption

6.

--- SENTENCE 579 ---
The length-weight relationship has the exponent 3, i.e.  $W = a^*CL^3$ 

Lobster length-weight relationship is not perfectly cubic, the b coefficient is smaller than 3

Although all these assumptions are not totally verified in our study case, the role of this model is useful for the comparison between an actual situation and a projected one by changing values of F (e.g. by multiplying the number of fishermen) and CL at the recruitment (e.g. by implementation of a new size limit).

--- SENTENCE 580 ---
In other words: "It provides advice that helps managers prevent growth overfishing (a condition that occurs when small lobsters are caught before they have grown to optimum size) by indicating the optimum size at which the fishery can begin to catch lobsters at any given value of fishing mortality" (Pitcher, 1993).

--- SENTENCE 581 ---
Furthermore, YPR is considered as relatively robust compared with other assessments, provided that estimates of input parameters are accurate, and a high level of confidence can be placed in the management advice that YPR analysis provides (Gulland, 1983).

#### 5.

--- SENTENCE 592 ---
CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS

This study provides the first analysis of the current lobster fishery in Tutuila Island.

--- SENTENCE 593 ---
Panulirus penicillatus is the main species found in markets.

--- SENTENCE 594 ---
Fishermen spear lobsters incidentally while diving for finfish by night at the start of the outer reef slope.

--- SENTENCE 595 ---
Because of this technique, Parribacus caledonicus is rarely caught whereas they are numerous on the first part of the reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 596 ---
This species of high taste quality (per se and also because there are rarely speared) should be easily marketable at the same price as spiny lobsters.

--- SENTENCE 597 ---
All spiny lobsters are speared implying that fishermen determine if lobsters were undersized or berried after they are already dead<sup>4</sup>.

--- SENTENCE 598 ---
If market owners accept these illegal lobsters, they will be sold, if not, they will be used for home/self consumption.

--- SENTENCE 599 ---
So, enforcement must be applied in the same time in the markets, and during landings.

--- SENTENCE 600 ---
Furthermore, speared lobster flesh is of poor quality and could be hazardous if kept too long before cooking (due to bacteria contamination).

--- SENTENCE 601 ---
Thus, we recommend banning of the spear fishing of spiny lobsters as it is already done in other Pacific countries (Pitcher, 1993).

--- SENTENCE 602 ---
In New Caledonia, all Crustaceans must be sold whole and not speared, spear fishing being allowed by day for recreation or subsistence only (Coutures & Chauvet, 2003).

--- SENTENCE 603 ---
Fishermen will learn to catch lobsters by hand, and we advise the handbook of Prescott (1980b) edited for this purpose.

--- SENTENCE 604 ---
This author indicates also how fishermen can store lobsters alive in the water in home-made traps.

--- SENTENCE 605 ---
This stocking technique allow waiting for transportation or accumulating enough lobsters for sell (e.g. from Manu'a Islands).

--- SENTENCE 606 ---
After a few months, we believe that the efficiency of fishermen will be at the same level as with the spearfishing technique, or even will increase the CPUE from the catches of slipper lobsters.

--- SENTENCE 607 ---
The only risk is a depletion of the slipper lobster population, as they are easier to catch and for which there is no size limit.

--- SENTENCE 608 ---
A future survey focused on this species will be necessary to evaluate the impact of any new regulations.

--- SENTENCE 609 ---
<sup>&</sup>lt;sup>4</sup> It is known that professional fishermen are able to estimate perceptibly with high accuracy the length of lobsters or other finfish.

--- SENTENCE 610 ---
If animal is even so caught indicates that the fisherman knows that it is marketable or for self-consumption.

--- SENTENCE 611 ---
We did not have enough accurate data on imports from 'Western' Samoa to analyze, but we suspect that there is an increasing trend.

--- SENTENCE 612 ---
All new American Samoa regulations should be advertised through the Fisheries Division of Apia, or perhaps, we should share our data and implement the same regulations.

--- SENTENCE 613 ---
The size of the lobster fishery around Tutuila Island is consistent with the results of studies undertaken in other Pacific islands.

--- SENTENCE 614 ---
The landings are small, but reflect the small size of the island.

--- SENTENCE 615 ---
Fishing mortality is small, and there is no sign of overfishing.

--- SENTENCE 616 ---
The YPR model confirms that increasing of the length of recruitment from the actual value (6.9 cm / 2.72 in for males; 6.5 cm / 2.56 in) to the legal size (7.94 cm / 31/8 in) should increase the total catches slightly, especially for the males.

--- SENTENCE 617 ---
The females are more vulnerable because of their slower growth, but are less numerous in the catches because of the fishing technique.

--- SENTENCE 618 ---
We must note that the regulation banning the catch of berried females does not have a scientific basis (Pitcher, 1993, Coutures, 2000a).

--- SENTENCE 619 ---
It is clear that the recruitment level in Tutuila is not linked to the quantity of eggs released here.

--- SENTENCE 620 ---
Because of the very long time (9-10 months) during which spiny lobster phyllosoma larvae are transported by ocean currents (Booth & Phillips, 1991), recruits are able to come from distant islands (MacDonald, 1971).

--- SENTENCE 621 ---
The particular habitat of these species provides them a natural MPA in rough sites, which are rarely calm enough to be fished anywhere in the Pacific Ocean and in numerous remote islands they are unfished.

--- SENTENCE 622 ---
However this maybe not be the case for *Parribacus caledonicus* which has a smaller geographic distribution, and unknown larval behavior (Coutures, 2000b; Coutures, 2001b).

--- SENTENCE 623 ---
Genetic variation from different populations throughout the Pacific, both for spiny lobster and slipper lobster, should provide information on the dispersion level of the phyllosomata, and if self-recruitment is possible (Pitcher, 1993).

--- SENTENCE 624 ---
The field survey did not allow obtaining accurate growth values.

--- SENTENCE 625 ---
A potential survey would be to briefly study a well delimited unfished population (e.g. Fagatele Bay, if we consider that it is a real reserve).

--- SENTENCE 626 ---
A sampling protocol as such as Ebert & Ford (1986) used in Enewetok will provide accurate values of growth, natural mortality and abundance at the scale of the bay.

--- SENTENCE 627 ---
Then, these values can be used in the YPR model.

--- SENTENCE 628 ---
The habitat of this species is more or less the same throughout the Pacific Ocean, so we admit that values of growth and natural mortality drawn from the literature are close for populations living at the same latitude.

--- SENTENCE 629 ---
It must be noted that the absence of enforcement during the survey allowed measuring all the catches, and not only the marketable part.

--- SENTENCE 630 ---
Thus, accuracy of parameters, especially Z and F, was not biased by the lack of small lobsters or berried females caught but kept for home consumption.

#### Appendix IV

#### According to Anonymous (1986?)

--- SENTENCE 993 ---
[...] There are at least five management methods that could be applied to ['American'] Samoa's lobster resource to help insure a healthy population of harvestable adults.

--- SENTENCE 994 ---
These methods include the implementation of closed seasons, gear restrictions, size limits, marine sanctuaries and the selective harvest of non-berried lobsters.

--- SENTENCE 995 ---
The pros and cons of these management strategies as they pertain to Samoa will be discussed below.

--- SENTENCE 996 ---
Author's notes in red: remarks and regulations already implemented since this paper was written.

--- SENTENCE 997 ---
#### A. Gear Restrictions - No spearing

#### Pros

- 1.

--- SENTENCE 998 ---
Lobsters harvested by hand or traps are not harmed and undersize or berried individuals can be released.
- 2.

--- SENTENCE 999 ---
The increased difficulty in harvesting lobsters by hand aids management by reducing catches and therefore increasing spawning success.

--- SENTENCE 1000 ---
See B. Cons 2.
- 3.

--- SENTENCE 1001 ---
To sell living or at least whole animals decreases public health risks, and increases preservation duration and food quality.

--- SENTENCE 1002 ---
It makes possible stocking live animals in the sea for several weeks for "buffering" the market or waiting for events.

#### Cons

1.

--- SENTENCE 1003 ---
Spiny lobsters in American Samoa inhabit narrow cracks in shallow (often turbulent) reef areas and are extremely difficult to catch by hand

--- SENTENCE 1004 ---
But this technique is used in many places in the Pacific (see Prescott, 1980b)

2.

--- SENTENCE 1005 ---
A law prohibiting the spearing of lobsters would be unpopular in the fishing community and could encourage resentment of all fishery regulations.

--- SENTENCE 1006 ---
Needs public discussion

#### **B** Selective Harvest (No berried females)

Already implemented in American Samoa (for spiny lobsters only)

#### **Pros**

1.

--- SENTENCE 1007 ---
The protection of berried females will increase spawning in our waters and should increase recruitment of juveniles on our local reefs or the reefs of neighboring islands.

--- SENTENCE 1008 ---
Already implemented in American Samoa for spiny lobsters only

#### Cons

- 1.

--- SENTENCE 1009 ---
For this regulation to be effective, the use of spears would also have to be prohibited.

--- SENTENCE 1010 ---
This may unreasonably limit fishing success.
- 2.

--- SENTENCE 1011 ---
The long planktonic larval stage of spiny lobsters could mean that lobsters hatched here will drift away to other islands before they are ready to settle.

--- SENTENCE 1012 ---
Protecting berried females on Tutuila may not noticeably increase the numbers of larvae settling here.

--- SENTENCE 1013 ---
However, it is not as clear for slipper lobsters with smaller geographic distributions, and long planktonic development but unknown larval behavior (Coutures, 2000a).

--- SENTENCE 1014 ---
Females seem "berried" several times a year.

--- SENTENCE 1015 ---
This regulation strongly limits the catches.
- 4.

--- SENTENCE 1016 ---
Enforcement staff must acquire skills to separate easily and quickly males and females with several features, in order to be able to recognize animals from which eggs have been removed from the abdomen (see Appendix I).

#### B. Marine Sanctuaries

#### Pros

1.

--- SENTENCE 1017 ---
A "no harvest" area on Tutuila would insure that an undisturbed group of spiny lobsters could add to the fishery via spawning and/or migration out of the sanctuary areas.

--- SENTENCE 1018 ---
A marine sanctuary already exists in Fagatele Bay, Tutuila, A.S, and in National Park of American Samoa (NPSA).

--- SENTENCE 1019 ---
#### Cons

- 1.

--- SENTENCE 1020 ---
It would be difficult to agree upon where a marine sanctuary could be established.

--- SENTENCE 1021 ---
Most villages would not like a marine sanctuary within their jurisdiction meaning that remote areas are the most likely candidates.
- 2.

--- SENTENCE 1022 ---
Sanctuaries in remote areas would be very difficult to patrol making enforcement ineffective.

--- SENTENCE 1023 ---
Rough sea conditions during our Trade Wind Season can create "natural sanctuaries" on the south shore of all islands.

--- SENTENCE 1024 ---
This predictable phenomenon may preclude the need for additional sanctuaries.

#### C. Minimum Size

(already implemented for spiny lobsters, CL = 3 1/8 in - 7.94 cm)

#### Pros

- 1.

--- SENTENCE 1025 ---
An appropriate minimum size on spiny lobsters would allow lobsters to reach sexual maturity and spawn before they were first subject to harvest.

--- SENTENCE 1026 ---
A "good" minimum size increases the Maximum Sustainable Yield.

--- SENTENCE 1027 ---
The immediate effect of this type of regulation is to increase the numbers of lobsters visible on the reefs.

--- SENTENCE 1028 ---
This provides the public with visual evidence of the success of regulations and fosters a positive attitude toward regulations in general.
- 3.

--- SENTENCE 1029 ---
It has been established that fishermen can train their eye to determine a particular size fish or shellfish with a high degree of accuracy.

#### Cons

- 1.

--- SENTENCE 1030 ---
A minimum size law would have to be enforced at the landing site and in retail outlets.

--- SENTENCE 1031 ---
This type of regulation would only be 100% effective if spears were eliminated from the fishery.

#### D. Closed Season

#### **Pros**

- 1.

--- SENTENCE 1032 ---
A closed season on all harvest would insure that lobsters can spawn and grow unmolested during that time.

--- SENTENCE 1033 ---
Our local lobster stocks appear to spawn all year allowing flexibility in the time of the closure.

--- SENTENCE 1034 ---
The closed season could coincide with a period of traditionally low harvest pressure.

#### Cons

1.

--- SENTENCE 1035 ---
Any total harvest closure may unfairly impact a subsistence economy and struggling commercial/retail industry.

--- SENTENCE 1036 ---

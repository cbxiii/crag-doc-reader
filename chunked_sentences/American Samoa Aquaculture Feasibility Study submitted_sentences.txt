#### **Background and Purpose**

The American Samoa Coast Zone Management Program (ASCZMP) of the Department of Commerce (DOC) funded a feasibility study of aquaculture opportunities for American Samoa in recognition of the economic potential of this activity.

--- SENTENCE 5 ---
American Samoa has a long history of pilot aquaculture efforts which sets the stage for future development, as well as close ties to Samoa where aquaculture development has flourished.

--- SENTENCE 6 ---
The Pacific Islands in general have many forms of aquaculture that provide a basis for transfer to American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 7 ---
Thus, potential to establish diverse types of aquaculture is clearly possible.

--- SENTENCE 8 ---
Among the most favorable factors that make aquaculture an appealing option are: 1) good freshwater resources; 2) some protected bays; 3) diverse species with potential for aquaculture; 4) need to reduce reliance on imported protein sources; 4) educated population and presence of technical specialists; and 5) availability of fish meal.

--- SENTENCE 9 ---
Despite the known potential, there are obstacles to aquaculture development that must be overcome before progress can be made.

#### **Findings and recommendations**

American Samoa is similar to the rest of the world in terms of aquaculture development since the potential far exceeds the realization of this potential.

--- SENTENCE 174 ---
Generally aquaculture development in any location is limited by common constraints and unless these are addressed, aquaculture development will be stymied.

--- SENTENCE 175 ---
However, current conditions are favorable to start aquaculture development in several forms, but small-scale farming and aquaculture for conservation or environmental purposes are the most likely types to be successful at this time.

--- SENTENCE 176 ---
There are three basic forms in which American Samoa can pursue aquaculture:

--- SENTENCE 177 ---
*Small-scale farming by individuals or community-based groups*-pilot projects and small-scale farming are feasible at this time assuming that the responsible agencies and the private sector can work together to provide support and resources.

--- SENTENCE 178 ---
*Large-scale commercial farming*-this is far less feasible under current conditions since infrastructure, services, policy framework and investment are not immediately available.

--- SENTENCE 179 ---
However, small-scale efforts can help set the stage for larger efforts in the near future.

--- SENTENCE 180 ---
*Aquaculture for conservation and environmental purposes*-some forms of this may be feasible and could go hand-in-hand with small-scale farming efforts.

--- SENTENCE 181 ---
For example, using bivalves for water quality purposes or coral culture for restoration could be coupled with small scale farming at the community level.

--- SENTENCE 182 ---
In order for aquaculture pilot projects or small-scale efforts to be successful, the following would be needed:

- Species that can be cultured with the least amount of technical capacity, fewest and least expensive inputs, and which provide benefits to the stakeholders in a timely fashion.

--- SENTENCE 183 ---
Initially it is best to avoid forms of aquaculture where capital and operational costs are high and where external sources of goods are required as these factors increase the likelihood of failure.

--- SENTENCE 184 ---
It is also rare for communities to have the patience to engage in efforts where benefits take a long time to accrue, or where the benefits are not concomitant with the effort or time required.

--- SENTENCE 185 ---
Stakeholders also compare how the benefits from aquaculture compare to other opportunities they have with other activities.
- Dedicated technical assistance for a minimum of three to five years for each project is required.

--- SENTENCE 186 ---
Generally longer-term support is needed.

--- SENTENCE 187 ---
This assistance is best delivered by local extension agents that have sufficient resources and expertise to render technical support.

--- SENTENCE 188 ---
Local extension agents can be supported by non-resident specialists as needed.

--- SENTENCE 189 ---
For example, there are researchers and extension specialists who work with all of the

- species mentioned in this report in the Pacific Region, and their expertise can be tapped by local specialists.
-

--- SENTENCE 190 ---
It is important to conduct an economic analysis of costs and benefits prior to choosing which type of aquaculture to support and at what scale.

--- SENTENCE 191 ---
The market must also be considered.

--- SENTENCE 192 ---
- Willingness of the stakeholders (e.g. villagers) to "own" the initial pilot efforts and to take responsibility for the execution of the work is also extremely important.
-

--- SENTENCE 193 ---
The government must be willing to provide three to five years of technical support as well as some of the initial financing, although contributions from stakeholders (e.g. part of the financing, labor) should be required in order to avoid over dependence on government financial support.
- Long-term capacity needs to be built through educational and training opportunities so reliance on external specialists is reduced.
-

--- SENTENCE 194 ---
Since aquaculture is a multi-disciplinary and multi-sectoral activity that ranges from natural resource management to financing and marketing topics, it is necessary for multiple agencies to coordinate closely to support new efforts.

#### **Assessment of Species, Culture Systems and Sites**

American Samoa is fortunate in having a large number of marine, estuarine and freshwater species with potential for aquaculture.

--- SENTENCE 213 ---
Most of these species, as well as their culture systems, have been developed elsewhere in the Pacific Region, so re-inventing the wheel is not necessary.

--- SENTENCE 214 ---
Lessons learned from other areas can be readily transferred.

--- SENTENCE 215 ---
A network of technical and extension specialists also exists in the region and these specialists can be tapped to support local efforts.

--- SENTENCE 216 ---
American Samoa also has diverse sites where one or more species can be cultured.

--- SENTENCE 217 ---
However, it is important to carefully match species and their culture systems with appropriate sites.

--- SENTENCE 218 ---
Poor site selection is a major cause of failure for aquaculture.

--- SENTENCE 219 ---
It is also important to work closely with private sector or community members who are responsible for new projects so that role and responsibilities of each stakeholder group are clearly understood and carried out.

--- SENTENCE 220 ---
The stakeholders the team spoke with consistently mentioned the possibility of theft as a major reason that aquaculture could fail.

--- SENTENCE 221 ---
It was also noted that there are few small boats due to the tsunami.

--- SENTENCE 222 ---
Most open-water sites would require the use of small boats, although they do not necessarily need to be motorized.

--- SENTENCE 223 ---
In the calmer bays, some sites could be accessed on foot.

--- SENTENCE 224 ---
The DOC has a project to build an improved model of an alia.

--- SENTENCE 225 ---
These boats could also be used for aquaculture.

--- SENTENCE 226 ---
The following is a discussion of the species, culture systems and sites that have the highest degree of feasibility under current conditions in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 227 ---
Much of this analysis is based on prioritizing species that can be cultured without investing in hatcheries, or which would require minimal investment for infrastructure.

--- SENTENCE 228 ---
The reason for this criterion is that for most species, hatchery production requires the ability to culture large volumes of microalgae, which is an expensive and technically exacting activity.

--- SENTENCE 229 ---
Moreover, even simple hatcheries are expensive to build and maintain, and must be staffed with highly trained personnel.

--- SENTENCE 230 ---
This is not to say that hatcheries should not be included in longer term development goals, only that this must be planned carefully and stable funding needs to be assured.

--- SENTENCE 231 ---
There is a long history of regional hatcheries failing due to inconsistent technical and financial support.

--- SENTENCE 232 ---
Additionally, if hatcheries are to be built, capacity building and education must go along with this to ensure that there are qualified staff available.

--- SENTENCE 233 ---
The simplest forms of aquaculture can proceed in the short-term without the expense and complications of building a hatchery.

--- SENTENCE 234 ---
#### *Tilapia*

The term "tilapia" refers to a wide range of cichlid fish species in the genera *Oreochromis*, *Sarotherodon* and *Tilapia*.

--- SENTENCE 235 ---
Each species, as well as hybrids between species, have unique

characteristics that must be evaluated in the context of the local situation.

--- SENTENCE 236 ---
There is great variation in their feeding and reproductive habits, as well as salinity tolerance.

--- SENTENCE 237 ---
Tilapia have become one of the most common freshwater species for aquaculture on a global basis due to their rapid growth, tolerance of extreme environmental conditions and ease of reproduction.

--- SENTENCE 238 ---
Some tilapia species can benefit from fertilizing the pond which produces an algal bloom and zooplankton which the fish can eat.

--- SENTENCE 239 ---
This can greatly reduce the amount of feed required.

--- SENTENCE 240 ---
Despite being one of the easiest fish to culture, caution is indicated because there is a requirement for some degree of technical expertise

American Samoa imports a large amount of frozen fish which could possibly be replaced by local tilapia production.

--- SENTENCE 241 ---
Non-fillet frozen fish represented 14.8% of \$123M AS imports in 2017.

--- SENTENCE 242 ---
Tagarino (2019) reports that wholesalers sell approximately 225,600 pounds of tilapia per year, representing an increase of 58,600 pounds per year since 2004.

--- SENTENCE 243 ---
The retail price is \$1.86 per pound, a \$0.48 per pound increase since 2004.

--- SENTENCE 244 ---
This makes tilapia more expensive than chicken.

--- SENTENCE 245 ---
If farmers use the locally-produced fish feed made by ASCC, tilapia farming can be profitable.

--- SENTENCE 246 ---
Some farmers may obtain higher prices through direct sales.

--- SENTENCE 247 ---
Many locations in American Samoa are classified as "food deserts" (Asifoa-Lagai 2012) where limited access to locally-produced, healthy foods leads to an over dependence on imported, nutrition-poor foods.

--- SENTENCE 248 ---
More tilapia production in the villages, for example by school or women's groups, could help provide locally-sourced protein.

--- SENTENCE 249 ---
Fish culture is also an excellent educational activity for school children.

--- SENTENCE 250 ---
The Sea Grant and Land Grant programs at the ASCC have a long-standing effort to develop and promote tilapia culture.

--- SENTENCE 251 ---
This included developing the capability to manufacture local feeds on a small-scale.

--- SENTENCE 252 ---
This feed is produced through ASCC procuring the fish meal and the farmer provides the starch and oil.

--- SENTENCE 253 ---
ASCC has the equipment for small-scale feed manufacture.

--- SENTENCE 254 ---
Kelley Tagarino and Francis Leito were helpful in providing background information on their work.

--- SENTENCE 255 ---
They have data showing that the combination of reducing feed costs along with increased retail prices are at the point where tilapia culture can be economically feasible.

--- SENTENCE 256 ---
Given the initial success of this work, tilapia culture is ready for expansion and could become a significant source of food and income.

--- SENTENCE 257 ---
Additionally, even small-scale production can be important as a local food sources.

--- SENTENCE 258 ---
Many countries have school-based food production programs including fish culture, which can provide a steady source of food for school children.

--- SENTENCE 259 ---
Tilapia culture could be another way to take advantage of the freshwater resources of American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 260 ---
Particularly in the case of villages which surround wetlands, freshwater could be extracted to support tilapia culture.

--- SENTENCE 261 ---
This is best done by diverting a limited amount of water from the upland areas to supply ponds built outside of the wetlands, rather than siting ponds in the wetlands.

--- SENTENCE 262 ---
Wetland areas are generally poor sites for ponds due to flooding and because ponds

built in low-lying areas cannot be drained.

--- SENTENCE 263 ---
The latter is particularly important for tilapia farming as these species tend to breed prolifically in ponds and exceed the carrying capacity.

--- SENTENCE 264 ---
The ponds must be periodically drained and dried to get rid of the overabundance of small fish.

--- SENTENCE 265 ---
In any case, if tilapia farming is expanded, it may be important to calculate a water budget for the farming area to avoid creating competition for freshwater between residents and fish farmers.

--- SENTENCE 266 ---
Based on the information provided by the ASCC staff and observations made by the DOC and consultant teams, the following actions are needed to support expanded tilapia culture:

- A steady supply of tilapia fingerlings with good genetics can increase growth rates and potentially the appearance and marketability of the product if red strains of tilapia are used.

--- SENTENCE 267 ---
- Production efficiency can be increased if all male strains, sex reversal or hand sexing can be implemented.

--- SENTENCE 268 ---
Male tilapia grow faster than females.

--- SENTENCE 269 ---
Reducing or eliminating the number of females also limits over population of the ponds by thousands of small tilapia which leads to slower growth and stunting.
- Extension personnel are essential to help farmers become self-sufficient.

--- SENTENCE 270 ---
While the Land Grant and Sea Grant programs at the ASCC currently provide technical assistance, more support for them would be helpful and could help kick start larger-scale fish farming.
-

--- SENTENCE 271 ---
The local fish feed production has served as an important proof of concept that fish feed can be locally produced.

--- SENTENCE 272 ---
This program requires financial support and could be expanded.

--- SENTENCE 273 ---
A steady supply of a carbohydrate source is needed since using local sources is preferable to purchasing flour (although the latter clearly works).
- Proper siting and construction of ponds is important.

--- SENTENCE 274 ---
Poor site selection and pond design can make it difficult or impossible to manage a pond effectively.

--- SENTENCE 275 ---
There is a tendency to build ponds in existing depressions or low-lying areas since this reduces the labor requirement.

--- SENTENCE 276 ---
However, tilapia culture depends on the ability to drain and dry ponds to eliminate the over-production of small tilapia.

--- SENTENCE 277 ---
Once ponds are dried, they should be cleaned and lime is often added to adjust the soil pH.

--- SENTENCE 278 ---
This increases primary production once the pond is filled again.

--- SENTENCE 279 ---
Primary production in ponds serves as an additional food sources for tilapia and reduce the amount of feed needed.

--- SENTENCE 280 ---
A survey of suitable sites near villages that have interest in tilapia farming could identify adequate locations, including areas near, but not in wetlands, where an adequate water supply could be sourced.

--- SENTENCE 281 ---
- Involving groups such as children and elders that may need supplement protein sources should be considered.

--- SENTENCE 282 ---
School ponds can be a good source of food as well as a way to teach basic science.

#### **Seaweed (***limu***)**

A review of the literature finds that three species are consumed in American Samoa: *Caulerpa racemosa*; *Caulerpa* spp.

--- SENTENCE 283 ---
The Samoan name for *Caulerpa* (Seagrapes in English) is reported as *limu fuafua* and *H. durvillei* may be *limu mumu* (Skelton 2003).

--- SENTENCE 284 ---
It appears from local interviews that consumption of seaweed has been reduced or forgotten in recent years and among the youth.

--- SENTENCE 285 ---
Seaweeds are a relatively easy/low cost species group to culture, provide high levels of nutrition and feeding is not required.

--- SENTENCE 286 ---
There may be another 20 or so species which could potentially be consumed.

--- SENTENCE 287 ---
For example, *Grateloupia filicina*, *Codium mamillosum* and *Asparagopsis taxiformis* are present.

--- SENTENCE 288 ---
These species were traditionally consumed by Hawaiians and continued to be prized although collection from the wild is increasingly difficult as many coastal areas in Hawaii have seen the seaweed populations decline.

--- SENTENCE 289 ---
(*limu kohu* in Hawaiian) is the most highly valued seaweed in Hawaii with the price being around \$20 per pound.

--- SENTENCE 290 ---
This species has not yet been successfully produced in commercial aquaculture but *G. filicina* and several *Codium* species can be reared through vegetative propagation.

--- SENTENCE 291 ---
During the visit to Aunu`u, the residents reported harvesting a green and a brown seaweed.

--- SENTENCE 292 ---
The green seaweed is most likely one of the *Caulerpa* species.

--- SENTENCE 293 ---
The "brown" seaweed was not identified, but residents said that it was cooked, then eaten with coconut cream.

--- SENTENCE 294 ---
None of the three Samoan specialists involved in the interview had heard of this latter species nor the practice of cooking a seaweed.

--- SENTENCE 295 ---
The "brown" seaweed may be *H. durvillei*, which is in the Division Rhodophyta (red seaweeds).

--- SENTENCE 296 ---
The villagers said that both species appear seasonally on the exposed, southern part of the island.

--- SENTENCE 297 ---
The prices for the green seaweed was reported to be \$10 for a small bundle.

--- SENTENCE 298 ---
Residents of Masefau also reported that seaweed used to be gathered and consumed but said that this was no longer done.

--- SENTENCE 299 ---
Seaweed culture could have potential for American Samoa as it generally requires little technology and can provide both healthy food and income.

--- SENTENCE 300 ---
Methods have been developed for *Caulerpa* and experiments are being conducted in the Philippines with *H. durvillei* (Figure 6).

--- SENTENCE 301 ---
The latter is not usually cultured for food, but rather for as a source of the red pigment rphycoerythrin and other nutraceuticals such as a lambda-carrageenan-like polysaccharide (Santiañez et al 2016).

--- SENTENCE 302 ---
Both substances have high value, but confirmation with buyers would be needed before deciding to produce this species for its secondary compounds.

--- SENTENCE 303 ---
Researchers at UHH are conducting research on culture methods for *Caulerpa*, and the results can be shared with interested parties.

Two non-native species are reported as having been introduced for culture for the carrageenan industry, *Eucheuma* spp.

--- SENTENCE 307 ---
and *Kappaphycus* spp., but populations of these species are not reported as having persisted.

--- SENTENCE 308 ---
These species are among the most commonly cultured in the world, but reintroduction is not recommended as they have the potential to become invasive and global prices are low.

--- SENTENCE 309 ---
Small-scale seaweed production is common in countries such as the Philippines, Indonesia, Fiji and Tanzania but may be less feasible in the other Pacific Islands as in most cases, seaweed prices are low, and residents may have more lucrative opportunities.

--- SENTENCE 310 ---
Given that the price of this international commodity is so low, if American Samoa stakeholders decide that seaweed culture may be an option, only the higher priced species that can be sold for food or exported should be considered.

--- SENTENCE 311 ---
Additionally, caution should be taken to avoid importing seaweeds as there are many areas with seaweed diseases.

--- SENTENCE 312 ---
Climate change impacts such as increased sea surface temperatures may also impact seaweed culture.

--- SENTENCE 313 ---
This happened in the case of Tanzania where seaweed began to suffer from the "ice-ice" disease which bleached the seaweeds.

--- SENTENCE 314 ---
It then became important to choose sites where the water was cooler to reduce the incidence of "ice-ice" disease (Haws, pers obs.).

--- SENTENCE 315 ---
Thus site selection is important when considering seaweed culture.

#### **Giant Clams**

Giant clams (Tridacnids) have a long history in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 316 ---
It is believed that the native species are *Tridacna maxima*, *T. squamosa* and *Hippopus hippopus*.

--- SENTENCE 317 ---
The first two have been persistently present, although the latter is believed to have been re-introduced to aquaculture after local stocks were depleted.

--- SENTENCE 318 ---
Other species such as *T. gigas* and *T. derasa* were also introduced for aquaculture.

--- SENTENCE 319 ---
Around 1986, DMWR personnel visited Palau to learn spawning methods and *T. derasa* was imported for the first time.

--- SENTENCE 320 ---
The introduced *T. derasa* clams were around 4 inches in length and were housed at the Waikiki Aquarium during the transhipment.

--- SENTENCE 321 ---
Initially the clams were kept in the borrow pit area near Coconut Point, Nu`uuli.

--- SENTENCE 322 ---
They were later moved to Alofau.

--- SENTENCE 323 ---
There were problems with poaching and the remaining clams were put at Taema Bank at about 65 feet, but these were also stolen (David Itano, pers.

--- SENTENCE 324 ---
In 1989, a second project was started with funds from the Center for Tropical and Subtropical Aquaculture (CTSA) for *T. derasa*, followed by a third in 1991 for *H. hippopus*.

--- SENTENCE 325 ---
*T. gigas* was also introduced at this time.

--- SENTENCE 326 ---
A small hatchery was built and several full-time personnel were hired over the years in which the various projects were executed.

--- SENTENCE 327 ---
Trials to test growth rates and survival with the various species were conducted at Nu`uuli, Ofu and Alafau (Bell 1993).

--- SENTENCE 328 ---
The latter report details eleven introductions of different species from 1986 to 1991.

--- SENTENCE 329 ---
The DMWR operated a giant clam hatchery in the 1990's near the airport for several years and at least three farms operated for some time.

--- SENTENCE 330 ---
In 2004, an effort was made to build a hatchery at Alao with Chief Ava Hunkin, although this facility did not come fully into operation (Figure 7).

--- SENTENCE 331 ---
One of the main issues with this site was the difficulty of installing a seawater intake line due to the wave action on that area of coast.

--- SENTENCE 332 ---
Mr. Simon Ellis, Director of the Marine and Environmental Research Institute of Pohnpei (MERIP) was the lead trainer for this effort and could serve as a resource person in the future.

--- SENTENCE 333 ---
At that time, there were also plans to begin culturing soft corals at the small hatchery but this did not take place as the hatchery effort was discontinued.

Costs would no doubt be higher using current values but increasing the scale of the farming could also lower it.

--- SENTENCE 345 ---
There were only a few thousand clams being grown at that time.

--- SENTENCE 346 ---

Although giant clam farming has been demonstrated to be biologically feasible, many countries have had their giant clam trials halted by problems with theft, including the past efforts in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 348 ---
Unless mechanisms are in place to prevent this, it is unlikely that giant clam farming will be feasible.

--- SENTENCE 349 ---
It would also be important to determine the market potential for clams as ornamental specimens and the prices for the various product (e.g. shells, meat).

--- SENTENCE 350 ---
Giant clams can also help with tourism development.

--- SENTENCE 351 ---
Giant clam "circles" (large clams aggregated in shallow areas) have been popular tourist attractions in Kosrae and Palau in the past.

#### **Other bivalves (oysters, clams, mussels)**

Bivalves other than giant clams may be possible culture candidates if they can be obtained through spat collection, or from other hatcheries.

--- SENTENCE 370 ---
Production of bivalve larvae and spat requires a hatchery with the capability to produce large amounts of several species of microalgae culture, which requires technical expertise and incurs high costs.

--- SENTENCE 371 ---
A bivalve hatchery would require several years to develop and significant investment.

--- SENTENCE 372 ---
Dr. John Glude first assessed the potential for shellfish aquaculture in 1971.

--- SENTENCE 373 ---
He observed that there were two clam species that supported a small fishery, *Gafrarium tumidum* and *Lioconcha lorenziana*

--- SENTENCE 374 ---
(Glude 1972; Uwate et al. 1984).

--- SENTENCE 375 ---
These species were not observed during this study.

--- SENTENCE 376 ---

--- SENTENCE 377 ---
He recommended following up on the trials.

--- SENTENCE 378 ---
This recommendation is still valid.

--- SENTENCE 379 ---
*Saccostrea* oyster species are cultured in several areas, including in Australia.

--- SENTENCE 380 ---
Aside from the clam species mentioned in Glude's report, there are other bivalves which may be a possibility for aquaculture.

The tuagane clam seems to be relative abundant in some lagoons.

--- SENTENCE 390 ---

The Hawaii shellfish hatcheries (including the PACRC at UHH) sell certified disease-free spat, so introduction of diseases is not a high risk.

--- SENTENCE 394 ---
These can be purchased from the Hawaii hatcheries.

--- SENTENCE 413 ---
One critical aspect of shellfish production is sanitation.

--- SENTENCE 414 ---
Filter-feeding bivalves accumulate bacteria and viruses from the water and thus can be unsafe to eat.

--- SENTENCE 415 ---
Shellfish sanitation involves regular water testing in the growing area.

--- SENTENCE 416 ---
This would need to be done at each growout site to evaluate the levels of total and fecal coliform bacteria.

--- SENTENCE 417 ---
These bacteria are the most commonly used indicators for shellfish sanitation.

--- SENTENCE 418 ---
For bivalves such as giant clams where only the muscle is consumed, routine water testing may not be necessary.

--- SENTENCE 419 ---
However, it would need to be determined whether U.S. regulations related to shellfish sanitation are applicable if the shellfish are intended only for local consumption and not for export.

--- SENTENCE 420 ---
The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and individual state agencies are responsible for shellfish sanitation in the U.S.

--- SENTENCE 421 ---
Even if FDA shellfish sanitation regulations are not required in American Samoa, analogous regulations and monitoring would still be needed to avoid the possibility of shellfish-borne diseases which can be deadly.

--- SENTENCE 422 ---
If it is decided that spat collection is an option to pursue, the spat could be collected in nearly any site (e.g. Nu`uuli Pala Lagoon) even if the water quality is poor, then moved to a cleaner site for growout.

--- SENTENCE 423 ---
Generally spat need to be transferred to cleaner waters before they reach a size of 20 mm.

--- SENTENCE 424 ---
The only exception may be if heavy metals were present in the collection area and were detectable in the spat.

--- SENTENCE 425 ---
In this case, it would not be possible to use these spat.

--- SENTENCE 426 ---
Filter-feeding bivalves can also be use for water quality mitigation purposes, but this would require that people are prevented from stealing or eating them.

--- SENTENCE 427 ---
Pearl culture may be another option, but a hatchery would most likely be needed.

--- SENTENCE 428 ---
Mr. Peter Williams, a well-known pearl farmer from Manihiki, Cook Islands, conducted surveys in Samoa and American Samoa in the mid-2000's (Williams pers.

--- SENTENCE 429 ---
He was only able to find a few scattered specimens which makes spat collection unlikely.

--- SENTENCE 430 ---
#### *Coral and live rock*

Corals may be cultured for the marine ornamental trade or for conservation purposes such as restoration or to create artificial reefs for coastal protection.

--- SENTENCE 431 ---
There are several coral farms in the USAPI, which include MERIP, Marshall Islands Mariculture Farm (MIMF) and Aquaculture Kosrae.

--- SENTENCE 432 ---
These farms culture a variety of marine ornamentals such as giant clams, corals and fish in order to be able to offer a variety of species to buyers which prefer to buy as much as possible from one supplier.

--- SENTENCE 433 ---
During the snorkel survey at Masefau, several species of brightly colored corals were observed that may be potential culture candidates (Figure 12).

--- SENTENCE 434 ---
Aside from bright coloration, the species has to be easy to fragment and grow, look attractive under aquarium lights and be able to survive transportation.

--- SENTENCE 435 ---
The candidate species should also be unique and not one that is readily available from other regions.

--- SENTENCE 436 ---
Soft corals can also be grown and are in demand for aquaria.

!

--- SENTENCE 437 ---

--- SENTENCE 438 ---
Other factors such as the appearance under aquarium lights, ability to survive shipping and price would need to be evaluated for each species.

--- SENTENCE 439 ---
One company which buys only cultured products is Ocean, Reefs and Aquarium (ORA, orafarm.com).

--- SENTENCE 440 ---
This company has a facility in the Marshall Islands and buys products from other areas such as Pohnpei and Kosrae.

--- SENTENCE 441 ---
Some examples of hard and soft corals sold by ORA or produced at MERIP are shown in Figure 13.

--- SENTENCE 442 ---
MERIP provides technical support to about 20 small-scale farmers in Pohnpei to produce hard and soft corals.

--- SENTENCE 443 ---
MERIP also plays a key role by handling the sales, shipping and permitting tasks.

--- SENTENCE 444 ---
A similar model of partnership between smallscale private farmers and a non-profit agency could be developed in American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 445 ---
It is important to work directly with buyers from the beginning of a project as they have precise specifications for the various types of coral and how they are cultured and shipped.

--- SENTENCE 452 ---
Coral culture is generally done through breaking off small pieces from wild coral (fragmentation), attaching the fragment to a substrate where it grows into a small coral specimen.

--- SENTENCE 453 ---
These are then shipped to buyers who redistribute to retail outlets.

--- SENTENCE 454 ---
Although this sounds simple, establishing coral culture as a viable enterprise is not just a question of being able to grow the coral, it is critical to be able to communicate and coordinate closely with the buyers.

--- SENTENCE 455 ---
Also, most buyers prefer to buy multiple species from a single seller, which is one of the factors of success for the farms in Micronesia since most of them offer giant clams, corals, fish and other ornamentals.

--- SENTENCE 456 ---
Live rock is a product used in seawater aquaria and is in high demand since most seawater aquaria require many pounds of it.

--- SENTENCE 457 ---
It is used in aquaria as a biological filter, to introduce new organisms and for decoration.

--- SENTENCE 458 ---
Live rock is produced by putting suitable substrate "base rock" in the aquatic environment and allowing it to be colonized naturally by marine organisms such as coralline algae, coral and small animals.

--- SENTENCE 459 ---
The substrate can be natural or artificial, but usually attempts are made to use a light weight substrate to reduce shipping costs.

--- SENTENCE 460 ---
Old, bleached coral can be used as well as mixtures of cement and other materials.

--- SENTENCE 461 ---
As long as the substrate is placed in areas where it will not impact the reef, this can be a highly sustainable activity.

--- SENTENCE 462 ---
Care must also be taken that the live rock harvested is actually cultured, and that live coral is not being harvested under the guise of producing live rock.

--- SENTENCE 463 ---
To start live rock production, a pilot would be needed to test different sites to determine in which area the colonization of the base rock results in the most attractive assortment of organisms.

#### *Mangrove crab*

Mud crab culture is a topic which invokes a high degree of interest due to the high demand and prices wherever crab species are found.

--- SENTENCE 464 ---
This form of aquaculture is practiced in locations in South East Asia and Africa.

--- SENTENCE 465 ---
Some of the technology has been transferred to Palau by Dr. Miguel delos Santos who is from the Philippines.

--- SENTENCE 466 ---
The most common form of mud crab culture is done by capturing juvenile crabs in wetland areas, then growing them either in net pens in the wetland, or in land-based tanks.

--- SENTENCE 467 ---
Either system requires that large amounts of fish be used to feed the crabs, which can be problematic since the fish creates waste and water quality problems in the wetland areas.

--- SENTENCE 468 ---
Also, feeding fish to crabs may compete with the use of fish for human consumption.

--- SENTENCE 469 ---
Net pen structures built in a wetland area may also cause impacts.

--- SENTENCE 470 ---
Given these issues, promoting mud crab culture is not recommended.

--- SENTENCE 471 ---
Instead, managing the crab catch in a sustainable fashion is a more desirable route, as well as protecting the wetland area in which crabs breed and grow.

#### *Marine Fish*

Production of marine fish generally requires a sophisticated hatchery and highly trained personnel.

--- SENTENCE 472 ---
Even with hatchery infrastructure in place, it may take 2-3 years before a marine fish hatchery is functional due to the need to obtain and condition fish broodstock.

--- SENTENCE 473 ---
In the case of marine fish species that have not been cultured before, research may also be necessary to work out the required hatchery protocols.

--- SENTENCE 474 ---
Until such a hatchery exists in American Samoa, there are other options for culturing marine or brackish water fish: 1) work with hatcheries in other areas to buy fish fingerlings for stocking; or 2) use capture-based methods to obtain stock for local species.

--- SENTENCE 475 ---
Hawaii would seem to be a natural place to obtain hatchery produced fish, yet the State is now suffering from a lack of fish fingerlings.

--- SENTENCE 476 ---
The Oceanic Institute (OI) of Hawaii Pacific University has the capability to produce several species of marine fish (moi, mullet, coral grouper) but any effort with them would have to be fully funded as they usually do not sell fingerlings.

--- SENTENCE 477 ---
OI works with researchers in Palau to research methods for producing coral grouper and potentially a similar collaborative arrangement could be made with an American Samoa agency.

--- SENTENCE 478 ---
The PACRC has a new marine fish hatchery, but fingerlings may not be available until mid-2020.

--- SENTENCE 479 ---
Either option will be expensive given shipping costs.

--- SENTENCE 480 ---
Also, caution is indicated in bringing in fish from other regions, even if the species in question is found locally, since there is risk of diseases.

--- SENTENCE 481 ---
Unless genetic studies have been done which show that the fish from the hatchery locale is genetically similar to the population in American Samoa, there is also a risk of impacting local fish genetics.

--- SENTENCE 482 ---
A simpler and much less expensive option is to stock aquaculture operations using juvenile fish that are captured locally.

--- SENTENCE 483 ---
There are several methods for doing this (Figure 14).

--- SENTENCE 484 ---
Juvenile fish can be collected from nursery areas, e.g. wetlands.

--- SENTENCE 485 ---
Mullet may be a good candidate for this.

--- SENTENCE 486 ---
Another method involves capturing fish as the larval fish begin to settle out on reef or sea grass areas.

--- SENTENCE 487 ---
This method is currently being tested in Pohnpei, FSM, by Simon Ellis.

--- SENTENCE 488 ---
Mr. Ellis and his team at the Marine and Environmental Research of Pohnpei (MERIP) have developed methods using specially designed nets to capture rabbitfish larvae as they settle out into seagrass areas.

--- SENTENCE 489 ---
The fish are then kept in tanks until they are large enough to transfer to floating net pens in a lagoon.

--- SENTENCE 490 ---
This method can be environmentally friendly since only a very small percentage of the fish population are captured.

--- SENTENCE 491 ---
Generally it is habitat availability which limits fish recruitment, not the number of fry that settle in reef areas.

--- SENTENCE 492 ---
Most juvenile fish will die from predation before becoming mature.

--- SENTENCE 493 ---
It may be possible to develop similar methods for species in American Samoa, although this would require research to determine in which locations and seasons the various fish species can be caught.

--- SENTENCE 494 ---
Specialists from the DMWR may need to conduct studies to determine how many fingerlings could be removed without affecting the fisheries stock.

--- SENTENCE 495 ---
Care must also be taken not to disturb the habitat in which the capture takes place.

--- SENTENCE 496 ---
It is also necessary to determine where the grow out phase of fish farming could take place.

--- SENTENCE 497 ---
American Samoa offers some complications due to the high energy nature of its coast line.

--- SENTENCE 498 ---
Although net pen engineering has advanced to the point where cages can be located the open ocean, these operations are costly and complex to manage.

--- SENTENCE 499 ---
Smaller net pens may be feasible in some of the calmer, more protected areas such as Masefau, Aoa or Afono.

--- SENTENCE 500 ---
Even some of the Pago Pago harbor may be useable for net pens, although water quality would need to be assessed first.

--- SENTENCE 501 ---
Land-based grow out facilities may also be a future possibility, but again, these are expensive to build and manage.

#### **Education and Extension Capacity**

American Samoa is fortunate in having numerous specialists in fisheries management, marine science and related fields at institutions such as the American Samoa Community College, DMWR, and U.S. agencies such as NOAA.

--- SENTENCE 505 ---
For example, Kelley Tagarino and Francis Leito, along with their predecessors, were able to start a small-scale tilapia farming industry and manufacture fish feed using local ingredients.

--- SENTENCE 506 ---
One deficit, however, is the lack of dedicated aquaculture extension agents with experience culturing multiple species and with resources to train and assist aquaculture stakeholders.

--- SENTENCE 507 ---
In order to advance with either pilot projects or further assessment, at least one highly qualified aquaculture extension agent would need to be hired and placed at one of the agencies.

--- SENTENCE 508 ---
It would also be desirable to begin building future capacity by providing resources to the ASCC for undergraduate education in aquaculture and possibly through scholarships to four-year and graduate educational institutions in the region.

--- SENTENCE 509 ---
Incentives to attract recent graduates back to American Samoa may also need to be provided.

--- SENTENCE 510 ---
Currently trained aquaculture specialists are in high demand in Hawaii and other regions, and salaries tend to be relatively high even for new graduates if they have hands-on experience.

--- SENTENCE 511 ---
Another model that has worked in other countries a working group model where a multiinstitutional group made up of local and external specialists work collaboratively to develop and possibly implement small-scale projects as well as to work on other crucial aspects of aquaculture such as policy development and financing.

#### **Wetlands and aquaculture**

The consultants were asked to consider the possibility of using wetlands to support aquaculture development.

--- SENTENCE 512 ---
American Samoa is fortunate in having extensive wetland areas ranging from mangroves to freshwater stream habitats.

--- SENTENCE 513 ---
These areas provide ecological services including serving as nursery, breeding and roosting sites for many aquatic and terrestrial species.

--- SENTENCE 514 ---
They also serve as sites for traditional agriculture and help protect the coast lines.

--- SENTENCE 515 ---
Many wetlands have been permanently altered or impacted including the largest wetland area, the Nu`uuli Pala Lagoon (Volk 1993).

--- SENTENCE 516 ---
It is vitally important to protect wetlands of all types so they can continue to serve these purposes.

--- SENTENCE 517 ---
When wetlands are lost, fisheries decline and the coastline is more vulnerable to storms.

--- SENTENCE 518 ---
Wetlands generally do not make good sites in which to practice aquaculture.

--- SENTENCE 519 ---
For example, if ponds are built in wetland areas, they are usually difficult to drain which makes it hard to harvest and manage to pond.

--- SENTENCE 520 ---
Net pens or other enclosures can be installed in wetland areas, but feeding fish or crabs that may be held in these can create problems with water pollution.

--- SENTENCE 521 ---
The best way to utilized wetlands to benefit aquaculture and fisheries is to protect them.

--- SENTENCE 522 ---
They can however, be used as sites to collect limited numbers of fish or bivalves for aquaculture.

--- SENTENCE 523 ---
Wetlands also play a valuable role in retaining freshwater.

--- SENTENCE 524 ---
Some water can be extracted from some types of wetlands to support ponds built outside the wetland, but care must be taken to not divert so much water that the function of the wetland is affected.

### **Summary and next steps**

American Samoa is well-positioned to begin planning and development of small-scale pilot projects for a selected number of species identified during this study.

--- SENTENCE 525 ---
The most feasible opportunities for small-scale aquaculture development are, by species: tilapia, giant clams, bivalves that can be obtained through spat collection, seaweed and corals.

--- SENTENCE 526 ---
Marine fish that can be collected from the wild as juveniles or fingerlings as a source for fish farms could also be considered.

--- SENTENCE 527 ---
Other options may be possible in the future, but these would require investment in infrastructure such as hatcheries, as well as having a team of highly qualified technical personnel.

--- SENTENCE 528 ---
One tremendous advantage for American Samoa is the availability of fish meal for making aquatic feeds.

--- SENTENCE 529 ---
Each of the options presented in the report will require further study and discovery of key facts, but it may be possible to do this fairly quickly.

--- SENTENCE 530 ---
For example, given the abundance of experience with giant clams in American Samoa and elsewhere in the Pacific, the biological feasibility is not in question, but issues such as the logistics for importing or collecting broodstock clams, permits, identifying the best sites, how to work with communities and how to prevent poaching would need more definition.

--- SENTENCE 531 ---
Other important steps can also be taken.

--- SENTENCE 532 ---
For example, any aquaculture development activity needs oversight and technical support.

--- SENTENCE 533 ---
This could be done in several ways.

--- SENTENCE 534 ---
Specialists from different agencies could team up in working groups to develop and support pilot projects.

--- SENTENCE 535 ---
Or, funding could be sought to hire a dedicated extension agent to oversee and guide development of one or a few pilot projects, although this person would need support from various agencies.

--- SENTENCE 536 ---
This may require inter-agency agreements that clearly outline roles and responsibilities for each participating entity.

--- SENTENCE 537 ---
Each option also requires careful study of market opportunities as well as an analysis of the costs.

--- SENTENCE 538 ---
Opportunities to build capacity for aquaculture can be increased through support to ASCC which is has marine science and aquaculture educational opportunities.

--- SENTENCE 539 ---
The question is how to support students to obtain advanced degrees, gain hands-on experience and then return to American

Samoa to work in the field.

--- SENTENCE 540 ---
Scholarships and internships could be beneficial in allowing more students to obtain relevant degrees, but jobs need to be available to them after graduation.

--- SENTENCE 541 ---
Community and private sector involvement are also important.

--- SENTENCE 542 ---
Projects which are entirely executed by government agencies often do not survive.

--- SENTENCE 543 ---
The ideal situation is one in which the government works hand-in-hand with private sector or community partners, with the end goal of the non-governmental partner eventually taking full control over the work.

--- SENTENCE 544 ---
Profitability must also be a goal as grants or subsidies are most effective as seed money, but eventually the activity must pay for itself.

--- SENTENCE 545 ---
In conclusion, the possibilities of re-starting aquaculture development in American Samoa are good, particularly if private and public sector partners can work together to provide support and overcome challenges together.

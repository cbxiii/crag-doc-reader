### **Introduction**

The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA)

--- SENTENCE 5 ---
National Ocean Service (NOS) initiated a coral reef research program in 1999 to map, assess, inventory, and monitor U.S. coral reef ecosystems (Monaco et al. 2001).

--- SENTENCE 6 ---
These activities were implemented in response to requirements outlined in the Mapping Implementation Plan developed by the Mapping and Information Synthesis Working Group (MISWG) of the Coral Reef Task Force (CRTF) (MISWG 1999).

--- SENTENCE 7 ---
As part of the MISWG of the CRTF, NOS' Biogeography Team has been charged with the development and implementation of a plan to produce comprehensive digital coral-reef ecosystem maps for all U.S. States, Territories, and Commonwealths within five to seven years.

--- SENTENCE 8 ---
Joint activities between Federal agencies are particularly important to map, research, monitor, manage, and restore coral reef ecosystems.

--- SENTENCE 9 ---
In response to the Executive Order 13089 and the Coral Reef Conservation Act of 2000, NOS is conducting research to digitally map biotic resources and coordinate a long-term monitoring program that can detect and predict change in U.S. coral reefs, and their associated habitats and biological communities.

--- SENTENCE 10 ---
Most U.S. coral reef resources have not been digitally mapped at a scale or resolution sufficient for assessment, monitoring, and/or research to support resource management.

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
Thus, a large portion of NOS' coral reef research activities has focused on mapping of U.S. coral reef ecosystems.

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
The map products will provide the fundamental spatial organizing framework to implement and integrate research programs and provide the capability to effectively communicate information and results to coral reef ecosystem managers.

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
Although the NOS coral program is relatively young, it has had tremendous success in advancing towards the goal to protect, conserve, and enhance the health of U.S. coral reef ecosystems.

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
One objective of the program was to create benthic habitat maps to support coral reef research to enable development of products that support management needs and questions.

--- SENTENCE 15 ---
Therefore this product was developed in collaboration with many U.S. Pacific Territory partners.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
An initial step in producing benthic habitat maps was the development of a habitat classification scheme.

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
The purpose of this document is to outline the benthic habitat classification scheme and protocols used to map American Samoa, Guam and the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands.

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
Thirty-two distinct benthic habitat types (i.e., four major and 14 detailed geomorphological structure classes; eight major and 18 detailed biological cover types) within eleven zones were mapped directly into a geographic information system (GIS) using visual interpretation of orthorectified IKONOS satellite imagery.

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
Benthic features were mapped that covered an area of 263 km2 .

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
In all, 281 km2 of unconsolidated sediment, 122 km2 of submerged vegetation, and 82.3 km2 of coral reef and colonized hardbottom were mapped.

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
This CD-ROM will provide the user a suite of information including access to the primary and derived data sets, detailed information on how the mapping was conducted and data was collected, custom tools used to create the product, and reports summarizing the results:

- ~ Access to primary data sources (i.e., IKONOS imagery, accuracy assessment field data, ground validation field data, spatial accuracy field data)
- ~ Access to derived data sources (i.e., benthic habitat GIS files, final reports, shoreline GIS files)
- ~ Directions for using the "habitat digitizer" extension in ArcView
- ~ A description of the specific methods used to create the habitat maps
- ~ An assessment of the thematic accuracy of the maps

To see or download more information about NOAA's Coral Reef Mapping Program, visit NOAA's Biogeography Program: http://biogeo.nos.noaa.gov/projects/mapping/pacific/.

### **Chapter 1: Classification Scheme for Benthic Habitats**

## Chapter 1

#### Developing the Habitat Classification Scheme

A hierarchical classification scheme was created to define and delineate shallow-water benthic habitats.

--- SENTENCE 22 ---
The classification scheme was influenced by many factors including: requests from the management community, NOS's coral reef mapping experience in the Florida Keys and Caribbean, existing classification schemes for the Pacific and Hawaiian Islands (Holthus and Maragos 1995; Gulko 1998; Allee et al. 2000), other coral reef systems (Kruer 1995; Reid and Kruer 1998; Lindeman et al. 1998; Sheppard et al. 1998; Vierros 1997; Chauvaud et al. 1998; Mumby et al. 1998; Kendall et al. 2001), quantitative habitat data for the U.S. Pacific Territories, the minimum mapping unit (MMU - 1 acre for visual imagery interpretation), and analysis of the spatial and spectral limitations of satellite IKONOS imagery.

--- SENTENCE 23 ---
The hierarchical scheme allows users to expand or collapse the thematic detail of the resulting map to suit their needs.

--- SENTENCE 24 ---
This is an important aspect of the scheme as it will provide a "common language" to compare and contrast digital maps developed from complementary remote sensing platforms.

--- SENTENCE 25 ---
Furthermore, it is encouraged that additional hierarchical categories be added in the resulting geographic information system by users with more detailed knowledge or data for specific areas.

--- SENTENCE 26 ---
For example, habitat polygons smaller than the MMU can be delineated, such as reef holes found in parts of the U.S. Pacific Territories, or habitat polygons delineated as colonized pavement using this scheme could be further attributed with health information (i.e., bleached, percent live cover) or species composition (i.e., Porites, Montipora).

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
The hierarchical scheme was prepared through consultation, meetings, and workshops that included key coral reef biologists, mapping experts, and professionals throughout the pacific territories.

--- SENTENCE 28 ---
Modifications were made throughout the development process based upon feedback provided by workshop participants and other contributors.

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
Additional modifications were made during the mapping process to ensure that each category definition reflected the intended habitats and zones encountered in the field as accurately as possible.

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
For instance, the separation of biological cover and geomorphological structure in the present scheme represents a significant evolution of previous versions of the classification schemes developed for mapping of the Caribbean and Hawaiian Islands.

#### General Description of the Classification Scheme

The classification scheme defines benthic habitats on the basis of three attributes: large geographic "zones" which are comprised of smaller geomorphological structure and biological cover of the reef system.

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
Every polygon on the benthic community map will be assigned a structure and cover within a zone (i.e., uncolonized sand in the lagoon, or coral on aggregate reef on the bank).

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
Biological cover and geomorphological structure are further defined by three density classes.

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
Zone indicates polygon location, biological cover indicates the predominate biological component colonizing the surface of the feature, and geomorphological structure indicates the physical structural composition of the feature.

--- SENTENCE 34 ---
The description of each cover and structure includes an example image.

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
The zone descriptions include schematic descriptions.

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
The hierarchical scheme was prepared through consultation, meetings, and workshops that included key coral reef biologists, mapping experts, and professionals throughout the island territories.

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
The separation of biological cover and geomorphological structure in the present scheme represents a significant evolution of previous versions of the classification schemes developed for mapping of the Caribbean and Hawaiian Islands.

*Description of Geomorophological Structure Types**

Fourteen distinct and non-overlapping geomorphological structure types were identified that could be mapped by visual interpretation of the IKONOS imagery.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
Habitats or features that cover areas smaller than the MMU were not considered.

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
For example, sand halos surrounding patch reefs are too small to be mapped independently.

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
Structure refers only to predominate physical structural composition of the feature and does not address location (e.g., on the shelf or in the lagoon).

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
The structure types are defined in a collapsible hierarchy ranging from four major classes (coral reef and hardbottom, unconsolidated sediment, other delineations, and unknown), to thirteen detailed classes (sand, mud, spur and groove, individual and aggregated patch reef, aggregate reef, scattered coral/rock in unconsolidated sediment, pavement, rock/boulder (volcanic and carbonate), reef rubble, pavement with sand channels, artificial, and unknown).

### **Unconsolidated Sediment:**

**Sand:** Coarse sediment typically found in areas exposed to currents or wave energy.

!

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
Fine sediment often associated with river discharge and build-up of organic material in areas sheltered from high-energy waves and currents.

--- SENTENCE 97 ---
### **Coral Reef and Hardbottom:**

**Spur and Groove:** Habitat having alternating sand and coral formations that are oriented perpendicular to the shore or bank/shelf escarpment.

--- SENTENCE 98 ---
The coral formations (spurs) of this feature typically have a high vertical relief relative to pavement with sand channels (see below) and are separated from each other by 1-5 meters of sand or hardbottom (grooves), although the height and width of these elements may vary considerably.

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
This habitat type typically occurs in the fore reef or bank/shelf escarpment zone.

!

--- SENTENCE 100 ---
### **Chapter 1: Description of Structure Types**

**Patch Reefs:**

--- SENTENCE 101 ---
Coral formations that are isolated from other coral reef formations by sand, seagrass, or other habitats and that have no organized structural axis relative to the contours of the shore or shelf edge.

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
Distinctive single patch reefs that are larger than or equal to the MMU.

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
Clustered patch reefs that individually are too small (less than the MMU) or are too close together to map separately.

!

--- SENTENCE 104 ---
[](_page_9_Picture_5.jpeg)

**Aggregate Reef:** High relief lacking sand channels of spur and groove.

!

--- SENTENCE 105 ---
Flat, low-relief, solid carbonate rock with coverage of macroalgae, hard coral, zoanthids, and other sessile invertebrates that are dense enough to begin to obscure the underlying surface.

--- SENTENCE 106 ---
**Rock/Boulder:** Solid carbonate blocks and/or boulders or volcanic rock.

!

--- SENTENCE 107 ---
Dead, unstable coral rubble often colonized with filamentous or other macroalgae.

--- SENTENCE 108 ---
This habitat often occurs landward of well developed reef formations in the reef crest or back reef zone.

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
**Pavement with Sand Channels:** Habitats of pavement with alternating sand/surge channel formations that are oriented perpendicular to the shore or bank/shelf escarpment.

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
The sand/surge channels of this feature have low vertical relief relative to spur and groove formations and are typically erosional in origin.

--- SENTENCE 111 ---
This habitat type occurs in areas exposed to moderate wave surge such as the bank/shelf zone.

### **Chapter 1: Decription of Zone Types**

### **Description of Zones**

Thirteen mutually exclusive zones were identified from land to open water corresponding to typical insular shelf and coral reef geomorphology (Fig. 1-3).

--- SENTENCE 116 ---
These zones include: shoreline intertidal, vertical wall, lagoon, back reef, reef flat, reef crest, fore reef, bank/shelf, bank/shelf escarpment, channel, dredged (since this condition eliminates natural geomorphology), unknown, and land.

--- SENTENCE 117 ---
Zone refers only to each benthic community's location and does not address substrate or cover types within.

--- SENTENCE 118 ---
For example, the lagoon zone may include patch reefs, sand, and seagrass beds; however, these are considered structural elements that may or may not occur within the lagoon zone and therefore, are not used to define it.

!

--- SENTENCE 119 ---
Reef separated from the shore by a relatively wide, deep lagoon.

!

--- SENTENCE 120 ---
Reef platform is continuous with the shore.

!

--- SENTENCE 121 ---
Reef surrounding a lagoon.

--- SENTENCE 122 ---
**Shoreline Intertidal:** Area between the mean high water line (or landward edge of emergent vegetation when present) and lowest spring tide level (excluding emergent segments of barrier reefs).

--- SENTENCE 123 ---
Typically, this zone is narrow due to the small tidal range in the U.S. Pacific Territories.

--- SENTENCE 124 ---
**Typical Habitats:** Mangrove, hao, sand, seagrass, rock/boulder (volcanic and carbonate)

**Vertical Wall:** Area with near-vertical slope from shore to shelf or shelf escarpment.

--- SENTENCE 125 ---
This zone is typically narrow and may not be distinguishable in remotely sensed imagery, but is included because it is recognized as a biologically important feature.

--- SENTENCE 126 ---
**Typical Habitats:** rock/boulder, algae, and coral

**Lagoon:** Shallow area (relative to the deeper water of the bank/shelf) between the shoreline intertidal zone and the back reef of a reef or a barrier island.

--- SENTENCE 127 ---
This zone is protected from the high-energy waves commonly experienced on the bank/shelf and reef crest.

--- SENTENCE 128 ---
If no reef crest is present there is no lagoon zone.

--- SENTENCE 129 ---
**Typical Habitats:** sand, seagrass, algae, pavement, rock/boulder (volcanic and carbonate), patch reefs

**Back Reef:** Area between the seaward edge of a lagoon floor and the landward edge of a reef crest.

--- SENTENCE 130 ---
This zone is present when a reef crest and lagoon exist.

--- SENTENCE 131 ---
**Typical Habitats:** sand, reef rubble, seagrass, algae, patch reef

**Reef Flat:** Shallow (semi-exposed) area between the shoreline intertidal zone and the reef crest of a fringing reef.

--- SENTENCE 132 ---
This zone is protected from the high-energy waves commonly experienced on the shelf and reef crest.

--- SENTENCE 133 ---
Reef flat is typically not present if there is a lagoon zone.

--- SENTENCE 134 ---
**Typical Habitats:** sand, reef rubble, seagrass, algae, patch reef

**Reef Crest:**

--- SENTENCE 135 ---
The flattened, emergent (especially during low tides) or nearly emergent segment of a reef.

--- SENTENCE 136 ---
This zone lies between the back reef and fore reef zones.

--- SENTENCE 137 ---
Breaking waves will often be visible in aerial images at the seaward edge of this zone.

--- SENTENCE 138 ---
**Typical Habitats:** reef rubble, aggregated coral

**Fore Reef:** Area from the seaward edge of the reef crest that slopes into deeper water to the landward edge of the bank/shelf platform.

--- SENTENCE 139 ---
Features not forming an emergent reef crest but still having a seaward-facing slope that is significantly greater than the slope of the bank/shelf are also designated as fore reef.

--- SENTENCE 140 ---
**Typical Habitats:** spur and groove

### **Chapter 1: Decription of Zone Types**

**Bank/Shelf:**

--- SENTENCE 141 ---
Deep water area (relative to the shallow water in a lagoon) extending offshore from the seaward edge of the fore reef to the beginning of the escarpment where the insular shelf drops off into deep, oceanic water.

--- SENTENCE 142 ---
The bank/shelf is the flattened platform between the fore reef and deep open ocean waters or between the shoreline/intertidal zone and open ocean if no reef crest is present.

--- SENTENCE 143 ---
**Typical Habitats:** sand, patch reefs, algae, seagrass, pavement, pavement with sand channels, other coral reef habitats

**Bank/Shelf Escarpment:**

--- SENTENCE 144 ---
The edge of the bank/shelf where depth increases rapidly into deep, oceanic water.

--- SENTENCE 145 ---
This zone begins at approximately 20 to 30 meters depth, near the depth limit of features visible in aerial images.

--- SENTENCE 146 ---
This zone extends well into depths exceeding those that can be seen on aerial photographs and is intended to capture the transition from the shelf to deep waters of the open ocean.

--- SENTENCE 147 ---
**Typical Habitats:** sand, \_spur and groove

**Channel:**

--- SENTENCE 148 ---
Naturally occurring channels that often cut across several other zones.

--- SENTENCE 149 ---
**Typical Habitats:** sand, mud, pavement

**Dredged:** Area in which natural geomorphology is disrupted or altered by excavation or dredging.

--- SENTENCE 150 ---
**Typical Habitats:** sand, mud

**Unknown:** Zone, Cover, and Structure uninterpretable due to turbidity, cloud cover, water depth, or other interference.

--- SENTENCE 151 ---
Terrestrial features above the spring high tide line.

# Chapter 2

#### **Introduction**

The Habitat Digitizer Extension to ArcView 3.x® was developed to facilitate mapping benthic habitats of the U.S. Pacific Territories using the classification scheme described in Classification Methods.

--- SENTENCE 153 ---
The extension was originally created to map habitats using this scheme by visually interpreting orthorectified IKONOS imagery.

--- SENTENCE 154 ---
The extension's capabilities have been expanded to allow users to map from other georeferenced image data such as satellite images and side scan sonar.

--- SENTENCE 155 ---
The extension allows users to rapidly delineate and attribute polygons using simple menus.
- 3.

--- SENTENCE 156 ---
It also allows new hierarchical classification schemes to be easily created, modified, and saved for use on future mapping projects.

!

--- SENTENCE 157 ---
The extension is available on the *Shallow-Water Benthic Habitats of American Samoa, Guam, and the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands* CD-ROM or over the Internet at http://biogeo.nos.noaa.gov/products/apps/digitizer/.

--- SENTENCE 158 ---
The extension and accessory files are found in "data/ext/hab\_dig.zip".

This folder contains several files including:

#### **Hardware and Software Requirements**

The habitat digitizer extension is compatible with ArcView 3.x® and requires hardware similar to that recommended for proper operation of ArcView.

--- SENTENCE 161 ---
Additional memory may enhance performance for handling large image files.

--- SENTENCE 162 ---
The appropriate Image Support extension (IMG, etc.) is required depending on the format of the image files used.

--- SENTENCE 163 ---
The Image Analyst extension is not necessary, but is recommended to facilitate manipulation of image brightness, contrast, and color balance.

--- SENTENCE 164 ---
### **Getting Started**

To begin using Habitat Digitizer, save the habitat.avx file in either ArcView's Ext32 directory or the USEREXT directory.

--- SENTENCE 165 ---
The coral.hcs and coral.avl files can be saved anywhere, but preferably, they should be placed in the working directory of the project.

--- SENTENCE 166 ---
After starting ArcView, load the Habitat Digitizer Extension (and any other desired extensions) by selecting "File/Extensions" and click on the check box next to the Habitat Digitizer Extension in the "Available Extensions" list.

--- SENTENCE 167 ---
Click OK to install the extension.

--- SENTENCE 168 ---
If a project already exists that used the Habitat Digitizer Extension, opening the project will automatically load the extension.

--- SENTENCE 169 ---
#### **Setting the Projection Parameters for the Image Data**

The Habitat Digitizer allows users to specify a Minimum Mapping Unit (MMU), digitizing scale, and offers several other spatial functions that require the View's projection and Map Units to be set properly.

--- SENTENCE 170 ---

# Chapter 3

#### **Introduction**

Satellite imagery is a valuable tool for natural resource managers and researchers since they provide an excellent record of the location and extent of habitats.

--- SENTENCE 327 ---
Benthic habitat maps of American Samoa, Guam, and the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands were created through visual interpretation of multispectral IKONOS imagery using the NOAA Habitat Digitizer extension.

--- SENTENCE 328 ---
Habitat boundaries were delineated around signatures (e.g., areas with specific color and texture patterns) in the orthorectified imagery mosaic corresponding to habitat types in the Classification Scheme.

--- SENTENCE 329 ---
The custom Habitat Digitizer extension was used, digitizing at a scale of 1:6,000 with a 1-acre minimum mapping unit.

--- SENTENCE 330 ---
Generally, feature detection of seafloor habitats was possible from the shoreline to water depths of approximately 30 meters, depending on water clarity.

--- SENTENCE 331 ---
In order to optimize the satellite imagery for visual interpretation, a number of processing steps were implemented to enhance the geopositioning and clarity of the imagery.

--- SENTENCE 332 ---
These steps include: orthorectification to remove spatial distortions in the imagery due to relief displacement; pansharpening; deglinting; and generating normalized reflectance values.

### **Satellite Imagery**

The IKONOS satellite, owned and operated by Space Imaging Incorporated, LLC, provides commercially available panchromatic (black and white) and multispectral (blue/green/red/near-infrared) imagery.

--- SENTENCE 333 ---
The panchromatic imagery has a 1 m pixel dimension (meaning features larger then 1 m2 can be detected in the imagery).

--- SENTENCE 334 ---
The multispectral imagery has a 4 m pixel dimension (meaning features larger then 16 m2 can be seen in the imagery).

--- SENTENCE 335 ---
The IKONOS imagery is purchased in 11 km wide swaths that are mosaicked together to produce complete images of locales.

--- SENTENCE 336 ---
IKONOS satellite imagery provides precise and robust data with spectral and spatial resolution suitable for shallow water benthic mapping.

--- SENTENCE 337 ---
Furthermore, satellite imagery provides efficient and effective global coverage for repeated imaging of remote islands that are often obscured by cloud cover.

!

--- SENTENCE 338 ---
[](_page_23_Picture_6.jpeg)

All of the IKONOS imagery was purchased in National Imagery Transmission Format (NITF) with the associated Rational Polynomial Coefficients (RPCs or satellite ephemeris data).

--- SENTENCE 339 ---
When using image analysis software capable of reading NITF files with associated RPCs, the initial horizontal positioning error never exceeded 15 m (for locations where there is little or no vertical relief to affect image pixel displacement).

#### **Orthorectification**

During orthorectification, digital imagery is subjected to algorithms that eliminate each source of spatial distortion.

--- SENTENCE 340 ---
The result is a georeferenced digital mosaic of several imagery scenes with uniform scale throughout the mosaic.

--- SENTENCE 341 ---
After an orthorectified mosaic is created, visual interpreters can accurately and reliably delineate the boundaries of features in the imagery as they appear on the computer monitor using a software interface such as the Habitat Digitizer.

--- SENTENCE 342 ---
Through this process, natural resources managers and researchers are provided with spatially accurate maps of habitats and other features visible in the imagery.

--- SENTENCE 343 ---
### **Mosaicking the Imagery**

Georeferencing/mosaicking of the imagery was performed using PCI OrthoEngine, and Erdas OrthoBase.

--- SENTENCE 344 ---
The NITF IKONOS imagery was orthorectified using the Rational Functions extracted from the NITF, then further supplemented with ground control and corrected for terrain displacement using the DEM's where available.

--- SENTENCE 345 ---
When multiple scenes were available for a given island, these were collectively incorporated into the orthomosaic project through bundle adjustment.

--- SENTENCE 346 ---
Each scene was exported as a separate orthorectified file for further image processing.

--- SENTENCE 347 ---
In addition, the best segments of each scene were selected for creation of the final mosaic.

--- SENTENCE 348 ---
Segments of each scene were selected to minimize sun glint, cloud interference, turbidity, etc. in the final mosaic.

--- SENTENCE 349 ---
Where possible, parts of images obscured by sun glint or clouds were replaced with cloud/glint free parts of overlapping images.

--- SENTENCE 350 ---
As a result, most mosaics have few or no clouds or sun glint obscuring bottom features.

--- SENTENCE 351 ---
However, in some cases, clouds, sun glint, or turbid areas could not be replaced with overlapping imagery.

--- SENTENCE 352 ---
In these areas, such obstructions were minimized but could not be eliminated completely.

#### **Ground Control Points (GCPs) for Georeferencing**

Fixed ground features visible in the IKONOS imagery were selected for ground control points (GCPs) which were then used to georeference the imagery (i.e., link the image pixels to a real world coordinate system such as Universal Transverse Mercator).

--- SENTENCE 353 ---
NOAA's National Geodetic Survey (NGS) gathered ground control data (horizontally accurate to within 5 cm of its location on the earth) on seventeen of the twenty-one islands mapped in the U.S. Pacific Territories (see Figure).

--- SENTENCE 354 ---
GCPs were measured and then differentially corrected to the closest Continuously Operating Reference System (CORS).

--- SENTENCE 355 ---
We obtained points with a wide distribution throughout the imagery whenever possible since this results in the most accurate registration throughout each image.

--- SENTENCE 356 ---
Only ground control points for terrestrial features were collected due to the difficulty of obtaining precise positions for submerged features.

!

--- SENTENCE 357 ---
#### **Image to Image Tie-Points**

Image to image tie-points (distinct features visible in overlap areas of each frame such as street intersections, piers, coral heads, reef edges, and bridges) were then used to further co-register the imagery, especially for photos taken over open water where ground control points were not available (see Figure).

--- SENTENCE 358 ---
Softcopy photogrammetry software has the ability to automatically find such features common to overlapping imagery but this automated function has mixed results for submerged features.

--- SENTENCE 359 ---
Georeferencing/mosaicking of the TIFFs was performed using PCI OrthoEngine, and Erdas OrthoBase.

--- SENTENCE 360 ---
The NITF IKONOS imagery was orthorectified using the Rational Functions extracted from the NITF, then further supplemented with ground control and corrected for terrain displacement using the DEM's where available.

--- SENTENCE 361 ---
When multiple scenes were available for a given island, these were collectively incorporated into the orthomosaic project.

--- SENTENCE 362 ---
Each scene was exported as a separate orthorectified file for further image processing.

--- SENTENCE 363 ---
In addition, the best segments of each scene were selected for creation of the final mosaic.

--- SENTENCE 364 ---
Segments of each scene were selected to minimize sun glint, cloud interference, turbidity, etc. in the final mosaic.

--- SENTENCE 365 ---
Where possible, parts of images obscured by sun glint or clouds were replaced with cloud/glint free parts of

overlapping images.

--- SENTENCE 366 ---
As a result, most mosaics have few or no clouds or sun glint obscuring bottom features.

--- SENTENCE 367 ---
However, in some cases, clouds, sun glint, or turbid areas could not be replaced with overlapping imagery.

--- SENTENCE 368 ---
In these areas, such obstructions were minimized but could not be eliminated completely.

#### **Digital Elevations Models (DEM)**

Pre-existing U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) 10 m2 resolution digital elevation models (elevation contour intervals 1 m) were available for eight of the nineteen islands and used to correct for relief displacement (see Figure).

--- SENTENCE 369 ---
Once a draft orthorectified mosaic was produced, a set of independent ground control points (i.e., check points) were used to measure the quality of each mosaic's rectification and ensure that it required horizontal and vertical spatial accuracy limits.

--- SENTENCE 370 ---
If the spatial accuracy was not acceptable based on this comparison, additional modifications were made to the GCPs, tie-points, etc., until a satisfactory mosaic was created for each island.

--- SENTENCE 371 ---
In general, mosaics were georeferenced such that pixels are positioned within one pixel width of their correct location.

#### **Image Analysis**

Several intermediate, derived products were produced as the satellite imagery was processed for use in producing the benthic habitat maps.

--- SENTENCE 373 ---
First, the raw satellite images were converted from Digital Numbers (DNs) to normalized reflectance.

--- SENTENCE 374 ---
Normalized reflectance (or at-satellite reflectance) converts DNs into standardized, satellite-independent, comparable values.

--- SENTENCE 375 ---
First developed for Landsat satellite imagery, the algorithm used to perform this conversion was modified for IKONOS image processing.

--- SENTENCE 376 ---
As part of the conversion from DNs to at-satellite reflectance, the following equation is used (Green et al. 2000):

R = pi \* L/

--- SENTENCE 377 ---
(Eo cos(theta0) 1/r2 )

--- SENTENCE 378 ---
L = radiance (from calibration provided by Space Imaging).

--- SENTENCE 379 ---
r = earth-sun distance in Astronomical Units.

--- SENTENCE 380 ---
= the solar zenith angle

 Eo = the mean solar exo-atmospheric irradiance in each band.

--- SENTENCE 381 ---
(A convolution of the spectral response and solar radiation from Neckel and Labs (1984) was used to get Eo.)

--- SENTENCE 382 ---
The acquisition angles (ephemeris data) of the satellite relative to the ground at the time of image acquisition were also used.

--- SENTENCE 383 ---
Calibration coefficients for the satellite, provided by Space Imaging, were used to calculate at-satellite radiance, which was then transformed to reflectance.

--- SENTENCE 384 ---
The normalized reflectance imagery was then transformed into water reflectance (or the signal < 10 cm above the water surface).

--- SENTENCE 385 ---
Water reflectance uses the near-infrared band to remove radiance attributed to atmospheric and surface effects (Stumpf et al. 2003).

--- SENTENCE 386 ---
Water reflectance estimates how the signal (photons) received by the satellite is diminished as it passes through the atmosphere on the way down to the water-atmosphere boundary and on the way back up to the satellite after the signal leaves the water-atmosphere boundary.

--- SENTENCE 387 ---
Water reflectance also estimates how the signal at the satellite is diminished by water vapor, clouds, specular effects at the water surface (wave surface glint), and other signal- absorbing and diffusing materials.

--- SENTENCE 388 ---

#### **Determining the Optimum Digitizing Scale**

Results of an experiment conducted during benthic habitat mapping of the Caribbean were used to determine the optimum digitizing scale to maximize accuracy and minimize map production time.

--- SENTENCE 400 ---
In the Caribbean digitizing experiment a 25 acre area composed of a variety of habitat types was mapped at 1:1500, 1:3000, 1:6000, and 1:12,000 on-screen scale (scale that the image appears on the computer monitor).

--- SENTENCE 401 ---
Five replicates were conducted at each scale.

--- SENTENCE 402 ---
Each trial was timed so we could evaluate the influence of mapping scale on production time.

--- SENTENCE 403 ---
Resulting maps were evaluated for deviations in polygon detail relative to the map digitized at 1:1500 scale.

--- SENTENCE 404 ---
At 1:1500, individual pixels are clearly discernible allowing highly detailed and accurate maps to be created by closely following the contours of even the most convoluted habitat boundary.

--- SENTENCE 405 ---
Additional increases in zoom do not result in an increase in map detail and accuracy since individual pixels are already visible at 1:1500.

--- SENTENCE 406 ---
Therefore, the map created at 1:1500 scale was used as a reference against which to compare maps digitized at scales of 1:3000, 1:6000, and 1:12,000.

--- SENTENCE 407 ---
The results of this experiment indicated that there is no appreciable loss in polygon detail and accuracy by digitizing at 1:6000 while mapping time was dramatically reduced.

--- SENTENCE 408 ---
Therefore all polygons were digitized at this scale except when subtle habitat boundaries were not easily discernible at 1:6000 and zooming out to a more broad scale was required to place

boundaries correctly.

--- SENTENCE 409 ---
In this case, digitizing generally took place at a scale of approximately 1:10,000.

# Chapter 4

#### **Introduction**

The quality of the habitat information derived from remotely sensed data is determined by the quantitative process of accuracy assessment.

--- SENTENCE 438 ---
The purpose of accuracy assessment is to identify and quantify errors in the maps by comparing the attributes of the map versus reference data at various sites.

--- SENTENCE 439 ---
It is important that the mapmaker know how reliably a given habitat can be classified.

--- SENTENCE 440 ---
This parameter is called "producers accuracy".

--- SENTENCE 441 ---
The users of a map product want to know what percentage of the polygons of a particular class are correctly attributed.

--- SENTENCE 442 ---
This parameter is called "users accuracy".

--- SENTENCE 443 ---
Furthermore, remotely sensed imagery that may be suitable for mapping coral reef habitats can be acquired from a wide variety of platforms and imaging systems, each having it's own strengths and limitations.

--- SENTENCE 444 ---
It is important to identify the technical merits of each, one measure of which is the thematic accuracy of the map products.

--- SENTENCE 445 ---
To determine the overall accuracy of the mapped product, GIS data prepared by visually interpreting IKONOS satellite imagery was assessed for accuracy using conventional methodologies.

--- SENTENCE 446 ---
It was proposed that specific areas being mapped be used as test areas for this work.

--- SENTENCE 447 ---
A statistically robust data set composed of random field habitat observations were collected within the test areas to assess the accuracy of the mapped product.

--- SENTENCE 448 ---
These areas were chosen based on input from the local marine biologists and coral reef managers.

--- SENTENCE 449 ---
These groups provided advice on the location of the most diverse benthic communities and also areas of particular importance based on management strategies and marine protected areas.

--- SENTENCE 450 ---
Thus, it was the goal of this team to collect accuracy assessment field data representing as many of the habitats that occur in these regions as possible.

--- SENTENCE 451 ---
The thematic accuracy of all mapped products was determined at the most general and detailed levels of the classification scheme including both the biological cover type and geomorphological structure.

--- SENTENCE 452 ---
Sixteen coral reef test areas were selected based on the diversity of the habitat types and to assure that all benthic habitats throughout the U.S. Pacific Territories were represented.

--- SENTENCE 453 ---
The accuracy of all maps is, therefore, considered a conservative representation of the thematic accuracy of the habitat maps prepared using the same methods for imagery collected throughout the remainder of the U.S. Pacific Territories.

### **Evaluating Thematic Accuracy**

#### **Thematic Accuracy in American Samoa, Guam, and the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands**

An accuracy assessment system was designed and executed to quantify the thematic accuracy of the maps generated at all levels of the classification scheme.

--- SENTENCE 454 ---
Statistical analysis methods have been applied that have been developed by other researchers (Hudson and Ramm 1987, Congalton 1991, Rosenfield et al. 1982).

--- SENTENCE 455 ---
In this work, 20 to 30 field habitat observations were completed per detailed structure as well as detailed biological cover type (see Figure).

--- SENTENCE 456 ---
The accuracy assessment is prepared from a matrix that compares the attribute assigned to a polygon that was generated from the interpretation of the image with that of the determination from field observation.

#### **Results of Overall Accuracy Assessment of Benthic Habitat Map Products**

Thematic accuracy of the benthic habitat maps was determined using the aforementioned methods.

--- SENTENCE 496 ---
The mapped habitat type was compared with that of the actual habitat type from field observation.

--- SENTENCE 497 ---
The data is organized into columns representing the field habitat assessment and the rows organized into mapped habitat type.

--- SENTENCE 498 ---
The correct class for each of the incorrect attributes was recorded and included in a comprehensive matrix at the most detailed level of the classification scheme.

--- SENTENCE 499 ---
Four of these detailed matrices were generated, one each for biological cover and geomorphological structure in American Samoa and the Marianas Archipelago.

--- SENTENCE 500 ---
Error matrices were prepared at the detailed and general levels to identify patterns of confusion in the interpretation of the signatures in the imagery.

--- SENTENCE 501 ---
This information was incorporated into ongoing work to improve the accuracy of mapped product.

--- SENTENCE 502 ---
A complete description of these results can be found in the final project report, Project Completion Report: Mapping of Benthic Habitats for U.S. Pacific Territories, at http://biogeo.nos.noaa.

--- SENTENCE 503 ---
Traditionally, the data is organized into columns that represent the field habitat validation data and the rows are organized into the interpretation of the images.

--- SENTENCE 504 ---
The overall accuracy is typically measured by dividing the total correct determinations by the total number of assessments.

--- SENTENCE 505 ---
This result only incorporates the major diagonal of the table and excludes the omission and commission errors whereas the Tau analysis indirectly incorporates the off-diagonal elements as a product of the row and column marginals.

--- SENTENCE 506 ---

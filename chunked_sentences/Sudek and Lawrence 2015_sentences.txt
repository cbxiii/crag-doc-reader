# 1. Program Overview

In 2005 the Department of Marine and Wildlife Resources (DMWR) initiated a comprehensive long-term monitoring program for the reefs in American Samoa, the American Samoa Coral Reef Monitoring Program (ASCRMP).

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
Funding was provided through the Coral Reef Conservation Program (CRCP) of the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) and the National Ocean Services (NOS).

--- SENTENCE 15 ---
Although there have been previous monitoring surveys in American Samoa covering a variety of spatial and temporal scales, the ASCRMP represents the first attempt to annually monitor representative reefs in American Samoa, starting initially around Tutuila and eventually in the Manu'a islands and the two atolls, Rose and Swains.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
The program has been set up with long-term government funding, and is therefore capable of accumulating long-term data sets annually over a larger geographic scale.

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
The primary objectives of the American Samoa Coral Reef Monitoring Program are to:

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
(1) Monitor the status and trends in the distribution and abundance of reef biota on reefs in American Samoa, and (2) Provide environmental managers, as well as other decision makers, with information that is pertinent to managing coral reefs in the territory.

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
As a monitoring program, ASCRMP's goal is to document change i.e. where, how much, and what kind of changes take place at the various monitoring sites.

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
The ideal is to resolve change at scales which will allow judgments to be made as to which changes are within normal, natural variability, and which are outside it.

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
Eleven sites around Tutuila Island were initially chosen by the American Samoan Governors Coral Reef Advisory Group (CRAG) and a Monitoring Working Group that was established for this purpose.

--- SENTENCE 22 ---
Monitoring sites chosen represented what was thought to be a reasonable sample size around the main island of Tutuila and the nearby small island of Aunu'u, providing a reasonable geographic distribution and some of the variety of reef types and exposures (i.e. windward/leeward).

--- SENTENCE 23 ---
The site plan for the ASCRMP incorporated federal, territory, and community-based MPAs.

--- SENTENCE 24 ---
Three of the 11 monitoring sites are community-based MPAs within DMWR's Community-based Fisheries Management Program (CFMP) (Fagamalo, Vatia, and Amaua) and 2 sites are federal MPAs (Fagatele and Tafeu).

--- SENTENCE 25 ---
The project is coordinated by two staff members that are funded by the program; the Coral Reef Monitoring Benthic Ecologist, and the Coral Reef Monitoring Fish Ecologist.

--- SENTENCE 26 ---
The Benthic Ecologist position was filled by Dr. Doug Fenner from 2006 until August 2013 and in May 2014 Dr. Mareike Sudek was hired to fill the benthic ecologist position.

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
The Fish Ecologist position was initially filled by Lesley Whaylen in 2006 and then by Benjamin Carroll between 2007 and August 2012.

--- SENTENCE 28 ---
The DMWR's Chief Fisheries Biologist (Dr. Domingo Ochavillo) filled in the fish ecologist duties up until February 2013 when the position was filled part-time by DMWR fisheries supervisor Alice Lawrence and part-time by DMWR fisheries technician Saolotoga Tofaeono.

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
The position was filled full-time by Alice Lawrence in February 2014 which continues until the present time.

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
Based on population density and subsequent impacts to the watershed, the ASCRMP incorporated villages in the complete range of the ASEPA watershed classification scale; pristine, minimal, intermediate, and extensive (DiDonato 2004).

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
Three of the 11 monitoring sites (Leone, Nu'uuli and Faga'alu) are categorized as Extensive, three sites are categorized as Intermediate (Amaua, Aoa, and Fagasa), one site is categorized as Medium (Vatia), and three sites are categorized as Pristine (Fagatele, Tafeu and Fagamalo).

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
In 2007 an additional site, Massacre Bay, was added on the northwest sector to improve north-south/east-west balance, and thereby provided a balance of the four geographical quadrats of Tutuila (NW, NE, SE & SW).

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
In 2009 five sites surrounding Swains Atoll were surveyed for the first time and the program is able to participate in opportunistic research trips to Rose and Swains when possible.

--- SENTENCE 34 ---
Dr. Douglas Fenner conducted monitoring surveys in the Ofu and Olosega reef flat and lagoons in November 2011, which was visited again in February 2015 by Alice Lawrence and Mareike Sudek to establish semi-permanent transects (using GPS coordinates).

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
The program utilizes the DMWR Boston Whaler vessel to access survey sites around Tutuila, however the boat was out of action between January 2014 and November 2014, and again between November 2015 and April 2017.

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
There were attempts to utilize local fishing boats to conduct surveys with varying success, due to problems with late payments, safety issues and logistics related to the smaller and slower engines used by the local fishing boats.

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
In an attempt to pool resources and increase efficiency between the NOAA CRCP-funded Coral Reef Monitoring Program and the three USFWS-funded DMWR coral reef monitoring programs, in January 2013 the DMWR Fisheries division made a decision to integrate the programs which included the Key Reef Species Program (KRSP), the Community-based Fisheries Management Program (CFMP), and the No-take MPA Program (NTMPA).

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
The key objectives of the integration were to reduce duplication of effort and to enable the effective monitoring of 30 MPA and non-MPA sites using the same monitoring methods with the same trained surveyors.

--- SENTENCE 39 ---
It was hoped that a larger, more statistically robust monitoring data set would be available to answer different management questions by the different programs.

--- SENTENCE 40 ---
The fish ecologist worked on developing accessible monitoring protocols, and implementing training for species ID, monitoring methods, data entry and analysis.

--- SENTENCE 41 ---
Details of the Integrated Coral Reef Monitoring Program are shown in Appendix 1.

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
Unfortunately in September 2014 the USFWS Sports Fish Restoration grantors decided to cancel the agreement to integrate the programs due to grant administration issues.

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
This caused a halt in survey efforts as the integrated survey methods, which had already been carried out at six sites, require four divers in the water.

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
This led to complications with use of the DMWR boat and boat drivers for conducting surveys.

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
The ASCRMP program staff worked to adjust the monitoring survey protocols to allow the monitoring to be conducted by only two divers, and to increase data quality, add additional data, and make it more comparable to data collected by other monitoring programs (for example NOAA CREP, and the ASEPA watershed monitoring program).

--- SENTENCE 46 ---
In 2015 a total of 8 out of the 12 monitoring sites were surveyed, with benthic cover and demography data as well as fish diversity and biomass data collected.

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
The 10 surveys conducted in 2014 by DMWR Fisheries staff and the program fish ecologist as part of the integrated program, are considered as 'training surveys' and were not included for analysis.

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
Additionally, in 2015, bleaching monitoring sites were established at 8 reef slope sites, 3 reef flat sites and 4 pools.

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
Temperature loggers were installed and are regularly exchanged.

#### 2.1 Benthic survey methods

Up until 2014, the line-point intercept method (LPI) was utilized to assess benthic cover.

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
Four 50 m transects were laid out along a depth contour of 8-10 m and benthic categories recorded every 50 cm (100 data points/transect).

--- SENTENCE 54 ---
In addition, a roving dive was conducted to record species diversity recorded on the DACOR scale.

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
For more information on these methods see reports from 2005 to 2013 compiled by Doug Fenner.

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
In 2015, the methods were altered to increase data quality, add additional data, and make it more comparable to data collected by other monitoring programs.

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
The LPI method was changed to photo quadrats to increase the amount of data points collected and decrease the time spend collecting the data.

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
Additionally, coral demographic data is now collected along the transect to be able to make inferences of size distribution and recruitment.

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
Two 100 m tapes are laid out end to end on the 30 ft depth contour along the reef slope consisting of six individual transects (0-25 m, 30-55m, 60-85m, 90-115m, 120-145m, 150-175m).

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
The beginning of each transect will be permanently marked with a rebar (six rebars total at 0, 30, 60, 90, 120, and 150m).

--- SENTENCE 61 ---
For each transect, benthic photos are taken every meter using a 1m long monopod (26 photos per transect; a total of 156 photos per site).

--- SENTENCE 62 ---
These photos are then analyzed using CPCe to obtain benthic cover estimates for the individual sites (20 points/photo = 520/transect).

--- SENTENCE 63 ---
In addition, a 1 x 1 m quadrat is haphazardly laid out on the reef every 20m (10 per site) and every coral colony whose center falls within the quadrat isidentified to genus, assigned a growth form and the largest diameter and width is measured (Adapted from Houk et al. 2013).

--- SENTENCE 64 ---
When possible (i.e. when an extra diver is available), large invertebrates (giant clams, sea cucumbers, sea urchins and crown-of-thorn starfish) are counted and measured on a 2 m wide belt along the six transects.

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
If possible (i.e. when an extra diver is available), turf height is also measured at each site using 20 1x1 m quadrats (every 10 m) with 8 haphazard measurements per quadrat.

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
This will indicate grazing pressure at the site (turf >5 mm = indicates reduced herbivory).

# 2.2 Fish survey methods

Up until 2013 the fish survey methods included recording all diurnally active, non-cryptic reef fish were to species level using belt transects.

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
At each site, 6 replicate 30m transects were sampled with several passes and widths being used to sample different groups.

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
The first pass was used to sample larger, more mobile species 7.5m either side of the transect as the transect is laid out.

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
On the second pass parrotfish were recorded 5m either side of the tape.

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
The third pass surgeonfish were recorded 2.5m either side of the tape.

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
Every other species was then recorded 2.5m either side of the tape on the fourth pass, except damselfish, which were recorded on the fifth pass 1m either side of the tape.

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
Each individual fish was counted and an estimation of length recorded.

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
The fish ecologist and the benthic ecologist conducted their surveys separately and at times the distance between the divers underwater could be up to 150 meters.

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
The Integrated Coral Reef Monitoring Survey protocols were developed, tested and implemented between January 2013 and September 2014.

--- SENTENCE 76 ---
Information about the program and the monitoring protocols is shown in Appendix 1.

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
In 2015, the fish survey methods were altered to improve diver safety protocols in line with the updated DMWR Dive Safety Manual and in particular to ensure that the benthic ecologist and the fish ecologist were able to conduct the surveys in close proximity to each other.

--- SENTENCE 78 ---
Survey methods were designed to increase data quality and make it more comparable to data collected in the territory by other monitoring programs such as the ASEPA coral reef monitoring program (Houk *et al.* 2013) and the NOAA CREP protocols (Heenan *et al*. 2014).

--- SENTENCE 79 ---
Starting at 0m and then every 20m, a Stationary Point Count (SPC) is conducted in a 7.5m radius from the diver for 3 minutes.

--- SENTENCE 80 ---
A total of 12 replicate SPCs are completed along the transect tape.

--- SENTENCE 81 ---
Each SPC is a visually estimated 15m diameter cylindrical plot extending from the substrate to the limits of vertical visibility.

--- SENTENCE 82 ---
Each count consists of two components.

--- SENTENCE 83 ---
The first of these is a 1 min species enumeration period in which the diver records the taxa of all species observed within their cylinder.

--- SENTENCE 84 ---
At the end of the 1 min period, the diver begins the tallying portion of the count, systematically working through their species listing and recording the number and estimated size (total length, TL, to the nearest cm) of each individual fish.

--- SENTENCE 85 ---
The tallying portion is conducted as a series of rapid visual sweeps of the plot, with one species-grouping counted per sweep.

--- SENTENCE 86 ---
To the extent possible, the diver remains at the center of their cylinder throughout the count.

--- SENTENCE 87 ---
However, small, generally site-attached and semi-cryptic species, which tend to be underrepresented in counts made by an observer remaining in the center of a 7.5-m radius cylinder, are left to the end of the tally period, at which time the observer swims through their plot area carefully searching for those species.

--- SENTENCE 88 ---
In cases where a species is observed during the enumeration period but is not present in the cylinder during the tallying period, divers record their best estimates of size and number observed in the first encounter during the enumeration period and mark the data record as "non-instantaneous."

--- SENTENCE 89 ---
Surveys are not conducted if horizontal visibility is < 7.5 m, i.e., when observers cannot distinguish the edges of their cylinder.

--- SENTENCE 90 ---
Biomass per fish is then calculated using the standard length-weight equation.

#### 2.3 Bleaching

In 2015, bleaching monitoring sites were established at 8 reef slope sites, 3 reef flat sites and 4 pools, including installation of temperature loggers (see Appendix 3 for a list of sites with GPS coordinates).

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
On slopes, a 100 m tape was laid out on the 30 ft depth contour along the reef consisting of three individual transects (0-25 m, 30-55m, and 60-85m).

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
In pools and on reef flats, three 25 m transects were laid out haphazardly.

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
On the slope, the beginning of each transect was permanently marked with a rebar (3 rebars total at 0, 30 and 60m).

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
In pools and on reef flats, the beginning and the end of the transect were permanently marked with a rebar (six rebars total, 2 rebars/transect).

!

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
2.3.1: Map of Tutuila showing the bleaching monitoring sites.

--- SENTENCE 97 ---
Some sites have pool, reef flat and/or slope associated with it.

--- SENTENCE 98 ---
For each transect, benthic photos were taken every meter using a 1m long monopod on slopes and a 0.75m monopod on reef flats and in pools (26 photos per transect; a total of 78 photos per site).

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
These photos will then be analyzed using CPCe to obtain coral cover estimates for the site.

--- SENTENCE 100 ---
The coral cover estimates can then be compared to cover data after a bleaching event to assess the impact bleaching had on the reef assemblage.

--- SENTENCE 101 ---
Two HOBO® Pendant temperature loggers (www.onsetcomp.com) were installed at each site near two of the stakes using zip ties.

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
The logger (length 5 cm) was attached to an area of reef (solid rock) where no coral was injured in the process.

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
The loggers were exchanged every few months to maintain a continuous data set.

--- SENTENCE 104 ---
During a bleaching event, in addition to the photos, a belt survey will be carried out to obtain data on the percent coral that is bleached and how different genera are affected.

--- SENTENCE 105 ---
Every coral colony within a 1 m wide belt along the transects will be identified to genus and assigned a bleaching severity ranging from 0-4 (0=no bleaching; 1=partially bleached; 2=white; 3=bleached and partly dead; 4=recently dead; see Marshall & Schuttenberg 2006).

--- SENTENCE 106 ---
Additionally, a number of bleached colonies (a max of 5 per transect) will be tagged using numbered cable ties to monitor bleaching mortality/recovery over time at the induvial colony level.

--- SENTENCE 107 ---
Tagged colonies will be photographed during and after the bleaching event has ended.

--- SENTENCE 108 ---
The software 'Image\_J' will be used to calculate tissue mortality and/or recovery for each colony (area<sup>2</sup> ).

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
In addition, both divers will make an estimate of the general proportion of coral affected by bleaching for the reef as a whole using a scale from 0-4 (0=<1% - no bleaching; 1=1-10% - mild bleaching; 2=10-50% moderate bleaching; 3=50-90% - high bleaching; 4=>90% - extreme bleaching; see Marshall & Schuttenberg 2006).

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
Up to this date, no major bleaching occurred around Tutuila and so these methods have yet to gain results.

Analysis

#### 4.1 Benthic surveys

Mean percent coral cover ±SE was visualized using a bar graph and analyzed in SigmaPlot 13.0 using a one-way RM ANOVA to test for significant changes in coral cover over time.

--- SENTENCE 135 ---
The mean was used because not all sites were surveyed every year and therefore the dataset had a lot of missing data, weakening an analysis.

--- SENTENCE 136 ---
2014 and 2015 excluded due to 'training' and wrong site; change of methods

Benthic assemblage analyses were carried out using PRIMER7 and PERMANOVA+.

--- SENTENCE 137 ---
Data was transformed into a zero-adjusted Bray-Curtis similarity matric (Clarke *et al.* 2006) and examined using Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCO) plots.

#### 4.2 Fish surveys

Using the count and size estimate data collected during the surveys, the body weight of individual fish was calculated using length-to-weight conversion parameters taken from FishBase.

--- SENTENCE 138 ---
The fish biomass was calculated per unit area (g/m<sup>2</sup> ) to enable comparison between methods.

--- SENTENCE 139 ---
Species data was summarized into the following consumer groups: ''primary consumers'' (herbivores and detritivores); ''secondary consumers'' (omnivores and benthic invertivores); ''planktivores''; and ''piscivores,'' with classifications based on diet information taken from FishBase.

--- SENTENCE 140 ---
The 2014 and 2015 data were excluded due to these surveys conducted during training for the Integrated Monitoring program.

--- SENTENCE 141 ---
Fish assemblage analyses were carried out using PRIMER7 and PERMANOVA+.

--- SENTENCE 142 ---
Data was transformed into a zero-adjusted Bray-Curtis similarity matric (Clarke *et al.* 2006) and examined using Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCO) plots.

Results

#### 5.1 Benthic cover

In previous reports, coral cover was reported on an island scale (i.e. mean coral cover/year).

--- SENTENCE 144 ---
And although the graph implies a trend of increasing coral cover (Fig. 5.1.1), no significant difference in mean coral cover was found over time (One-way RM ANOVA: df = 11, F=1.978, p=0.066) between 2005-2013 (2014 and 2015 were excluded from the analysis, see section 3 for details).

--- SENTENCE 145 ---
There was a number of missing data (see the n values in Fig. 5.1.1), especially in the later years which is when coral cover appears to be increasing.

--- SENTENCE 146 ---
The average value for 2014 was particularly affected because the 3 sites that were surveyed that year had high coral cover.

--- SENTENCE 147 ---
This exemplifies how the number of sites surveyed (depending if high or low coral cover sites happen to be surveyed that year) can skew the average and show a misleading increase or decrease in coral cover.

--- SENTENCE 148 ---
It is therefore recommended that changes in benthic cover are only assessed at the site level if the dataset hasn't sufficient replication to report on an island-scale.

Coral assemblages on Tutuila are dominated by encrusting coral colonies (Fig. 5.1.7).

Discussion

In previous reports, coral cover was reported on an island scale (i.e. mean coral cover/year).

--- SENTENCE 235 ---
However, between 2005 and 2015, not all sites were surveyed every year.

--- SENTENCE 236 ---
It is therefore likely that the apparent trend of increasing coral cover in 2012 and 2013, and similarly the trend in decreased fish biomass between 2011 and 2015 is an artefact of less sites surveyed, 12 sites are not sufficient to draw conclusions at the island-scale.

--- SENTENCE 237 ---
It is therefore recommended that changes in fish biomass and benthic cover are analyzed and interpreted at the site level rather than an island-scale.

--- SENTENCE 238 ---
Generally, reefs on Tutuila are dominated by encrusting Montipora spp.

--- SENTENCE 239 ---
and overall, sizes of corals on the north side appear to be larger than the south side.

--- SENTENCE 240 ---
A distinct difference can be observed between the benthic composition of the north and south side of Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 241 ---
It is therefore recommended to subset the data into north and south sites for statistical analysis.

--- SENTENCE 242 ---
There also appears to be a pattern along an anthropogenic impact scale (EPA watershed classification based on population density).

--- SENTENCE 243 ---
However, one high impact site (Leone) is grouping with the low impact sites.

--- SENTENCE 244 ---
This site has, despite the high population density in its watershed, a very healthy coral reef with high coral cover and high diversity.

--- SENTENCE 245 ---
As only 8 sites were monitored there is not enough statistical power to subset the data set as recommended above.

--- SENTENCE 246 ---
However, in 2016 an extension of the monitoring program is planned in coordination with the Ridge to Reef program funded by the USEPA Wetland Program Development Grant.

--- SENTENCE 247 ---
In light of the need for more robust monitoring datasets the monitoring program staff have been working towards increasing the number of survey sites across anthropogenic and biophysical gradients to understand contribution of individual local stressors to reef health.

--- SENTENCE 248 ---
The program staff have been integral in the development of the USEPA-funded Ridge-to-Reef project which incorporates the existing 12 sites into a total of 30 sites.

--- SENTENCE 249 ---
In conjunction with water quality sampling of associated watersheds it will be possible to conduct analyses to investigate the influence of anthropogenic activities, and using GIS-based data to incorporate the effects of biological and physical factors such as benthic habitat, oceanographic conditions and geology.

--- SENTENCE 250 ---
The information will serve as a basis for watershed and coral reef based evaluations to inform local resource management decisions and implementation of effective community-based interventions.

#### **Fish Survey Data Summary**

--- SENTENCE 315 ---
Overall, the highest mean fish biomass were recorded within the National Park boundary between Pool 300 and Pool 600.

--- SENTENCE 316 ---
The highest mean fish biomass was recorded at Pool 400A with a mean biomass of 46.8 g/m-2 , of which 69% of the total biomass along this transect was attributed to herbivorous fish.

--- SENTENCE 317 ---
Noticeably, a much lower mean biomass of 12.9 g/m-2 was observed at the nearby transect Pool 400B, suggesting a high variability of mean fish biomass within the same pool.

--- SENTENCE 318 ---
The lowest mean fish biomass of 4.3 g/m-2 was recorded at Olosega C, which was a site adjacent to the Olosega landfill site.

--- SENTENCE 319 ---
The relatively moderate mean fish biomass observed along the three transects at Pool 200, adjacent to the Ofu airport, suggests that this area has overall lower biomass than the areas inside the National Park.

--- SENTENCE 320 ---
Mean fish biomass at Pool 200 ranged from 11.0 g/m-2 at Pool 200A to 20.9 g/m-2 at Pool 200C.

--- SENTENCE 321 ---
The highest proportion of the biomass along these transects was attributed to herbivorous fish, ranging from 65% at Pool 200A to 86% at Pool 200C.

The three transects surveyed along the reef flat in front of Olosega village showed high variability in fish biomass, ranging from 4.3 g/m-2 at Olosega C to 29.3 g/m-2 at Olosega B.

--- SENTENCE 322 ---
The trophic composition of the fish observed along these transects was noticeably different than at all the other survey sites, with an equal or higher proportion of the total mean biomass being attributed to lower carnivorous fish compared to herbivorous fish.

--- SENTENCE 323 ---
A similar fish population composition was observed at Pool 100B, in front of Ofu village, where 59% of the total mean biomass was attributed to herbivorous fish and 41% attributed to lower carnivorous fish.

#### **Benthic Survey Data Summary**

Overall, Pool 100B, 200C, 300, 400A and 600 had the highest coral cover (19-24%).

--- SENTENCE 324 ---
Highest coral cover was found in Pool 300 (24%) (Table 2 and Figure 6).

--- SENTENCE 325 ---
Out of the coral cover documented for the specific sites, mounding, encrusting and branching coral was most often encountered (Table 7.3 and Figure 7.7).

--- SENTENCE 326 ---
All sites also showed high percentages of sand cover (8-40%) and hard bottom covered in turf (11-45%) which is characteristic for these pool environments (hard substrate bommies with sand channels).

--- SENTENCE 327 ---

--- SENTENCE 328 ---

--- SENTENCE 329 ---
Soft coral was only found in Pool 100A (23%) which had very high flushing rates.

--- SENTENCE 330 ---
High amounts of coral rubble were found at Olo A, Olo B, Olo C, Pool 200A and Pool 200B (34-43%) indicating disturbance (see Table 7.2 and Figure 7.6).

--- SENTENCE 331 ---
Low to intermediate levels of bleaching were seen (genera mostly affected were Millepora and Acropora, some massive Porites) and low levels of disease observed.

--- SENTENCE 332 ---
Observations of benthic indicators suggesting nutrient input were recorded on the nearshore reef flat areas in front of both villages as indicated by the green stars in Figure 5.

--- SENTENCE 333 ---
On the reef flat in Ofu Village a grey encrusting sponge was observed covering the benthic substrate and on the reef flat in Olosega village mats of cyanobacteria were observed.

# **American Samoa Integrated Coral Reef Monitoring Program Survey Protocols**

# **Alice Lawrence, Domingo Ochavillo, Sean Felise, Tafito Aitaoto, Saolotoga Tofaeono**

#### **October 2013**

#### **Background Information**

In 2013 the DMWR Fisheries division made a decision to integrate the four coral reef monitoring programs that had been implemented with varying effort and methods over the previous 6 years.

--- SENTENCE 442 ---
The four programs are:

- 1.

--- SENTENCE 443 ---
Key Reef Species Program (KRSP) *– funded by USFWS Sports Fish Restoration Program*
- 2.

--- SENTENCE 444 ---
Community-based Fisheries Management Program (CFMP) *– funded by USFWS Sports Fish Restoration Program*
- 3.

--- SENTENCE 445 ---
No-take MPA Program (NTMPA) *– funded by USFWS Sports Fish Restoration Program*
- 4.

--- SENTENCE 446 ---
American Samoa Coral Reef Monitoring Program (ASCRMP) *funded by* NOAA

--- SENTENCE 447 ---
Coral Reef Conservation Program

The integration of monitoring methods from all four programs will involve combining components of the ASCRMP and KRSP methods, and focusing on the reef slope area at 10m/30ft depth.

--- SENTENCE 448 ---
Reef flat areas at the same sites will also be monitored as well as 3 offshore bank areas.

--- SENTENCE 449 ---
The following text describes in detail the proposed objectives and methodology to be implemented by the ASICRMP.

#### **The key benefits to an integrated monitoring program include:**

- The CFMP program decided to switch from reef flat snorkel surveys to SCUBA surveys on the reef slope.

--- SENTENCE 450 ---
Integrating the four programs will **reduce the strain on the boat and staff resources, and reduce duplication of effort where site lists overlap.

--- SENTENCE 451 ---
**
- Historically, most programs failed to complete surveys at their designated sites within a 12 month period, mainly due to staff absences and boat availability issues.

--- SENTENCE 452 ---
The integrated effort will ensure that **all the sites are completed on time.

--- SENTENCE 453 ---
**
- Combining efforts into one program ensures that a total of 30 monitoring sites can be surveyed in one year and therefore **more data is available for each program to analyze.

--- SENTENCE 454 ---
**
- A team of 8 DMWR staff available to undertake the monitoring surveys, split into 2 teams of 4 staff who will survey on alternative days.

--- SENTENCE 455 ---
This ensures that if monitoring team members are sick or unable to dive on a scheduled day, a backup staff member will be available to step in and **enabling the survey schedule to stay on track**.
- Monitoring surveys are scheduled for a block of 4-6 months during the period of most favorable weather conditions.

--- SENTENCE 456 ---
This ensures that **data is comparable between sites and ensures continuity and motivation within the survey team.

--- SENTENCE 457 ---
**
- One main survey protocol, with the opportunity to add extra components depending on program needs, ensures that **all data collected is compatible and available for data analysis.

--- SENTENCE 458 ---
**
- Monitoring team is trained at the same time and tests are conducted prior to start of monitoring season to **ensure quality control of data recorded.

--- SENTENCE 459 ---
**
- A total of 30 monitoring sites will be surveyed, creating a larger and more robust data set which will be available for each program to analyze with respect to their program objectives.

#### **Goal & Objectives**

The key **goal** is to monitor the status and trends in the distribution and abundance of reef biota on reefs in American Samoa on a long-term basis, in order to provide information that is pertinent to managing coral reefs in the territory.

#### The **primary objectives** are to:

- 1.

--- SENTENCE 460 ---
Combine multiple program resources to undertake annual surveys at 20 sites in Tutuila, and at least every 2 years in the Manu'a Islands, Rose Atoll, and Swains Island.
- 2.

--- SENTENCE 461 ---
Track patterns in fish and invertebrate abundance and coral reef benthos data in selected sites.

--- SENTENCE 462 ---
The now integrated monitoring program will also collect detailed size-frequency data for the most abundant hard coral species in collaboration with NOAA CRED.

--- SENTENCE 463 ---
- 3. Detect threatening changes rapidly and investigate rapidly developing problems, such as coral bleaching, algal outbreaks, disease outbreaks, crown-of-thorns starfish outbreaks, or cyclone and storm damage.

--- SENTENCE 464 ---
Collaborate with other locally-based agencies to understand the impact of land-based sources of pollution at the monitoring sites (to include sedimentation, water quality).

- 3. Ensure that each MPA sites has a relatively comparable non-MPA site nearby.

--- SENTENCE 518 ---

--- SENTENCE 519 ---

--- SENTENCE 520 ---

- **Sedimentation –** Sedimentation traps will be deployed in selected sites and regularly retrieved for sediment analyses.
- **Manta tow surveys –** Towed surveys will be regularly conducted to estimate crown-of-thorns abundance.

Other modified large scale towed or timed-swim surveys will be conducted as needed, for example, to estimate sea cucumber abundance which is a developing fishery and derive estimates of stock size for fishery management policy purposes.

#### **DETAILED Survey Methods** (for Reef Slope sites at 30ft depth)

#### Personnel required**:**

- (1) Fish Surveyor 1 (Large-bodied fish and all other fish)
- (2) Fish Surveyor 2 (Transect layer, rugosity)
- (3) Benthic Surveyor 1 (LPI /Photo-quadrat),
- (4) Benthic Surveyor 2 (Invertebrates, Coral disease, bleaching, disturbances, & transect retrieval)
- **1.

--- SENTENCE 523 ---
Site location –** A central point is located using GPS coordinates (and a metal rebar at MPA sites).

--- SENTENCE 524 ---
Two dives are undertaken at each site, with two 50m-long transects surveyed per dive, therefore a total of 4 transects are surveyed at each site (total 200m transect length).
- **2.

--- SENTENCE 525 ---
Underwater Visual Census Fish Survey – '**Fish Surveyor 1' (FS1) works with **'**Fish Surveyor 2' (FS2) to determine a suitable location to tie the transect tape and start the transect (not necessary where a metal rebar exists).

--- SENTENCE 526 ---
FS1 starts swimming ahead along the 10m/30ft contour line with FS2 following behind laying the transect tape.

--- SENTENCE 527 ---
When the tape is nearing the 50m mark, FS2 makes a noise signal to inform FS1 that the transect is coming to an end.

--- SENTENCE 528 ---
The second transect is started at a 5m/15ft distance from the end of the first transect.

--- SENTENCE 529 ---
Appendix 1 and 2 show the survey sheet for the fish survey and the juvenile fish survey.

--- SENTENCE 530 ---
- **Fish Surveyor 1** records the abundance and size (in cm) of the Large (>35cm TL) and Mediumsized fish (20 to <35cm TL) using a 20m total belt width for the Large fish and a 5m belt width for the Medium fish.
  -

--- SENTENCE 531 ---
Once both transects have been swum, FS1 and FS2 wait for 7 minutes at the end of the second transect.

--- SENTENCE 532 ---
- FS1 records the abundance and size (in cm) of the Small-sized fish (<20cm TL) and juvenile fish using a 5m belt width.

--- SENTENCE 533 ---
**Transect Laying & Rugosity Measurements – '**Fish Surveyor 2' swims away from the central point, and lays a 50-metre transect tape along the 10m/30ft contour.

--- SENTENCE 534 ---
In attempt to minimize bias in laying the tape a route is chosen based on depth and anchoring points for the tape, and not the substrate.
  -

--- SENTENCE 535 ---
The tape is passed along the sides of projections, including live corals such as Pocillopora and table corals, which usually have an overhanging side.

--- SENTENCE 536 ---
- If it is passed around first one side of one projection and then the other side of another, it is anchored securely from wave action moving it either way at that point.
  - A cleft that is narrow and deep is crossed straight to an anchoring point on the other side.

--- SENTENCE 537 ---
If it is large, then the tape may be laid along one side of it, going up toward shallower water but staying at 10m/30ft depth, and then when the bottom rises to that depth, crossing to the other side and continuing on that side out of the canyon.
  - If the tape is stretched between two points far apart and the surge is heavy, to minimize bias, the point on the bottom that is first seen from a vertical viewpoint, is recorded.
  -

--- SENTENCE 538 ---
When the tape is nearing the 50m mark FS2 makes a noise signal to inform FS1 that the transect is coming to an end.

--- SENTENCE 539 ---
The second transect is started at a 5m distance from the end of the first transect, and a second 50m transect tape is laid in the same way as the first.

--- SENTENCE 540 ---
Once both transects have been swum, FS1 and FS2 wait for 7 minutes at the end of the second transect, before returning back along the transect.

--- SENTENCE 541 ---
FS2 follows behind FS1 and at the beginning of the second transect FS1 carefully lays a 15m long Steel Rugosity Chain along the reef surface, noting the distance between the start and the end of the chain ends.

--- SENTENCE 542 ---
FS2 repeats this at the beginning of the first transect.

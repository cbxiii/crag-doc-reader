# Arsenic speciation in marine fish and shellfish from American Samoa

Peter J. Peshut a,b,\*, R. John Morrison Barbara A. Brooks c

American Samoa Environmental Protection Agency, P.O. Box PPA, Utulei Office Building, Pago Pago, AS 96799, United States
School of Earth and Environmental Sciences, University of Wollongong, Australia
Hawaii Department of Health, Honolulu, United States

Received 28 April 2007; received in revised form 28 September 2007; accepted 9 October 2007

#### **Abstract**

We speciated arsenic compounds in marine fish and shellfish from two islands of the United States Territory of American Samoa in the South Pacific, and found that inorganic arsenic occurred as a minor fraction.

--- SENTENCE 3 ---
The proportion of inorganic arsenic was generally far below the levels of prevailing assumptions typically used in human health risk assessments when only total arsenic is analysed.

--- SENTENCE 4 ---
Fish and shellfish were collected from Tutuila and Ofu between May 2001 and March 2002 (n = 383 individual specimens, with 117 composites); sites were selected based on habitat type and were representative of those frequented by local fishers.

--- SENTENCE 5 ---
These islands have moderately developed reef fish fisheries among artisanal fishers, are far removed from any industrial or mining sources of arsenic, and presented an opportunity to study arsenic variations in marine biota from un-impacted environments.

--- SENTENCE 6 ---
Target species were from various trophic levels and are among those frequently harvested for human consumption.

--- SENTENCE 7 ---
We found evidence that arsenic concentrated in some marine species, but did not tend to follow classic trophic patterns for biomagnification or bioaccumulation.

--- SENTENCE 8 ---
For the majority of samples, inorganic arsenic was less than 0.5% of total arsenic, with only a few samples in the range of 1–5%, the latter being mollusks which are recognized to have unusually high arsenic levels in general.

--- SENTENCE 9 ---
This work supports the importance of speciation analysis for arsenic, because of the ubiquitous occurrence of arsenic in the environment, and its variable toxicity depending on chemical form.

--- SENTENCE 10 ---
Keywords: Toxicity; Speciation analysis; Risk assessment; Fish advisory

### 1.

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
Introduction

Arsenic is the 20th most abundant element in the Earth's crust, and is primarily associated with igneous and sedimentary rocks where it occurs mostly as inorganic forms at an average concentration of 2–5 mg kg<sup>-1</sup> (Tamaki and Frankenberger, 1992).

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
Arsenic is ubiquitous in the global environment and occurs at low background levels in all environmental media.

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
Despite being recognised early as a poison, arsenic has been widely used by humans since ancient times (Azcue and Nriagu, 1994;

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
Gorby, 1994; Feld-

0045-6535/\$ - see front matter © 2007

--- SENTENCE 15 ---
It is used in the manufacture of glassware, metal alloys, microelectronics, agricultural pesticides, and wood preservatives.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
It is released through mineral processing and fossil fuel combustion.

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
Arsenic is also mobilized naturally through volcanic, geothermal and microbiological processes, and by weathering of crustal rocks.

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
Buat-Menard et al. (1987) estimated that anthropogenic activities release around 30000 tonnes of arsenic to the atmosphere each year.

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
A more recent review (Matschullat, 2000) estimates that the total anthropogenic and natural releases to the global environment are considerably higher.

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
In many locations, environmental concentrations are high enough to pose serious human health concerns.

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
Examples include groundwater contamination in Bangladesh (Anawar et al., 2002), West Bengal (Mazumder et al., 2000), and the black-foot disease area of Taiwan (Chen et al., 1994; Chiou et al., 1995), and coastal marine pollution near

The toxic effects of arsenic depend on oxidation state, chemical species, exposure and dose, solubility in the biological media, and rate of excretion.

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
Chemical form is the principal factor for determining human health risks from exposure to arsenic ([Phillips, 1990;

--- SENTENCE 28 ---
Although arsenic can occur in the environment in several oxidation states, the chemical forms normally encountered are not particularly toxic to aquatic organisms

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
Among the commonly encountered forms inorganic trivalent arsenite is more mobile, more soluble, and some 50 times more toxic than pentavalent inorganic arsenate, and several hundred times more toxic than monomethylarsonic acid (MMA) and dimethylarsinic acid (DMA) ([Jain and Ali, 2000\)](#page-7-0).

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
Some other organo-arsenicals, such as arsenobetaine, arsenoribosides, and arsenocholine are effectively non-toxic [\(Shrain et al., 1999](#page-7-0)).

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
The chemical form of arsenic depends on many geochemical and biochemical processes, and arsenic species in environmental media vary widely depending on organism, media, and geographic location [\(Cullen and Reimer, 1989;

--- SENTENCE 33 ---

--- SENTENCE 34 ---
[Greene and Crecelius, 2006\)](#page-7-0).

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
We note that the use of assumptions to define the toxic fraction for arsenic can be excessively conservative, and could lead to inappropriate public health determinations or unnecessary government regulatory actions.

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
For human health risk assessments, speciation analysis for arsenic compounds is necessary to determine the toxic and non-toxic fractions in the media of concern.

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
Here, we analysed inorganic and total arsenic in marine fish and shellfish, and found that the former occurs generally as a minor fraction, far below the levels of prevailing assumptions often used in risk assessments when only total arsenic is analysed.

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
Given the varied forms and toxicities for this common element, our results support the importance of speciation analysis for risk assessments for arsenic exposure from marine biota.

## 2.

--- SENTENCE 39 ---
Methods

The work was undertaken on two islands of the United States Territory of American Samoa, a group of volcanic islands and coral atolls (total land area 200 km<sup>2</sup> ) in the remote South Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 40 ---
The volcanic island of Tutuila (135 km<sup>2</sup> , pop.

--- SENTENCE 41 ---
60 000) is the centre of government and commerce, and was selected to represent remote islands with significant population density and economic development.

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
Other islands in this group are small and sparsely populated, or uninhabited.

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
The volcanic island of Ofu (8 km<sup>2</sup> , pop. 200), located 110 km east of Tutuila, was selected as representative of remote islands with small populations and little development.

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
Tutuila and Ofu have moderately developed reef fish fisheries among artisanal fishers, though the number of fishers is small in proportion to population.

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
These islands are far removed from any industrial or mining sources of arsenic, and presented an opportunity to study arsenic variations in marine biota from un-impacted environments.

--- SENTENCE 46 ---
The work was part of a seafood toxicity study (not reported here) completed by the American Samoa Environmental Protection Agency (ASEPA) that determined if consumption advisories were warranted for the archipelago.

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
Fish and shellfish were collected from 10 locations on Tutuila and one location on Ofu between May 2001 and March 2002 ([Fig.

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
Sites were selected based on habitat type (high island fringing coral reef, or sheltered marine bay with no reef).

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
Fringing reefs on exposed coasts are the most common marine habitat of American Samoa, and are where all reef gleaning and most fishing occurs.

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
Target species were collected from eight coastal locations on Tutuila, and from one coastal location on Ofu.

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
There are few sheltered bays in the archipelago and sheltered sites were limited to Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
These included Pago Pago Inner Harbour and Pala Lagoon.

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
The Inner Harbour is currently under a fish advisory by the ASEPA and was selected to expand on previous data on toxicity.

--- SENTENCE 54 ---
Pala Lagoon was selected because it is a shallow sea bay with limited flushing by coastal water.

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
There is extensive residential and commercial development in watersheds of the Lagoon and Inner Harbour, and there is potential that accumulated terrigenous sediments are a sink and source for contaminants in aquatic biota.

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
All coastal and bay sites were representative of those frequented by local fishers.

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
Target species were from various trophic levels and are among those frequently harvested for human consumption.

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
For coastal sites, the holocentrid Sargocentron spp.

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
(squirrelfish, malau in Samoan), and Panulirus sp. (reef lobster, ula) were the target organisms.

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
For fish, 18–30 specimens were collected from each location and composited for analyses.

--- SENTENCE 61 ---
Three composites were prepared for whole fish, and three for muscle tissue.

--- SENTENCE 62 ---
Each composite contained three to five individual fish.

--- SENTENCE 63 ---
To the extent possible, the fish in each composite were of similar size.

--- SENTENCE 64 ---
For lobster, three individuals of Panulirus were collected from each location, except for Onesosopo, where two Panulirus and one Parribacus (slipper lobster) were collected.

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
Lobsters were analysed individually due to limited availability.

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
In addition to squirrelfish and lobster, the Ofu site included three composites of muscle tissue for Acanthurus lineatus (lined surgeonfish, alogo).

--- SENTENCE 67 ---
The surgeonfish were not a target species for arsenic, but the composites were inadvertently analysed so these data were included in the data set.

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
For sheltered bay sites, composites were prepared the same as for coastal sites, except that shellfish composites were limited to whole fish.

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
Target species for Pago Pago Inner Harbour included Asaphis violascens (clam, pipi), Caranx papuensis (brassy trevally, malaulie), Megalaspis cordyla (torpedo scad, atualo), and Mugilidae spp.

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
For Pala Lagoon, target species were the

Several genera and many species of holocentrids occur in American Samoa waters, and other family groups such as the mugilids are similar in this regard (Wass, 1984; Myers, 1991; Randall, 2005).

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
In the field, fish and shellfish were collected by hand, dip net, hook and line, and spear.

--- SENTENCE 78 ---
Specimens were measured at collection (standard length for fish, carapace length/width for lobster/crab, and greatest shell dimension for clams).

--- SENTENCE 79 ---
All specimens were immediately double wrapped in heavy-duty aluminium foil after body measurements were taken.

--- SENTENCE 80 ---
*Asaphis* were immediately rinsed in native marine water and all epiphytic growth and debris were removed from the shell with a small stainless steel brush.

--- SENTENCE 81 ---
*Asaphis* were held in clean seawater for 6 h immediately after collection to facilitate depuration.

--- SENTENCE 82 ---
Data labels were prepared for each individual specimen and labels and foil-wrapped specimens were placed in zip-seal type

plastic bags.

--- SENTENCE 83 ---
Bagged specimens were immediately placed on ice and transported to freezer storage within 4 h after collection.

--- SENTENCE 84 ---
For *Asaphis*, wrapping, labelling, and freezing followed the depuration period.

--- SENTENCE 85 ---
Specimens were stored frozen at -20 °C in American Samoa until transport to the analytical laboratory.

--- SENTENCE 86 ---
Arsenic was analysed by a commercial laboratory that specializes in marine metals chemistry (Battelle Pacific Northwest Laboratories, Washington, USA).

--- SENTENCE 87 ---
For transport to the lab, frozen specimens were packed on ice in heavy-duty polypropylene coolers and sent via over-night air-freight (trans-shipped through Honolulu).

--- SENTENCE 88 ---
Specimens remained frozen throughout the transport period.

--- SENTENCE 89 ---
Chain-of-custody discipline was maintained, and all samples were maintained within specified holding times.

--- SENTENCE 90 ---
At the laboratory, total arsenic was analysed by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (US EPA, 1994) with an achieved detection limit of 0.0025  $\mu g \, g^{-1}$  wetweight.

--- SENTENCE 91 ---
Inorganic arsenic was analysed by quartz furnace atomic absorption spectrometry (US EPA, 2001) with an achieved detection limit of 0.0091  $\mu g \, g^{-1}$  wet-weight.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
Fish muscle tissue included dorsal muscle without skin, whole fish included the entire fish with scales and viscera, and whole shellfish included all soft parts and body fluids without shell.

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
Tissue samples were freeze-dried then ground in a ball mill for homogenization.

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
For both total and inorganic arsenic analysis, 10 mL of 2 M NaOH was added to a 0.5 g representative aliquot of homogenized tissue in a 15 mL

#### 3.

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
Results

Arsenic concentrations in American Samoa target species per composite, by location, are summarized in Table 2.

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
For comparison, arsenic data for biota from other tropical locations are presented in Table 3.

--- SENTENCE 111 ---
There was considerable variation of arsenic levels among organisms and media from American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 112 ---
Overall, values for total arsenic ranged from 0.235 to 98.2  $\mu$ g g<sup>-1</sup>, and from <DL-0.2438  $\mu$ g g<sup>-1</sup> for the inorganic fraction, for 117 composites (n = 383 individual specimens), from 11 locations.

--- SENTENCE 113 ---
Inorganic arsenic was below the DL for 80 of 117 composites.

--- SENTENCE 114 ---
For composites where inorganic arsenic was measurable, concentrations ranged from 0.0096 to 0.2438  $\mu g g^{-1}$ , and comprised 0.01–37% of total arsenic.

--- SENTENCE 115 ---
Disregarding the whole fish mullet anomaly (see below) the detectable inorganic fraction was 0.01-6.7% of total arsenic for 31 of 37 composites, and <1% of total arsenic for 22 of 37 composites.

--- SENTENCE 116 ---
For composites where inorganic arsenic was below the DL, a potential maximum was calculated using the DL and the value for total arsenic.

--- SENTENCE 117 ---
Of the 80 composites where inorganic arsenic was below the DL, all had calculated values <4%, and 65 of the 80 were <1%.

--- SENTENCE 118 ---
Combining detectable inorganic arsenic

and calculated maximums, 87 of 117 composites had <1% inorganic arsenic as a fraction of total arsenic.

--- SENTENCE 119 ---
Shell-fish composites accounted for most of the detected inorganic arsenic (25 of 31 composites) if the whole fish mullet anomaly is excluded from the data set.

--- SENTENCE 120 ---
As a group, lobster had the highest total arsenic of all species studied (19.8–98.2  $\mu g \, g^{-1}$ ) and the lowest measurable percentage of inorganic arsenic (0.01–0.20%).

--- SENTENCE 121 ---
Inorganic arsenic in lobster ranged from <DL-0.0828  $\mu g \, g^{-1}$ , and was detected in 18 of 27 specimens.

--- SENTENCE 122 ---
Lobsters accounted for about half of the composites where inorganic arsenic was above the DL (18 of 37).

--- SENTENCE 123 ---
The other crustacean (crab) had considerably less total arsenic than lobster (0.527–1.93  $\mu g \, g^{-1}$ ) but was roughly comparable for inorganic arsenic (<DL-0.0126  $\mu g \, g^{-1}$ ).

--- SENTENCE 124 ---
After lobster, the squirrelfish had the next highest total arsenic, ranging from 0.235 to 60.0  $\mu g \, g^{-1}$ .

--- SENTENCE 125 ---
There are some apparent outliers in the squirrelfish total arsenic data, with 49 of 54 composites within 2.11–19.6  $\mu g \, g^{-1}$ .

--- SENTENCE 126 ---
For *C. papuensis* (trevally), total arsenic ranged from 0.277 to 0.935  $\mu g \, g^{-1}$ .

--- SENTENCE 127 ---
Total arsenic for *M. cordyla* (scad) was higher than for trevally (1.17–2.53  $\mu g \, g^{-1}$ ) and lower than for squirrelfish (with one exception).

--- SENTENCE 128 ---
Inorganic arsenic was below the DL for all trevally, all scad, and for all but 3 of 54 composites for squirrelfish.

--- SENTENCE 129 ---
The fraction of inorganic arsenic (detectable and calculated maximum) in trevally, scad and squirrelfish was 0.05–<4.0% of total arsenic.

--- SENTENCE 130 ---
Total arsenic in the clam *A. violascens* was relatively low,  $1.26-5.90~\mu g~g^{-1}$ , being generally about one-twentieth that of the lobster, yet, clams from Pago Pago Inner Harbour had the highest inorganic arsenic of all species studied ( $0.2118-0.2438~\mu g~g^{-1}$ ).

--- SENTENCE 131 ---
Clams from Pala Lagoon had about one-third to one-half the inorganic arsenic as those from the Inner Harbour,  $0.0711-0.1018~\mu g~g^{-1}$ , although the fraction of inorganic arsenic was similar for clams from these two sites (4-7%).

--- SENTENCE 132 ---
These bivalves had the highest % fraction of inorganic arsenic if the whole mullet are excluded from the analysis.

--- SENTENCE 133 ---
Total arsenic in the mugilids was lower than the clams by one-half to nearly an order of magnitude (0.316–0.944  $\mu g \, g^{-1}$ , muscle tissue and whole fish, inclusive), which was amongst the lowest total arsenic overall.

--- SENTENCE 134 ---
Inorganic arsenic in muscle tissue from the mullet was below the DL for five of six composites.

--- SENTENCE 135 ---
In contrast, the whole mullet had the next highest inorganic arsenic after *Asaphis*, ranging from 0.0946 to 0.1818  $\mu g \, g^{-1}$  (10–37% of total arsenic), which appeared anomalously high compared to all other species, and might be explained by sediment or detritus in the gut.

--- SENTENCE 136 ---
Whole mullet from Pala Lagoon had about twice the average percentage of inorganic arsenic as those from the Inner Harbour.

--- SENTENCE 310 ---
The piscivorous top carnivore C. papuensis (trevally) had amongst the lowest total arsenic in this study, and inorganic arsenic was below the DL for all trevally composites.

--- SENTENCE 311 ---
Moreover, these fish were collected from the Inner Harbour, where terrigenous sediment input and accumulation is high compared to other areas of the Harbour or to fringing reefs (Peshut, P., unpublished data).

--- SENTENCE 312 ---
The mid-level carnivore Sargocentron (squirrelfish) had typically much higher concentrations of total arsenic than did the trevally, but like the trevally, had inorganic arsenic levels below the DL for most composites (51 of 54).

--- SENTENCE 313 ---
There was no observable pattern of arsenic compartmentalization for the squirrelfish, and arsenic concentrations in these fish varied widely and inconsistently between media and amongst composites.

--- SENTENCE 314 ---
Our data suggest that inorganic arsenic does not readily accumulate in these tropical carnivorous fish.

--- SENTENCE 315 ---
It might be that biochemical processes convert ingested inorganic arsenic to the non-toxic forms, either in the predator or the prey species.

--- SENTENCE 316 ---
The detritivore Panulirus (lobster) had total arsenic in the range of two orders of magnitude greater than the trevally, and in the range of one order of magnitude higher than the squirrelfish.

--- SENTENCE 317 ---
This could indicate that a benthic feeding regime is more important than a pelagic one for arsenic ingestion.

--- SENTENCE 318 ---
Lower total arsenic in crab than in lobster, with roughly comparable inorganic arsenic levels, may be attributable to a difference in habitat or to the non-aquatic component in the crabs' diet.

--- SENTENCE 319 ---
Our results reinforce two important points regarding arsenic toxicity: total arsenic in seafood may not be a good indicator of arsenic toxicity towards humans; and, speciation analysis is necessary for accurate human health risk assessments.

--- SENTENCE 320 ---
Risk assessors who limit analyses to total arsenic must assume a factor for the inorganic arsenic concentration; the calculated inorganic fraction is then used to establish consumption limits.

--- SENTENCE 321 ---
This is usually done in the interests of expediency and cost.

--- SENTENCE 322 ---
In general, factors of 5– 10% are applied to total arsenic values [\(USFDA, 1993\)](#page-8-0).

--- SENTENCE 323 ---
A few examples show that investigators should look at this practice more closely.

--- SENTENCE 324 ---
In two studies from the same location for oyster consumption in Taiwan, [Han et al. \(1998\)](#page-7-0) assumed 10% as the inorganic fraction of arsenic in Crassostrea gigas, and calculated cancer risk factors nearly 10 times greater than [Liu et al. \(2006\)](#page-7-0) who measured inorganic arsenic in C. gigas at 1.64%.

--- SENTENCE 325 ---
[Greene and Crecelius \(2006\)](#page-7-0) applied speciation analysis and showed that a fish advisory for arsenic was unnecessary for important recreational and commercial marine fish from the Delaware Inland Bays on the east coast of the United States.

--- SENTENCE 326 ---
Speciation analysis for our work also proved to be of advantage.

--- SENTENCE 327 ---
We provided evidence that inorganic arsenic levels in biota from coastal waters of American Samoa are generally not a concern, and we showed that the inorganic arsenic component of the Pago Pago Inner Harbour fish advisory could be limited to shellfish.

--- SENTENCE 328 ---
We were also able to justify that a regulatory action to require the territory to establish a Total Maximum Daily Load for arsenic for the Inner Harbour was not applicable (Peshut, P., personal communication).

--- SENTENCE 329 ---
<sup>b</sup> WF = whole fish, WSF = whole shellfish, MT = muscle tissue.

--- SENTENCE 330 ---
Our results are consistent with recent literature where arsenic speciation is described for biota.

--- SENTENCE 331 ---
For the majority of our samples the proportion of inorganic arsenic was less than 0.5%, with only a few samples in the range of 1–5%, the latter being mollusks which are recognized to have unusually high arsenic levels in general.

--- SENTENCE 332 ---
Given the recognized health advantages of seafood consumption, it is important to note the indications of assumed inorganic arsenic concentrations in human health risk assessments.

--- SENTENCE 333 ---
The use of assumptions by risk assessors and risk managers could unduly influence populations against local seafood gathering and consumption.

--- SENTENCE 334 ---
This could result in a loss of health benefits, and negative impacts on the important socio-economic activities of mariculture and wild harvest.

--- SENTENCE 335 ---
Erroneous ratios could also lead regulatory authorities to require costly but unnecessary remediation or mitigation actions.

--- SENTENCE 336 ---
These could be especially important issues for areas with small economies, and where there is no industrial source of arsenic to justify remediation actions or consumption advisories.

--- SENTENCE 337 ---
This work supports that speciation analysis for arsenic deserves greater attention in studies of arsenic toxicity, because of the ubiquitous occurrence of arsenic in the environment, and its variable toxicity depending on chemical form.

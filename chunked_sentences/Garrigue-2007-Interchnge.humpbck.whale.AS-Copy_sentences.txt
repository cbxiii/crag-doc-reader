#### ABSTRACT

The movements of individual humpback whales between winter breeding grounds of Oceania (South Pacific) were documented by individual identification photographs collected from 1999 to 2004.

--- SENTENCE 8 ---
Photographs were collected with comparable effort across the six years in four primary island breeding grounds: New Caledonia, Tonga (Vava'u) the Cook Islands and French Polynesia (Mo'orea and Rurutu) and with a smaller effort in a few adjacent regions; Vanuatu, Fiji, Samoa, Niue, and American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 9 ---
Interchange among wintering grounds was assessed using, regional catalogues of fluke photographs, representing 776 annual sightings of 659 individual whales from Oceania.

--- SENTENCE 10 ---
Most resightings occurred within regions (n = 78) but 20 individuals were sighted in two (mostly adjacent) regions.

--- SENTENCE 11 ---
Previously undocumented exchanges were highlighted within central Oceania and the west Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 12 ---
No individual was sighted in more than two regions during this six-year period.

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
The documented movement between regions was one-directional except for one individual sighted first in French Polynesia, then in American Samoa and then back in French Polynesia.

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
Only one whale was resighted in more than one region during the same winter season.

--- SENTENCE 15 ---
No directional trend was apparent and movement between regions did not seem to be sex specific.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
The movement of individuals across the longitudinal borders of the Areas V and VI, has important implications for the allocation of historical catches from the Antarctic.

#### INTRODUCTION

Results of preliminary comparisons of humpback whales in the South Pacific through photographic catalogues and genetic analyses demonstrate both fidelity to local wintering grounds as well as a low level of migratory interchange among wintering grounds of Oceania, South Pacific (Garrigue et al., 2002, Garrigue et al., 2006, Olavarria et al. 2007).

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
Here we extend the previously published information on individual movement between wintering grounds of Oceania based on four study sites by comparing adding five secondary catalogues.

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
We expend our data set using the information collected during six winter seasons, 1999-2004.

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
The fully reconciled and quality controlled catalogue provides new insight into the migratory fidelity and interchange of individuals among breeding stocks *E* and *F*, as recognised by the IWC (IWC, 1998; Garrigue et al., 2006; Olavarria et al 2006) and allow the determination of the degree of migratory exchange among study sites.

## **MATERIAL AND METHODS**

Dedicated surveys of humpback whales in Oceania were conducted between 1999 and 2004 during the austral winter in the four primary sites described below: New Caledonia; Tonga; the Cook Islands; and French Polynesia (Figure 1).

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
Surveys were conducted in only one or two seasons in other adjacent sites: Vanuatu, Fiji, Samoa, Niue (Figure 1).

--- SENTENCE 22 ---
Surveys at American Samoa began in 2003 (Figure 1).

--- SENTENCE 23 ---
## **Primary study sites**

*New Caledonia.*

--- SENTENCE 24 ---
New Caledonia is spread over 1,450,000 km² between 18° and 23° S and between 158° and 172° E.

--- SENTENCE 25 ---
It consists of a main island called "Grande Terre" and three groups of smaller ones : the Loyalty Islands to the east, the Belep Islands to the north and the Isle of Pines to the south.

--- SENTENCE 26 ---
There are many uninhabited atolls further from the main groups, including Huon, Surprise, Chesterfield, Bellona, Matthew, Hunter and Walpole.

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
Some whaling is known to have occurred in the Loyalty Islands, although most of the effort was concentrated in the Chesterfield Atoll area (Townsend, 1935).

--- SENTENCE 28 ---
Humpback whale photoid surveys were conducted sporadically beginning in 1991 (Garrigue and Gill 1994), and for three months each austral winter from 1995 onwards (Garrigue et al., 2001).

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
The present study site covers approximately 1000 km² and is located in the southeastern portion of the lagoon off the main island.

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
The Tongan archipelago is a series of volcanic islands and coral atolls extending from Ata in the south to Niua in the north.

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
The primary area of humpback whale density is thought to be the three major island groups; Tongatapu in the south, the Ha'apai group in the middle and the Vava'u group in the north.

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
Vessel-based surveys and the collection of individual identification photographs were initiated in 1991 (Abernethy et al. 1992).

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
Each of the three main island groups has been surveyed in at least one year but most of the field effort from 1994-99 was concentrated around Vava'u.

--- SENTENCE 34 ---
The majority of field work has been conducted in August and early September, although work in some years included late July and early October.

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
The Cook Islands are a group of islands and atolls scattered over approximately 2,000,000 km² of the southwestern South Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
These islands are divided into two groups, the Northern Cooks and the Southern Cooks; the latter include nine islands and atolls lying between latitudes 18°S and 22°S. Little commercial whaling took place in this region in the 20th century and records of earlier (historical) catches there are sparse although there is evidence that the local islanders hunted whales in Cook Islands waters.

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
Surveys for humpback whales in the Southern Cook Islands began with an exploratory three-week project in 1998 and continued with a four-month field effort in 1999, 2000, 2001, 2002, 2003, 2004 and 2005 by Nan Hauser of the Center for Cetacean Research & Conservation.

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
To date the survey has been focused on three locations: (i) Palmerston Atoll, a small atoll lying at 18° 04' S, 163° 10' W on the north western margin of the Southern Cook group; (ii) Aitutaki, an island located at 18° 55' S, 159° 47'W, roughly 300 km east of Palmerston; and (iii) Rarotonga, an island located at 21° 14' S, 159° 48' W, roughly 430 km southeast of Palmerston.

--- SENTENCE 39 ---
Other brief surveys included the islands of Atiu and Mangaia.

--- SENTENCE 40 ---
French Polynesia comprises five archipelagos (the Marquesas, the Tuamotu atolls, the Gambiers, the Society Islands, and the Australs) in the central South Pacific Ocean.

--- SENTENCE 41 ---
Sightings of humpback whales in French Polynesia's waters have been submitted to the sighting and stranding network run by one of the authors (MP) since 1988 (Poole 1993, Poole and Darling 1999).

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
The primary study area for fieldwork has encompassed the nearshore waters of the high island of Mo'orea in the Society Islands, lying at 17° 30' S and 149° 50' W, 18 km northwest of Tahiti.

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
Observational surveys of humpback whales in the nearshore waters of Mo'orea began in 1991.

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
Additional shore and boat-based observations of humpback whales were begun in 1999 at Rurutu in the Austral Islands.

## **Individual identification**

Individual humpback whales were identified from photographs of the unique markings on the ventral surface of their tail flukes (photo-ID, Katona et al., 1979).

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
Photo-identification of individual whales was conducted within each study site and the photos matched during annual meetings of the Consortium.

--- SENTENCE 46 ---
Catalogues were first matched within site, recording the number of within-site (within and between-year) resightings.

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
This review lead to 'reconciled' catalogues for each of the four main study sites, French Polynesia, Cook Islands, Tonga and New Caledonia, and five smaller catalogues for the secondary study sites.

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
The reconciled regional catalogues of unique individuals were then inter-matched during annual meetings of the Consortium, recording the number of between-sites (within and between-year) resightings.

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
All between-sites resightings were confirmed by three independent matchers.

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
This review lead to a fully reconciled catalogue for Oceania, including all within-sites (between-year) resightings and all between sites (within and between-year) resightings.

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
For most purposes, the small number of within-year, within-sites resightings were ignored.

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
For the purpose of the present paper, analyses were carried out only with information from the breeding seasons 1999 to 2004, referred to as the "synoptic years".

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
An initial comparison included all photographs considered all potentially usable photographs, as judged by each individual investigator.

--- SENTENCE 54 ---
The results of this matching were reported (SC/A06/HW55) to the IWC workshop on the comprehensive assessment in Hobart (April 2006).

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
Subsequent to this workshop, members of the Consortium agreed to review all photographs following a set of quality control standards developed for the North Pacific SPLASH program (Calambokidis et al., 2001).

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
Following that protocol, photographs (including some photographs representing different annual sightings of some individual) were graded from the best quality (1) to the lowest quality (5) for five different categories, proportion of the fluke visible, fluke angle, the lateral angle of the photographer, exposure quality, and contrast quality.

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
A photograph that received a 4 or 5 in any of the five categories was considered of insufficient quality for a representative comparison of resight rates within or among sites.

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
Removal of those photographs, including those representing resightings within or between regions, resulted in a 'quality controlled, reconciled' catalogue for Oceania.

## **RESULTS**

## **Individual identification: photo-ID**

Prior to the quality control review, the reconciled catalog represented 1080 regional sightings (including withinsites between-year resightings) of 949 individually identified humpback whales in Oceania.

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
The quality control review was conducted on 978 photographs.

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
Following this process 34 % of the submitted photos were of insufficient quality and were rejected (Table 1).

--- SENTENCE 62 ---
The comparison of all the catalogues lead to a total of 659 humpback whales individually identified in Oceania on 776 sightings.

--- SENTENCE 63 ---

## **Within study sites resightings**

Seventy-eight resightings were found within study sites.

--- SENTENCE 65 ---
A total of 33 individuals were resighted in New Caledonia between 1999 and 2004 which representing 21 % of the individually identified humpback whales during the same period (Table 1).

--- SENTENCE 66 ---
In Tonga 9% (n = 25) individuals were resighted inside the region and 13% (n = 20) within French Polynesia (Table 1).

--- SENTENCE 67 ---
The number of photo-ID recaptures between two consecutive years ranged between 2 and 7 individuals for New Caledonia, 0 and 3 individuals in Tonga and between 0 and 8 for French Polynesia.

--- SENTENCE 68 ---
No individual was resighted in Cook Islands (Table 1).

--- SENTENCE 69 ---
No individual was resighted within the secondary study sites in which sampling took place for more than one year (Samoa and American Samoa, Table 1).

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
## **Between study sites resights**

The comparison of all the available catalogs before photo quality screening provided 28 matches among study sites (Table 2 and 3).

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
Following the quality screening, this number was reduced to 20 matches (Table 2).

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
Nine matches corresponded to individuals observed in one of the four main study sites and resighted in one of the secondary study sites (Vanuatu:3, Samoa:1, American Samoa:5).

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
The other matches (11) were between Tonga and New Caledonia, the Cook Islands and French Polynesia.

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
Another was resighted twice in Tonga and then twice in French Polynesia and one was resighted twice in Tonga and twice in the Cook Islands.

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
One individual was resighted during the same year in two regions (Tonga and the Cook Islands).

--- SENTENCE 76 ---
All observed movement was one directional with one exception: an individual was sighted in French Polynesia, then at American Samoa and then back in French Polynesia (Table 3).

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
No individual was sighted in more than two regions during the six years of synoptic surveys.

The screening process lead to a 29 % decrease in the matches originally found as eight more matches were found analysing all the available information before this process (Tables 2 and 3).

--- SENTENCE 79 ---
Only one photos was rejected from these matches (5 for TG, 2 for CI and 1 for AS).

## **DISCUSSION**

The first information on movements of humpback whales in the South Pacific came from Discovery tagging (Dawbin, 1959 and 1964).

--- SENTENCE 81 ---
However, exchanges between the islands of Oceania was not never discovered (Table 4) until the 1990s when photo-identification studies was undertaken to follow of individual whales (Abernethy et al., 1992; Hauser et al., 2000; Garrigue et al., 2001; Poole, 2002; Gibbs and Childerhouse, 2004).

--- SENTENCE 82 ---
As in previous comparisons (Garrigue et al. 2002), the present study confims that the majority of resightings of humpback whales in Oceania occurred within study sites (80 % of the matches) indicating site fidelity and limited demographic exchange.

--- SENTENCE 83 ---
The rate of resightings varied within the four main sites.

--- SENTENCE 84 ---
The highest percentage of resightings was measured in New Caledonia (21 %) and the lowest in the Cook Islands where none were observed in the course of this six year study.

--- SENTENCE 85 ---
No resight was reported from the secondary study sites even in those where field work has been conducted for two years.

--- SENTENCE 86 ---
However, subsequent research has produced resightings at American Samoa (Robbins and Mattila, unpublished data).

--- SENTENCE 87 ---
It is also probable that humpback whales are known to inhabit others regions of Oceania where sampling has not yet been conducted.

--- SENTENCE 88 ---
Relatively few resightings were found between study sites (20 % of the matches).

--- SENTENCE 89 ---
However, the present results highlights previously unreported movements, especially in central Polynesia.

--- SENTENCE 90 ---
Tonga is now know to exhibit exchange with Samoa, American Samoa, French Polynesia.

--- SENTENCE 91 ---
Similarly, French Polynesia exhibits exchange with American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
In the western Pacific there is now evidence of exchange between Vanuatu and both New Caledonia and Tonga.

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
Most of the whales observed in multiple regions have been identified only once in each region.

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
No individual whale was sighted in more than two regions and all observed movement was limited to adjacent regions.

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
Most (70 %) of the resights were in the Central Pacific and involved Tonga, the Cook Islands, French Polynesia, American Samoa and Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
The others (30 %), were observed in the southwest Pacific and involved New Caledonia, Vanuatu, Tonga.

--- SENTENCE 97 ---
No movements were documented between these the central and southwest Pacific in the six year of this study.

--- SENTENCE 98 ---
However, the inclusion of all data available revealed movement of one whale from New Caledonia (1998) to Tonga (2001) to French Polynesia (2004).

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
Thus, it is clear that even scarce there are also movements on an ocean-basin-wide scale.

--- SENTENCE 100 ---
Whales identified in Tonga were involved in 80 % of the movements found in the Oceania despite the fact that this population was assigned to Antarctic Area V stock based on photographic, genetic and acoustics studies (Olavarria et al., 2006, Garrigue et al., 2002, Helweg et al., 1998 and 2000).

--- SENTENCE 101 ---
The comparison highlighted that the highest rates of exchanges between the study sites were between French Polynesia, Cook Islands and Tonga (40 %) then between New Caledonia, Vanuatu and Tonga (30 %).

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
This is consistent with the information that estimated the highest gene flow between Tongan and New Caledonian humpback whales at the haplotype level (interchange of 3 females per year) and between the Cook Islands and Tonga at the nucleotide level (interchange of 7 females per year)

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
(Olavarria et al., 2006)

Only one of the two photographs involved in eight resightings was rejected using the quality control system.

--- SENTENCE 104 ---
They concerned two previously unknown exchanges between the following sites: Cook Islands and Niue, and Cook Islands and American Samoa.

--- SENTENCE 105 ---
The other rejected photographs only decreased the total amount of exchanges between sites.

--- SENTENCE 106 ---
Considering the geographic position of the islands, no directional trends could be found.

--- SENTENCE 107 ---
Half of the documented movement was in a westerly direction and half in an easterly direction.

--- SENTENCE 108 ---
Sex information was available for eleven of the animals that moved between regions: seven male and four female.

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
This suggests that movement is not sex-specific (although it might be sexed biased).

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
Overall, the limited movement of individuals between adjacent sites within of Oceania is consistent with the significant (but low) level of differentiation observed in mtDNA from these regions (Olavarria et al., 2006) and suggests that humpback whales wintering in New Caledonia, Tonga and French Polynesia are demographically independent and should be recognised as individual stocks.

--- SENTENCE 111 ---
Because of the limited exchanges between the different wintering grounds it was not possible to consider the humpback whales wintering in Oceania as a single population for the purpose of estimating abundance (Baker et al., 2006).

--- SENTENCE 112 ---
Knowledge of rate of exchanges is crucial to construct a model of the abundance in Oceania as well as the knowledge of the connection between breeding and feeding areas.

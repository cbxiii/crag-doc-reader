#### INTRODUCTION

Edible marine organisms have been and generally still are the major protein source in the diet of the peoples of Oceania.

--- SENTENCE 13 ---
Analogous methods and equipment have been developed throughout the area to harvest this bounty as a result of species similarities, likenesses in shoreline topography and physiography, common cultural heritages and interisland communication.

--- SENTENCE 14 ---
Today, the islands are experiencing similar trends toward westernization, urbanization and technological development which have resulted in similar modifications and improvements on traditional methods.

--- SENTENCE 15 ---
An examination of current and traditional fishing practice in American Samoa is, thus, broadly applicable to any island in Oceania.

--- SENTENCE 16 ---
The Samoan Islands consist of a chain of seven major islands located at 14°S latitude and ranging from 168° to 173° W longitude.

--- SENTENCE 17 ---
Most are high islands of basaltic composition.

--- SENTENCE 18 ---
They are divided politically into Western Samoa, comprised principally of the two largest and westernmost islands, and American Samoa, comprised principally of uninhabited Rose Atoll, three small islands in the Manu'a group and Tutuila Island.

--- SENTENCE 19 ---
Tutuila is about 32 km long and averages about four km in width.

--- SENTENCE 20 ---
Two-thirds of the coastline is bordered by narrow fringing reefs which are partially exposed at low tide.

--- SENTENCE 21 ---
There are no barrier reefs and only a single well-developed lagoon.

--- SENTENCE 22 ---
Beyond the breakers on the seaward margin of the reef flat the bottom slopes rapidly to very deep water.

--- SENTENCE 23 ---
Tutuila is almost bisected by a deep natural harbor, Pago Pago Bay.

--- SENTENCE 24 ---
Considerable urbanization has occurred along the shore of the bay and about a third of Tutuila's 30,626 inhabitants live there.

--- SENTENCE 25 ---
# TRADITIONAL FISHING PRACTICES

--- SENTENCE 26 ---
The early Samoans spent much of their time fishing, not only because it offered a means of providing food for the family, but because they enjoyed it as well.

--- SENTENCE 27 ---
They were expert fishermen thoroughly familiar with the habits and behavior of their prey.

--- SENTENCE 28 ---
Fishing, by definition, was traditionally the work of men and almost all of the men, including the chiefs, were fishermen.

--- SENTENCE 29 ---
The practice of wading on the reef at low tide, perhaps with a sharp stick or knife, and gathering small fishes, sea urchins, shellfish, sea cucumbers, seaweed, etc. was considered the work of women and children and was not "fishing".

--- SENTENCE 30 ---
Women were not allowed to fish outside the reef or from canoes though a number of the community fishing techniques were open for their participation as well as the use of basket traps and scoop nets.

--- SENTENCE 31 ---
A wide variety of specialized methods and fishing gear was used to harvest hundreds of species of fishes and invertebrates (Buck, 1930).

--- SENTENCE 32 ---
Common practices included the following: fishes were driven into rock or coral heaps and surrounded by a net.

--- SENTENCE 33 ---
The rocks and coral were then removed to capture the fishes.

--- SENTENCE 34 ---
Snares were used to catch mantis shrimp, jacks, eels and even large sharks which were lured under a canoe by a rattle made from coconut shells and through a noose woven from sennit.

--- SENTENCE 35 ---
Octopus were caught on lures made from

cowrey shells, rocks and coconut husks and dangled just above the bottom.

--- SENTENCE 36 ---
Gill nets, throw nets, scoop nets and seines were skill-fully woven with cord made from bark or coconut husks.

--- SENTENCE 37 ---
One of the most popular methods of net fishing involved laying the net across a channel through which fish normally exit the reef at low tide.

--- SENTENCE 38 ---
Several lengths of vine or line to which coconut fronds and other leaves were attached were tied together and used to encircle a portion of the reef flat with the net as part of the circumference.

--- SENTENCE 39 ---
A group of people, sometimes including an entire village, positioned themselves along the line which was steadily drawn in to tighten the circle.

--- SENTENCE 40 ---
The leaves on the line, assisted by a great deal of splashing, shouting and rock throwing, drove the encircled fish into the net.

--- SENTENCE 41 ---
Spears and harpoons were crafted from sticks and bones.

--- SENTENCE 42 ---
Traps were fabricated with sticks and cord.

--- SENTENCE 43 ---
Fish were narcotized with a mixture of wet sand and the grated fruit of the Baringtonia tree or the roots of certain vines.

--- SENTENCE 44 ---
The substance was used primarily to drive fish from shelter and into nets or other means of capture rather than as a poison to kill them directly.

--- SENTENCE 45 ---
Weirs were constructed on the reef flats from stones, coral and even leaves to direct fishes into a small pond or pen in which they were trapped at low tide.

--- SENTENCE 46 ---
The early Samoans made a variety of hooks and gorges.

--- SENTENCE 47 ---
The most highly refined hooks had shanks of polished pearl shell to which were attached turtle shell points.

--- SENTENCE 48 ---
A complex tradition evolved for making and trading these hooks which were used to troll for pelagic fishes such as skipjack tuna.

--- SENTENCE 49 ---
The lure was trolled from a bamboo rod placed in a holder at the stern of the canoe.

--- SENTENCE 50 ---
Many of the traditional techniques required a group of fishermen (Buck, 1930) and much of the enjoyment the Samoans derived from fishing related to its social aspects.

--- SENTENCE 51 ---
Two or three individuals were necessary to position a gill net or to man a shark-fishing canoe; several boat crews were required for a skipjack fishing expedition and perhaps a hundred people were needed for a large fish drive.

--- SENTENCE 52 ---
The larger groups required a leader to coordinate their efforts and to make decisions regarding when to fish, which methods to use, how to divide the catch, etc.

--- SENTENCE 53 ---
The leader or fishing chief was called the "tautai" and was generally elected by common consent because of his knowledge and ability as a fishermen.

--- SENTENCE 54 ---
Most of the traditional fishing was limited to the reef flat or just beyond.

--- SENTENCE 55 ---
Samoans were not accomplished divers nor did they often make long ocean voyages between islands.

--- SENTENCE 56 ---
Nevertheless, they built planked canoes of excellent quality for offshore fishing.

--- SENTENCE 57 ---
Smaller dugout canoes were used for fishing in bays and lagoons and on the fringing reef at high tide.

--- SENTENCE 58 ---
#### TRADITIONAL MANAGEMENT TECHNIQUES

Ownership of the reefs and their resources was traditionally vested in the chiefs of each village in a like manner to land ownership.

--- SENTENCE 59 ---
Seldom did a member of one village fish on the reefs or within sight of another village.

--- SENTENCE 60 ---
Marine resources were, thus, controlled by a council of village chiefs who could institute any management measure they desired or felt necessary.

# CURRENT FISHING PRACTICES AND THE RESULTANT CATCH

During fiscal years 1977-1980, the Office of Marine Resources, American Samoa Government conducted a survey in 13 shoreside villages of Tutuila Island.

--- SENTENCE 70 ---
The study was designed to measure the fishing effort exerted by subsistence and recreational fishermen on the fringing reef or shortly beyond and to estimate the resultant catch.

--- SENTENCE 71 ---
Offshore recreational fishing from powered vessels and large-scale commercial fishing were excluded.

--- SENTENCE 72 ---
The results are useful for determining the economic, recreational and cultural values of the fishery and for aiding the effort to maximize these values on a sustainable basis through management measures.

--- SENTENCE 73 ---
Hill (1977) also conducted a survey of the shoreline fishery of Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 74 ---
His period of study and sample sizes were considerably smaller than those of the present investigation but the conclusions are similar.

#### Methods

The study was divided into two distinct portions covering different villages and using different methods of data collection.

--- SENTENCE 75 ---
During 1977 and 1978, nine contiguous villages (Faganeanea to Lauli'i) along an 18.8 km stretch of shoreline which encompasses Pago Pago Bay and about three kilometers of the exposed coast on either side were surveyed (Fig. 1).

--- SENTENCE 76 ---
Fishing effort was classified into one of eight generalized methods: day gleaning, night gleaning, bamboo rod fishing, rod and reel fishing, day diving, night diving, throw netting and seining.

--- SENTENCE 77 ---
Gleaning consists of walking along the intertidal zone or on the reef flat at low tide and collecting edible invertebrates and fishes by hand or through use of a knife, stick or spear.

--- SENTENCE 78 ---
The use of a bamboo pole and a fixed length of line is usually involved in bamboo rod fishing though handline fishing is also included within this category.

--- SENTENCE 79 ---
Diving involves the use of goggles or a face mask by a swimmer.

--- SENTENCE 80 ---
Seining is defined as active net fishing (e.g. drive-net fishing) with the fishermen in the water during the entire fishing period as opposed to passive net fishing (e.g. gill-net fishing) where the net is left to fish by itself.

--- SENTENCE 81 ---
Two types of surveys were made: effort surveys designed to determine average daily fishing effort for each village and method; and catch surveys designed to determine catch/man-hour for each village and method.

--- SENTENCE 82 ---
From Tutuila's main highway and short access roads, an observer with a pair of binoculars can see more than 95% of the shoreline in the study area without having to leave his vehicle.

--- SENTENCE 83 ---
Effort surveys were conducted by driving back and forth along the perimeter road for an eight-hour time period and totaling the number of fishermen engaged in each fishing method for each village.

--- SENTENCE 84 ---
These totals were multiplied by the quotient obtained from dividing the number of hours in the survey period (eight) by the number of times the observer drove past a village (usually eight) to calculate total man-hours of effort for the eight-hour period.

--- SENTENCE 85 ---
A total of 201 eight-hour effort surveys were made according to a rigid schedule designed to account for all sources of variation.

--- SENTENCE 86 ---
They covered all portions of the 24-hour day equally as well as weekdays, Saturdays and Sundays in order to measure differential effort for these periods and days.

--- SENTENCE 87 ---
Surveys were conducted during all weather conditions, tidal levels, wave heights, moon phases, seasons, etc.

--- SENTENCE 88 ---
Average effort was calculated for each eight-hour period for weekdays, Saturdays and Sundays and summed to get 24-hours averages.

--- SENTENCE 89 ---
The 24-hour averages were, respectively, multiplied by the number of days in the year to determine annual effort.

--- SENTENCE 90 ---
Holidays (excepting religious holidays and those falling on a Sunday) were treated as Saturdays.

--- SENTENCE 91 ---
Catch surveys were conducted on days when no effort surveys were scheduled.

--- SENTENCE 92 ---
Fishermen were interviewed to determine number, age and sex of fishermen responsible for catch, fishing method, length of fishing period and species catch by number and weight.

--- SENTENCE 93 ---
A total of 748 catches resulting from 1,969 man-hours of effort were examined from the nine villages in the greater Pago Pago Bay area.

--- SENTENCE 94 ---
Average catch/man-hour was calculated for each village and method.

--- SENTENCE 95 ---
These figures in turn were multiplied by annual effort estimates to estimate annual catch.

--- SENTENCE 96 ---
Effort resulting in no catch was, unfortunately, not recorded during the 1977 catch surveys though the use of correction factors enables retention of the data.

--- SENTENCE 97 ---
The factors were calculated from the 1978 catch data and are simply the ratio of hours of total effort to hours of successful effort.

--- SENTENCE 98 ---
The factors for the eight fishing methods varied from 1.00 to 1.25 and averaged 1.10 indicating that about one hour out of every eleven yields no catch.

--- SENTENCE 99 ---
The corrected effort values for 1977 and the actual values for 1978 were totaled and divided by total catch to computer catch/man-hour.

--- SENTENCE 100 ---
The second portion of the study was conducted during 1979 and the fist half of 1980.

--- SENTENCE 101 ---
It covered four of the more outlying villages of Tutuila (Faga'itua, Masefau, Fagasa and Vaitogi) (Fig. 1).

--- SENTENCE 102 ---
These villages are widely scattered and were chosen for their location and reef physiography as more or less representative of all villages outside the greater Pago Pago Bay area.

--- SENTENCE 103 ---
For this portion of the study the observer spent an entire eight-hour period within one of the villages.

--- SENTENCE 104 ---
Again, the survey periods were scheduled for equal distribution among the four villages,

the hours of the day and the days of the week and occurred during all weather conditions, tidal levels, moon phases, seasons, etc.

--- SENTENCE 105 ---
Catch and effort data were gathered simultaneously by the observer who positioned himself so that he could see the coastline fronting the entire village.

--- SENTENCE 106 ---
For each fishing party, he noted the fishing method, number, age and sex of fishermen and length of the fishing period.

--- SENTENCE 107 ---
Upon termination of the party's effort, the observer identified and weighed the catch.

--- SENTENCE 108 ---
Because the villages are relatively small, there were generally only a few parties fishing at one time so data were rarely missed because two parties stopped fishing simultaneously.

--- SENTENCE 109 ---
During the year-and-a-half study period, 199 surveys representing 1,335 hours of observation were made in the four villages.

--- SENTENCE 110 ---
A total of 118 catches representing 687 hours of fishing effort were examined.

--- SENTENCE 111 ---
The proportion of the year the observer was on site in each village was determined for weekdays, Saturdays and Sundays.

--- SENTENCE 112 ---
The number of hours of observed effort for each day and method was then divided by this figure to estimate annual effort for each day and method.

--- SENTENCE 113 ---
Average catch/man-hour was calculated for each method and multiplied by annual effort estimates to compute annual catch.

--- SENTENCE 114 ---
#### Results

Adult males tend to do most of the fishing as indicated by Table I which lists age and sex of fishermen by fishing method for the 1,119 fishermen whose catches were examined.

--- SENTENCE 115 ---
Only the day gleaning effort was dominated by women and children.

--- SENTENCE 116 ---
They also accounted for a significant proportion of the night gleaning effort.

--- SENTENCE 117 ---
Boys do much of the bamboo rod fishing.

--- SENTENCE 118 ---
Fishing effort showed considerable variation depending upon the day of the week.

--- SENTENCE 119 ---
Medium effort was exerted during weekdays.

--- SENTENCE 120 ---
During Saturdays and non-religious holidays when people have more leisure time and are often engaged in preparation for their Sunday meal, fishing effort was 1.77 times as great.

--- SENTENCE 121 ---
Religious beliefs and custom tend to restrict fishing on Sundays between sunrise and sunset.

--- SENTENCE 122 ---
Sunday effort was found to be only 0.32 times that of a typical weekday.

--- SENTENCE 123 ---
Table II results from compilation and analysis of the 400 eight-hour effort surveys conducted during the study.

--- SENTENCE 124 ---
It estimates annual fishing effort by village and method totaling over 92,000 man-hours.

--- SENTENCE 125 ---
Most effort is involved in day gleaning, bamboo rod fishing and day diving.

--- SENTENCE 126 ---
Seining accounts for the least amount of effort.

--- SENTENCE 127 ---
Greatest effort was expended in Aua which has a relatively large population and a very well developed reef flat.

--- SENTENCE 128 ---
The least effort was expended in Vaitogi which has no fringing reef flat and a rugged, basaltic coastline exposed to tradewinds and swell.

--- SENTENCE 161 ---
The reefs within Pago Pago Bay have been subjected to increasing amounts of dredging, filling, siltation and pollution since the late nineteenth century.

--- SENTENCE 162 ---
Approximately 95% of the reefs at the back of the bay fronting the villages of Fagatogo and Pago Pago have been filled.

--- SENTENCE 163 ---
Tutuila's main commercial dock and the fuel dock as well as a couple of smaller docks are presently located in Fagatogo.

--- SENTENCE 164 ---
Table III illustrates the considerable influence of filled and degraded reefs and the presence of piers, as well as reduced exposure to waves and currents, on effort and methods.

--- SENTENCE 165 ---
Inner bay villages differ markedly from all others in several respects.

--- SENTENCE 166 ---
Because reef flats are lacking, almost no gleaning effort occurs.

--- SENTENCE 167 ---
Turbid water and the lack of productive reefs result in almost no diving.

--- SENTENCE 168 ---
Instead, most of the effort occurs with bamboo rod and rod and reel.

--- SENTENCE 169 ---
The use of hook and line is encouraged by the presence of piers and docks offering easy access to relatively deep water.

--- SENTENCE 170 ---
Outer bay and near bay villages show a similar distribution of effort among the various fishing methods and appear to differ somewhat from that of outlying villages in that gleaning seems to occur more often while diving seems to occur less often.

--- SENTENCE 171 ---
These differences are probably non-existent or nearly so because they arise primarily from ambiguous definitions of gleaning and diving during the first portion of the study.

--- SENTENCE 172 ---
Effort which involved wading on the reef flat was sometimes recorded as "gleaning" by the two observers recording data during the first two years even though goggles or a face mask were used concurrently.

--- SENTENCE 173 ---
During the second portion of the study, a third observer recorded the same type of effort as "diving".

--- SENTENCE 389 ---
The average annual catch for the villages listed in Table X is 266 kg/ha which is equivalent to almost 27 metric tons/km2.

--- SENTENCE 390 ---
It is important to note that this figure applies to localized reefs directly in front of villages and subjected to intensive fishing pressure.

--- SENTENCE 391 ---
Hill (1977) calculated an average catch of 12 tons/km<sup>2</sup> for a 15.4 km stretch of coastline between Faga'itua and Pago Pago which included some sparsely populated areas.

--- SENTENCE 392 ---
His figure may be a little low, however, because he estimated the night fishing catch at only one-tenth of the day catch.

--- SENTENCE 393 ---
Catch estimates listed in Table V indicate that the night catch is about one-quarter of the day catch.

--- SENTENCE 394 ---
Hill's estimate also excludes the mackerel catch which would add about 4 tons/km<sup>2</sup> to his figure.

--- SENTENCE 395 ---
He has justification for excluding mackerel because they frequent the mid-water habitat and are not dependent upon the reef.

--- SENTENCE 396 ---
The figures listed in Table X however, indicate that the average catch for the seven villages located outside Pago Pago Bay (274 kg/ha) is higher thant the average for the four villages inside the bay (255 kg/ha) so it cannot be argued that the large mackerel catch in the bay is responsible for the relatively high average value.

--- SENTENCE 397 ---
Munro (1978) has compiled catch data for a number of tropical Atlantic shelf areas (to the 200 m isobath) and found maximum annual yields to vary from O to 1.9 tons/km2 in the Caribbean and from 1.2 to about 4.3 tons/km<sup>2</sup> in Jamaica.

--- SENTENCE 398 ---
These are largely trap and handline fisheries.

--- SENTENCE 399 ---
Marshall (1979) suggests a finfish yield of 4 to 5 tons/km<sup>2</sup>/yr as the generalized potential for tropical superecosystems which he defines to include "coral reefs, the shallows adjacent thereto, plus the immediate slope beyond the reef".

--- SENTENCE 400 ---
This figure is at the high end of fishery yield data compiled from areas in the Caribbean, Bahamas, Indian Ocean and the tropical Pacific.

--- SENTENCE 401 ---
Yields calculated for Samoan reefs by Hill and in the present study are much higher.

--- SENTENCE 402 ---
They represent highly localized situations, however, which do not include adjacent and deeper areas from which there is considerable recruitment.

--- SENTENCE 403 ---
Ease of access and the shallow depths of Samoan fringing reefs result in more intensive fishing effort with a larger number of participants and a more varied methodology that in the areas referred to by Marshall and Munro.

--- SENTENCE 404 ---
A considerable portion of the Samoan catch consists of a diverse assemblage of smaller fishes which are generally not taken in areas where the fishery may be largely commercial.

--- SENTENCE 405 ---
About one-third of the catch is also comprised of invertebrates which are not included in the calculations for other areas.

--- SENTENCE 406 ---
Finally, the yields calculated for Samoan reefs may be larger because catches are derived from shallower bottoms which receive more sunlight and, consequently, are more productive and able to support a relatively greater biomass.

--- SENTENCE 407 ---
Herbivorous fishes which are highly prized by Samoan fishermen are particularly abundant.

--- SENTENCE 408 ---
The annual catch for the entire island of Tutuila is estimated at about 300 tons in Table IX.

--- SENTENCE 409 ---
Division of this figure by the area within the 37 m (20 ft) isobath (64 km²) surrounding Tutuila yields a figure of about 4.7 tons/km² which is equivalent to the maximum potential suggested by Marshall.

--- SENTENCE 410 ---
An additional 297 km² of shelf area surrounds the island to depths of 187 m (100 fm).

--- SENTENCE 411 ---
Annual catches of demersal and nearshore pelagic species (by commercial and recreational powerboat fishermen) within this area average only about 20 tons.

--- SENTENCE 412 ---
Exploitation of these resources, thus, decreases markedly with depth and distance from shore.

# TRENDS OF CHANGE IN FISHING PRACTICE

Changing Samoan fishing practices are largely due to modern technology, dilution and loss of culture, increasing population pressure and urbanization.

--- SENTENCE 414 ---
Methodological changes directly attributable to modern technology include the use of rods and reels, power-driven fishing boats and SCUBA along with increased access to fishes in deeper water through fishing from man-made piers and docks and around fish aggregation devices such as deep-anchored buoys and artificial reefs.

--- SENTENCE 415 ---
The efficiency of traditional methods is improved with the use of metal hooks and wire for traps and weirs; synthetic lines and nets; fins, diving mask and snorkel; rubber-powered lances and spearguns; underwater lights and gas lanterns; and dynamite and more potent poisons.

--- SENTENCE 416 ---
Some practices have change very little.

--- SENTENCE 417 ---
Men still do most of the fishing while women and children do most of the gleaning and Sunday is still considered a day for attending church and resting rather than fishing.

--- SENTENCE 418 ---
A 1980 survey conducted by the Development Planning Office, American Samoa Government revealed that less than 9% of the subsistence/recreational catch is sold so most is still consumed by the family and relatives.

--- SENTENCE 419 ---
A greater proportion of a fishermen's catch is consumed by his immediate family today, however, than was traditionally the case.

--- SENTENCE 420 ---
Prior to the availability of refrigeration, catches were more widely distributed because they would spoil if not consumed within a few days.

--- SENTENCE 421 ---
Increased efficiency and reliance on technology have resulted in a trend toward more generalized fishing methods (Hill, 1977).

--- SENTENCE 422 ---
Traditional methods and gear designed to capture a specific kind of fish in a particular location are no longer used because the required gear is not commercially produced or because the fish is as vulnerable to a generalized method using modern equipment.

--- SENTENCE 423 ---
The dependency of Samoans on their marine resources is decreasing.

--- SENTENCE 424 ---
Most no longer spend a major portion of their time fishing because fish and other foods can be easily purchased.

--- SENTENCE 425 ---
Canned and frozen fish, poultry and meat are an attractive alternative to self-caught fish because they are convenient to purchase and store, offer a variety of taste, make excellent gifts for weddings, funerals and other cultural functions, and convey a sense of prestige to those who use them.

--- SENTENCE 426 ---
Seafood consumption, however, remains high for Tutuila.

--- SENTENCE 427 ---
The data in Table XI are used to estimate per capita consumption at 67.3 kg/year.

--- SENTENCE 428 ---
In contrast, the 1977 per capita consumption of fishery products has been estimated at 5.8 kg for the United States and 10.3 kg for the state of Hawaii (Hudgins, 1980).

--- SENTENCE 429 ---
Only about 17% of the fish consumed on Tutuila is locally-caught.

--- SENTENCE 430 ---
Canned mackerel from Japan is the largest import.

--- SENTENCE 431 ---
Growing numbers of automobiles and buses have given the Samoans increased mobility and a widened choide of fishing grounds.

--- SENTENCE 432 ---
Hill (1977) found that 79% of the fishermen still limit their effort to the reefs of their own villages but there is an increasing tendency to fish the reefs of neighboring villages and the bay area.

--- SENTENCE 433 ---
While there are no quantitative data on group fishing effort prior to the study by Hill (1977), it appears there is also a trend toward decreasing group effort.

--- SENTENCE 434 ---
Hill found group size to average only 1.2 to 2.3 fishermen for seven of the eight methods he examined.

--- SENTENCE 435 ---
Group size for the eighth method (gill-net fishing) averaged 3.9 individuals.

--- SENTENCE 436 ---
Of the eight active methods analyzed in the present study, seining, the most obvious group fishing method, was found to be the least practiced.

--- SENTENCE 437 ---
A decreasing influence associated with the position of tautai parallels the decline in group effort and fishing in general.

--- SENTENCE 438 ---
Cultural breakdown has eroded the tautai's ability to ordain and direct fishing activities and the declining importance of fishing has further reduced the need for such a position.

--- SENTENCE 439 ---
There are few tautai's left on Tutuila and most are old and will probably not be replaced.

--- SENTENCE 440 ---
The present population of Tutuila is more than six times as large as it was in 1899 when American Samoa was ceded to the United States.

--- SENTENCE 441 ---
Increasing population coupled with a decrease in available fishing area due to reef destruction and pollution have resulted in declining catches and a reduction in per capita effort.

--- SENTENCE 442 ---
Total effort, however, is probably greater than it was at the end of the nineteenth century because of the much larger population.

--- SENTENCE 443 ---
Increased effort, along with new methodology and increased gear efficiency, has placed heavy pressure on the reources.

--- SENTENCE 444 ---
# CURRENT MANAGEMENT MEASURES AND FUTURE STRATEGY

--- SENTENCE 445 ---
The only American Samoa Government regulations relating directly to fisheries management prohibit the use of dynamite and the "reckless use" of poison.

--- SENTENCE 446 ---
A U.S. federal regulation prohibits the taking of sea turtles and their eggs though it is not effectively enforced.

--- SENTENCE 447 ---
Federal regulations included within the Environmental Protection Act assist in protecting the reefs and inshore environment from pollution, dredging, filling and siltation.

--- SENTENCE 448 ---
Rose Atoll has been established as a fish and wildlife refuge by the territorial and federal governments for the protection of sea turtles and sea birds.

--- SENTENCE 468 ---
Current management efforts by village councils are not as extensive as in the past.

--- SENTENCE 469 ---
Councils should be encouraged to take a more active role in future management schemes based on scientific principles as well as traditional religious beliefs and resource preservation for village use.

--- SENTENCE 470 ---
A fisheries manager armed with catcheffort and socio-economic data and some tentative management recommendations as a basis for discussion should meet with the councils and solicit their opinion regarding management objectives and the means for their achievement.

--- SENTENCE 471 ---
A management plan applicable to the island as a whole could then be formulated and returned to the councils for their approval and support.

--- SENTENCE 472 ---
Much or all of the enforcement responsibility would be assumed by the villages if the councils firmly believed that the management strategy was beneficial.

--- SENTENCE 473 ---
Both village council members and fishermen must have an appreciation for the marine environment and a basic knowledge of conservation principles and rationale in order for fisheries management to be effective.

--- SENTENCE 474 ---
These objectives can be achieved only through education.

--- SENTENCE 475 ---
Steps must be taken to instill these qualities in future generations so that Samoans can continue to derive substantial benefits from their shoreline fishery.

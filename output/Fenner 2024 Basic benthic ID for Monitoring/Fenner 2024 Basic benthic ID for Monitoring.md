### Introduction

 This is a basic guide to identifying life found in benthic monitoring surveys in American Samoa. It will cover the major groups such as algae, sessile invertebrates, relatives of corals, soft corals, hard corals, indicator species, and various kinds of damage including bleaching, predation, and diseases. Identification will only be of the most common or important species, non-corals will only to be to genus and in some cases with no scientific name. There will be references to works that have many more species and much more advanced identification, so things can be identified further. But there will be some general info on the importance of different groups, and some basic clues for identification, including the feel of things as well as their looks.

 In general, it is my feeling that in monitoring it is good to record as much detail as possible, to record to as fine a category as possible. You can always lump things together later if you want to. If you have a video or photo record of your transect then indeed you can go back and record more detail later if you want to. But if you do something like point intercept like I do, then if you don't record it when you first record the data, you don't have it and can't go back and do it in greater detail later. So that is an advantage of a video or photo method. On the other hand, if I ID things under water, I can do it with greater detail than I can do from photos or video. Things that are clear under water are sometimes blurry or off color in video or photos. I also like to look at the reef while I am collecting monitoring data, since I can often notice interesting or important things that may not be in my transects. An important part of monitoring is keeping an eye open for anything unusual, as well as just learning about the reef and trying to understand it better.

 Two of the most important groups on the bottom on coral reefs are corals and algae. We often put a lot more emphasis on corals than algae, but algae are actually often just as important as corals, and in some cases are just as pretty. I will begin with algae.

### ALGAE

 Algae can be grouped by taxonomic groups or by functional groups. We will do some of both, grouping them by taxonomy but discussing the functional groups as well. Monitoring categories are often by functional group. Functional groups include turf (filamentous algae), macroalgae, coralline algae, and cyanobacteria. The taxonomic groups are identified by color, green, brown, red, yellow, and blue-green (=cyanobacteria). Algae are natural, there is quite a bit of it on reefs that are far from people, so having quite a bit is not necessarily a bad thing. But some types bloom when there are too many nutrients or not enough herbivores, so it is good to monitor those. I'll be pointing out which ones those are. Other types of algae produce calcium and are major contributors to building reefs, in some places they can contribute even more than corals. So they are good things to have on a reef, and I'll be pointing out which ones are big contributors.

#### Green algae (Chlorophyta)

 Green algae are virtually always green, and algae in the other groups are not green, so color is a pretty good ID tool for groups. Some green algae are "filamentous" or "turf." Filamentous is made of fine filaments made of strings of single cells. Turf is a mixture of filamentous algae species but may also include some species that are like thin wires. In some systems, turf is considered anything less than about 2 cm (1 inch) tall. But there are filamentous species that make somewhat larger clumps. Those larger species are relatively rare, so the problem of how to record them isn't important most of the time.

 Turf algae is important on reefs as fish food. It grows fast but doesn't have toxic chemicals or lots of cellulose, so it is nutritious. Most herbivorous fish concentrate on turf algae, and it actually grows faster when it is cropped by herbivores. A reef that has little turf will generally have fewer fish than a reef with turf, so turf is good for fish. The filaments are too small to ID to even genus, and they are usually mixed assemblages and

can have filamentous red or brown species as well as green. It can also be called filamentous algae or algal lawn. Virtually every surface on a reef has something growing on it, and newly killed coral starts to turn green from turf in a few days. Rock that is not pearly white has some turf growing on it. Fish can keep it cropped close and hard to see except for the color.

![](_page_5_Picture_1.jpeg)

Green turf growing where a damselfish has bitten off coral polyps. The brown fuzzy algae is also turf.

![](_page_6_Picture_0.jpeg)

Turf, which is another term for filamentous algae. Every surface on a reef that doesn't have something else growing on it quickly grows turf. Any place where coral is newly dead or the reef is broken will be white immediately after the coral dies or the reef is broken. Very quickly, within a few days, the white surface will turn green with filamentous algae, also called turf. Anything that isn't white has something growing on it, which is virtually all surfaces on reefs (bare volcanic basalt would be black). Turf is commonly green fuzz, though it can be brown. Turf or filamentous algae does not have distinct "leaves" which are called thallus in macroalgae. Turf grows extremely fast, and is not defended with chemicals, calcium, or cellulose. So it is excellent fish food, and many fish eat it. When a fish scrapes the turf off, the base of the filament is left attached to the rock, and quickly grows back.

![](_page_7_Picture_0.jpeg)

Chorodesmis, a filamentous green alga that forms distinctive clumps. It contains toxic chemicals.

 Caulerpa is a type of macroalgae and a fun genus, not very common, but fairly easy to ID to genus and even species. They are amazing in that a whole plant is all one cell, so it has no divisions by cell walls, but it has many nuclei. It is edible. Each round ball is a "thallus."

![](_page_8_Picture_1.jpeg)

Caulerpa racemosa

 Halimeda is one of the most important genera of macroalgae. Each plant is one cell with many nuclei. It has plates that are usually in strings. Each plate produces calcium inside. When the plate dies the calcium is then in the form of a porous flake. Halimeda is abundant some places, so it produces lots of calcium, and some sands are made of all these little flakes or chips that are from Halimeda. So it is a major reef and sand builder, and makes an important contribution to reefs. There are several other kinds of algae that contribute calcium, which we will point out as we go. Halimeda is pretty common on some lower reef slopes, occasionally it can be the dominant form of life. Halimeda is an example of what are called "macroalgae" or "fleshy algae" or "frondose algae." Algae can pretty much be divided up into "turf" (filamentous algae) on the one hand, and "macroalgae" on the other. They can also be divided into "calcifying" and "noncalcifying" based on whether they produce calcium, though the ones that do produce it vary in how much they produce. Halimeda is one of the ones that produces a lot. Each green segment is called a "thallus." By the way, when you see it, often the segment at the end of a string is light green. That is a new segment. They can put on a new segment in only one night! They fill it with cytoplasm from the rest of the segments. The segments produce calcium probably partly to make themselves less attractive to eat. In a sense the difference between turf and macroalgae is between fast-growing undefended and heavily browsed turf on the one hand, and on the other hand macroalgae that is slow-growing and heavily defended. Macroalgae defends itself with calcium, and/or tough cellulose (as appealing to eat as wood), and/or nasty chemicals. Most fish don't like to eat macroalgae, they prefer turf. A reef that has more turf can feed more fish than one that has little turf.

![](_page_10_Picture_0.jpeg)

Halimeda

 Boodlea composita is a green alga formed of fibers in a tangled mass. The fibers are a bit hard, so if you crush it in your hand it will feel crunchy. It seems to respond to nutrients, coming and going near land. The coming and going may be in response to pulses of nutrients coming out of streams. An alga like Boodlea composita that is stimulated by nutrients, can be a bioindicator of nutrients, much easier to use to detect nutrients than chemical analysis, since you just look for it instead of having to pay for fancy chemistry. Also, the algae sum up the average nutrients, while if you take water samples for chemistry, most of the nutrients probably come out as pulses which you will miss unless you take a sample at just the right time during a pulse runoff rain event.

![](_page_11_Picture_1.jpeg)

Boodlea composita

 Dictyosphaeria is a green alga that makes small plants that are usually tightly attached to the substrate and may be tucked between other hard things. It is relatively solid, with no filaments or leaf-like structures. Algae in this genus bloomed in Kaneohe Bay, Oahu, when the effluent from sewage plants was still piped into the bay, and it became less common when they extended the pipes outside the bay so sewage effluent no longer was released in the bay. So it could be an indicator for nutrients. It is not very obvious here.

#### Brown algae (Phaeophyta)

 Brown algae have several important genera for reefs. One is Dictyota, which forms small flattened strips that divide sideways. It forms clumps, and can grow over corals. It is one of the species that grows when a reef makes a phase shift from coral to algae, and it is likely stimulated by nutrients. It is chemically defended with nasty chemicals, so fish don't like to eat it. It may come and go in an area, which may be natural or follow nutrient inputs coming and going. In 2008, it suddenly was growing all over the slopes including corals at Vatia. It had been decreasing and then the tsunami took the rest away. But it is a species to watch. It doesn't produce calcium.

![](_page_14_Picture_2.jpeg)

Dictyota

 Padina is a brown algae that produces thin flat curled surfaces. It is like Dictyota in that it is one of the species that can bloom in response to nutrients, and can increase when a reef shifts from coral to algae. It is also said to be readily eaten by herbivorous fish, so when it is present it is an indicator that there are likely not enough herbivorous fish in that area. For several years there was a lot of it along the shore of Coconut Pt. It produces small amounts of calcium, so while it is flexible, it feels crunchy if you crush it in your hand.

![](_page_15_Picture_1.jpeg)

Padina

 Sargassum is a brown algae that is so rare here I have never seen it here. It is reported from rocky intertidal. It is illustrated in the guide to the seaweeds of American Samoa by Posa Skelton. That guide is on pdf, is free, and I'd be glad to provide it to anyone. It has more species than presented here. Sargassum is one of the species that can take over when a reef changes phase from coral to algae, and it probably responds to nutrients. The reef flat on the south side of the big island in Fiji is covered with it, a mass of it. They have a lot of people in villages, piggeries, hotels releasing sewage, so lots of nutrients, also with lots of people they have fished the reefs pretty heavily. There is no way to know whether nutrients cause the algae bloom or lack of herbivorous fish, or both. I always say assume it is both, and work on both, because that way you won't pick the wrong one. If you have a place where it looks like nutrients are probably to blame, then work on that, if there is a place where herbivorous fish are mostly gone, work on that. By the way, herbivores are said to have "top down" control on algae, and nutrients have "bottom up" control of algae, and there is scientific evidence of both having effects on algae, though more studies report control by herbivores than by nutrients. Worst is if you have both nutrients and lack herbivores. The fact that our reef flat has almost no macroalgae most places is a stark contrast to the one in Fiji which is a mass of macroalgae, all bad types.

![](_page_16_Picture_1.jpeg)

Sargassum collected by Kelley Anderson Tagarino from a tide pool.

 Turbinaria is a brown algae that consists of a clump of very tough spiky units connected by a thin stem. It is known as one of the species that can take over in a phase shift from corals to algae, and is abundant along with Sargassum on the reef flats on the south side of Viti Levu, Fiji. I did not see it on reef flats until recently, and now there appear to be some, with maybe 3-6 feet between them so they are not very obvious, but I did not see them on reef flats before. This is one to watch.

#### Golden algae

 This you won't find in the books, and there are only a few kinds known. One type known from reefs is called "Golden Noodle" because it forms yellow noodle or tube-like extensions. It is very soft and fragile, and will fall apart in your hands. It is made of mucus with separate cells embedded in it. It has only relatively recently been discovered. It grows on rubble in shallow areas protected from waves, and comes and goes. It may respond to nutrients.

#### Red algae (Rhodophyta)

 Red algae usually are red colored, but some may be dark or brownish. Green algae, brown algae and golden algae can be reliably placed in their groups based on color. Most red algae are red or pink, but not all.

 Probably the most important red algae are coralline algae. The most common kind of them is encrusting, and it looks like purple or pink paint on rock. It is very much a live plant, but it produces hard calcium underneath it, and is an important contributor to reef building. Although most are purple, pink, or red, a few can be tan, brown, or yellow. Encrusting coralline algae are relatively smooth, hard, and not slimy. Corals always are spiny and feel rough like sandpaper to the touch (unless they are very fleshy, in which case they are slimy). They are never ever smooth and not slimy. Coralline algae needs to have a clean surface. They don't grow well in sediment, so they are rare in areas with lots of sediment, and they don't grow well with other algae growing on them, so there is less of it when turf or macroalgae is common. Actually, if you look under turf or macroalgae, you may find some of it, so that is not a totally strict requirement. But it is a good indicator of low sediment. It also grows well in water motion, probably because that helps clean sediment off of it. So it is most common near or on the reef crest, and usually there is less in deeper water, and less further back on the reef flat, or particularly in a pool. Some species grow well in low light, and are found under overhangs or under macroalgae. Some species can be distinguished by their color and pattern of lumping, but others can't.

 There are some red algae that look a lot like coralline algae, but technically speaking are not "non-geniculate coralline algae" which is a taxonomic category. One such genus is Peyssonnelia, which often makes flat shelves of various sizes. Some are bright red, form small shelves and grow in shade. A particularly large species forms brown shelves or whorls in the light at mid-depths or deep on reef slopes, and is very abundant some places. It looks like brown algae. If you bend the shelf, it will bend just so far, then suddenly break. It is partly calcified. Peyssonnelia is technically not coralline algae, but it is a calcareous algae. It is also not encrusting, it is a shelf or bracket, or could be called foliose. Note that although it looks brown, it is actually a red alga.

 There is a species of red algae that is also calcified, which forms feathery branching structures that are flexible enough to bend back and forth with the wave surge. They are generally found in shaded locations from shallow to mid depths, and can be abundant in places. It is Cheilosporum spectabile. It is a branching, articulated, coralline algae. It doesn't seem to be any problem.

 There are also red algae that are not at all calcareous. One is Galaxaura, or Dichotomaria marginata, which forms flexible pink flat small branching structures. It forms clumps that wave in the surge. It looks quite similar to Dictyota, but is pink, the color seems to be reliable for identification. There are others as well, but they all seem to be pretty rare.

#### Diatoms

 Diatoms are single cells that form a pillbox shaped shell around them made of silica glass. Most are phytoplankton, floating around in the water. But a few live stuck to objects. Rarely, a coral may have a yellowish area, which may be diatoms living on the surface of the coral. The following photo may be an example.

![](_page_43_Picture_2.jpeg)

Fire coral, Millepora, with yellow on the right that may be a coating of diatoms.

 This completes the algae proper, including the macroalgae. It is good to keep in mind that all algae compete with corals for space. Macroalgae can overgrow and shade coral, making it hard for coral. Macroalgae also usually have nasty chemicals in them, and some release some of those chemicals. If they are close to corals, those chemicals can damage the coral. Macroalgae can also mechanically damage coral, when waves move them back and forth, they may drag hard parts across the coral surfaces, abrading the tissue. And lastly, it has also been discovered that macroalgae can harbor coral diseases, and if they abrade the coral tissue can pass the disease on to the coral. Large mature corals are best able to defend themselves, but the newly settled coral recruits and juveniles are the most vulnerable to these things. So the first effect of too much macroalgae may be to shut down coral recruitment, and after that to start killing adult corals.

 In a healthy reef, the competition between algae and coral is not a terrible thing, and corals are very good at defending themselves. But if the reef is not healthy, with too many nutrients, too few herbivores, or sick coral from too high temperatures or too much disease, then the corals can loose the battle and the algae can take over.

#### Cyanobacteria

 Cyanobacteria can photosynthesize like algae, but they are actually not algae, even though people used to call them "blue-green algae." They are actually a kind of bacteria. The cells usually grow in strings, and they also secrete mucus, which helps hold strings of cells to each other. The result is very soft, stringy masses, which are slimy and often fall apart at the touch. Some of them can fix nitrogen that can be used as nutrients as well. They are said to be very sensitive to nutrients, so they can be indicators of nutrient runoff. They are a variety of colors, such as grey, black, yellow, orange, and red. Most we see are stringy, but some make more solid gelatinous blobs. Sometimes they are seen to have oxygen bubbles in the mucus, and often the sway back and forth with the wave surge. On Rose Atoll, a sunken longliner was surrounded by a large area of black cyanobacteria. Apparently the iron stimulated their growth. Iron is a micronutrient, and sea water is usually deficient in it, so adding iron can stimulate the growth of plankton or in this case, cyanobacteria. Now that the longliner has been removed, the cyanobacteria there are decreasing, though some remains. There are also a variety of other wrecks

Further, the wrecks surrounded by black cyanobacteria seem to only be on atolls, while wrecks on high islands don't have the black area. It may be that high islands release enough iron that cyanobacteria already have all they need so adding more does not stimulate them. In any case, it appears that black cyanobacteria might be an indicator for iron on atolls.

#### Seagrass

 Seagrass are flowering plants, descendants of land flowering plants that moved into the ocean. We have two very similar species that are so similar to each other you will never tell them apart in the water. Both are in genus Halophila, both have small oval leaves less than an inch long. #### Sponges: Porifera

 Most macroinvertebrates in American Samoa seem to be relatively small, and/or rare, or hidden. There are a few that are fairly common, including a couple of sponges. The diversity of sponges seems pretty good, but most are small and uncommon to rare. The most common sponge on reef slopes is an orange lump, which is in genus Stylissa.

 Sponges are filter feeders which have two sets of holes, small holes to take water in, and larger holes to put it back out. The small holes are usually too small to see. The larger holes usually are irregular in size and placement, and there are often quite a few. Sponges are among the oldest and simplest of all animals, and have a construction quite different from all other organisms. They have no nerves or muscle, so they are incapable of moving. If you touch it, the holes will not contract. These things distinguish it from sea squirts, which will be the next group we look at. Sponge taxonomy is based mainly on the fine details of the little glass spicules embedded in their tissue, as well as the protein fibers that make the body leathery and tough. Sponge taxonomy is even more difficult than coral taxonomy, so often we don't know what the name of a species is. However, when they are alive, many are quite distinctive and not difficult to distinguish. Sponges are usually flexible and somewhat leathery.

 Because sponges are filter feeders, their abundance and size can be an indicator of plankton abundance, and thus of nutrients. However, their populations may be influenced or even controlled by fish that feed on them and other animals that can feed on them, such as some nudibranchs. Many sponges are full of toxic chemicals, so only a relatively small number of fish and nudibranchs can eat them. Some angelfish can eat sponges, and hawksbill turtles eat sponges. Although sponges are small and uncommon most places here, there is an area with abundant sponges, some of which are large, reported by Paul Brown from deep water (200+ feet) in the harbor. This makes sense because the inner harbor often has green water from phytoplankton, and another bioindicator, small oysters just below the water line, are a good bioindicator of plankton in the harbor since they are abundant in the harbor but rare outside.

 One group of sponges that have been recommended as a bioindicator species are the boring sponges. These are sponges that bore into reef rock and coral skeletons. Some species are visible as small spots of sponge about 1/8 inch diameter sticking out of holes in reef rock. Some of the spots of sponge are openings that release water. When they bore into reef rock or coral skeletons, they etch out and remove tiny chips of calcium, which then come flying out the water that comes out the sponge opening. In reefs with lots of boring sponges (like in Florida), the reefs can be blanketed with a dusting of very fine white calcium dust which is these chips. There is one species that is well known from the Great Barrier Reef that is coal black and produces irregular smooth patches on

reef rock. We have black patches among the coralline algae, but if you touch them they are rock hard, not the leathery feel of a sponge, because they are algae not sponge. I have never ever seen a single boring sponge here. However, they may be hiding in the skeletons of coral, especially dead coral. I plan to search for them. But the low number of visible ones indicates low levels of plankton and thus nutrients in the water. There is also no sign of the dusting of white chips from boring sponges.

 The bright orange sponge Stylissa massa is probably our most common sponge. It lives on reef slopes.

#### Sea squirts

 Sea squirts can also be called tunicates or ascidians. Unlike sponges, they are complex animals with a nervous system, muscles, a heart and circulatory system, etc. Some are solitary individuals, and some form colonies of individuals. Solitary species have exactly two holes in an individual, one on the top and one on the side. Water goes in one opening and out the other, and inside there is a filter basket that filters out food, so they are filter feeders like sponges. If you touch them, the holes quickly close, which is very different from sponges. Their shapes are not irregular, and the hole sizes are not irregular. In colonial species there are usually two sizes of openings, large openings where water comes out, and small openings where water goes in. There are many small openings for each large opening. Each individual in the colony has its own small opening, but a group of them share the large opening. The sizes of the holes are uniform, and often they are arranged spatially in a very regular pattern. Colonial species have a larva that looks like a tadpole. The tail has a nerve cord and a notochord (which elasmobranches have, and other vertebrates like us have briefly early in our development). The head end has a mouth, a filter basket, and gill slits. So they are thought to have a common ancestor with vertebrates, and are placed with vertebrates in Phylum Chordata. The common ancestor was a very very long time ago (perhaps 500 million years or more). We have a black species which is solitary, but I think I've only seen it in the harbor.

 Because sea squirts are filter feeders, they can indicate plankton and thus nutrients. Like sponges, they are often filled with lots of toxic substances and thus only a few things can eat them. Some species have symbiotic algae and so don't need plankton and so their abundance may not indicate plankton. Those species are likely to be thin and encrusting and found only in the light. Diplosoma similis may be such a species.

#### Snails (gastropods)

 Snails usually have coiled shells. Here is a picture of a cone shell. Cone shells have cone-shaped shells. They are predators. They have modified the normal snail tongue, which is a ribbon with teeth on it. They enlarged a few teeth and coiled them, making tubes with sharp points, which are used as harpoons and syringe needles, to impale their prey and inject venom into them. Most cone shells eat worms, a few eat other molluscs and a few eat fish. Snails are slow-moving, so they have a major problem with fish. That is, if they stick a fish with their harpoon-tooth, the fish will dart off, and they will never find it and never get to eat it. So, they have to stop the fish before it can escape. They do that with super-fast acting, super-powerful chemicals in their venom. The chemicals are proteins, and they are super specific to nerve cells. A few of them can kill humans, so unless you know which ones are dangerous, it is best never to pick one up. Unless you see it is occupied by a hermit crab, then it is safe. There are about 600 species of cone shells, all in one genus, Conus, the largest single genus of animals.

#### Palolo worms (Palolo viridis)

Palolo worms are famous worms that have a mass spawning once a year and are avidly caught and eaten in the Samoas. They live in holes in the reef and are not seen except during spawning. During the year, they grow extra segments on their tail end, which are called the "epitoke". The extra segments are filled with either eggs or sperm. Each segment has a pair of paddles so it can swim. It also has an eye so it can swim upwards towards light. They spawn each year in October or November. They spawn seven days after the full moon. If that date is close to the end of October or the beginning of November, they will all spawn on one night. If that day is in the middle of October and November, spawning will be split between those two nights. They spawn at about 1 am on Tutuila, and at other times on other islands. When they spawn, the worm backs up enough that the reproductive section that is their tail section is out of the burrow. The tail end breaks off and swims upward and the rest of the worm goes back down the hole. There are separate sexes, one is tan and the other light blue. They swim in circles and then all of a sudden one of them explodes, releasing all their eggs or sperm and that sets the others off and they all explode. People try to catch them before they explode. They can be eaten raw or cooked, often with scrambled eggs. They are a delicacy. The spawning timing depends on several biological clocks, one is the time of year, another the time in the lunar cycle, and the third is the day-night cycle. The spawning is also well known in Tonga, Fiji, Vanuatu, and Indonesia, but most famous in the Samoas.

#### Crustaceans

 Crustaceans are arthropods, like insects, spiders and scorpions. Crustaceans like other arthropods have a shell on the outside which is made of chitin, and in crustaceans can be strengthened with calcium on the inside. Crustaceans living on coral reefs include lobsters, crabs, shrimp, and barnacles.

#### Indicator Summary

Indicators of plankton and thus nutrients:

Filter feeders which do not have symbiotic algae

Sponges (uncommon and small), especially boring sponges (not seen)

Sea squirts (uncommon and small)

Clams- burrowing (rare)

Feather duster or Christmas tree worms (rare)

Feather stars (rare here and hidden)

Basket stars (the author has not yet seen them here, but they are nocturnal)

Brightly colored seafans (rare)

Black corals (rare) Brightly colored soft corals (rare)

All of these are uncommon or rare here, except the oysters at the water line in the harbor.

Bioerosion is when living organisms erode away the calcium of the reef and/or corals. If bioerosion is greater than the amount of calcium deposited by corals and algae, the reef can erode instead of build. Bioerosion in natural, but if there is too much that can be a problem. Some of the species that are filter feeders are also bioeroders. There are two functional groups that can be distinguished, surface bioeroders and subsurface. Boring sponges and urchins are the only two that have been reported to be problems in other places.

#### Water Clarity:

Water clarity is easily measured by visibility distance. Water clarity is a pretty good indicator- the clearer the water the better the conditions for reefs and the murkier the water the worse. Sediment in water coming out of streams during and right after heavy rainfalls is very easy to see, and the darkness of the brown water and the extent of the area are clues to how much of a problem there is. So silt or sediment directly affect visibility and the brown color is a good indicator of the cause, sediment runoff from land. Nutrients can't be seen in water, they don't affect the visibility. The main nutrients are nitrogen in the form of nitrates, and phosphorus in the form of phosphates, plus perhaps iron as a trace element. If water has low visibility and the water is green, the cause is phytoplankton, and phytoplankton grow when nutrients are high enough. They need both nitrates and phosphates, so it is possible to have a lot of one and none of the other and not have a plankton bloom. Green water with low visibility is a very strong indicator of a plankton bloom which in turn is a strong indicator of high levels of nutrients in the water. Green water with low visibility is virtually always found only near shore and almost always only in bays or places where the water exchange is limited. In bays, the water doesn't get flushed, so nutrients that wash into the bay build up. So there are two causes to green water in a bay, one is nutrient runoff from land, and the other is lack of flushing. Lack of flushing is natural, it comes with the bay shape, and there isn't anything we can do about it. Nutrient runoff comes from people (as well as smaller amounts that are natural). But the same amount of nutrient runoff along an open coast will not produce a plankton bloom while it will in a bay with low flushing. Bays are just more sensitive because of the lack of flushing.

#### Fishing:

Giant clams, octopus, and some sea cucumbers could be indicators of fishing pressure, but it is hard to know what the natural populations would be without fishing. Octopus are very fast-growing and so populations can recover fast. In places with commercial sea cucumber fisheries that export to Asia, sea cucumbers have often been quickly fished out and may not recover.

The relationship between boring clams and plankton has been documented, there is a positive correlation with the abundance of clams higher where plankton is more abundant. There is even a way to monitor clams and other things that live on corals, as a way to measure that.

Keep in mind that no indicator is perfect, all indicator species can be influenced by things other than what they are said to indicate. For instance, sponges might be uncommon here because the Pacific has lots of fish that eat them. Mind you, a place like the Philippines or Indonesia has even more species that can eat sponges, and they have lots more than we have, some of which are large. But they are archipelagos with lots of high islands, many larger islands, all with nutrient runoff. It could be that macroinvertebrates are uncommon on Pacific Islands because there isn't enough plankton for their larvae to eat. That itself would be an indicator. But that wouldn't explain why most of the macroinvertebrates are small. Plus, fish have larvae that eat plankton too, and we have lots of them.

### Cnidaria

 The Cnidaria includes not only the hard corals, but several related groups, some of which look a bit like hard corals. We'll look at the different related groups before starting on the hard corals themselves.

#### Black Corals

 Black corals are rare here within safe diving depths. The only ones I have seen are the whip-like forms, but there are probably more bushy forms in deeper water somewhere. They form a single whip growing up from the bottom, in relatively low light, so down deeper on the slope. They are often green, though can be lighter colors, or they may just look lighter in photos. The whip has a central stiffening rod, which is surrounded by tissue, and the polyps are embedded in the tissue. Small tentacles extend from the polyps embedded in the tissue. Each polyp has exactly 6 tentacles. The hard skeleton at the base of some bushy species are the ones carved into jewelry some places. The scientific name for the group, Antipatharia, comes from an ancient belief that it could cure disease, but there is no evidence for that. There are two genera of the whip like species, one has tentacles on only one side, the other on both sides. The only similar thing here is the one gorgonian that forms branching colonies that are thicker rods, and has complete polyps extending, so each polyp has a column with a ring of tentacles at the top. Black corals have no zooxanthellae, they are filter feeders and so could be an indicator of plankton and thus nutrients.

#### Zoanthids

 Zoanthids form colonies of small polyps which might look a bit like corals, but they have no hard skeleton. Polyps are anywhere from maybe 1/8 inch up to about ½ inch in diameter, and have tentacles around the outer edge but not in the center of the polyp. The polyps are all connected to each other in a colony, but you can't always see the connections. There are three genera here which are easy to tell apart, but the species within those genera are either not worked out or not possible for us to distinguish. They may seem similar to corallimorphs, but corallimorphs have larger polyps, 3 inches or larger in diameter, and their polyps have tentacles all over the oral disc of the polyp. The zoanthids we see all have zooxanthellae, though some kinds live on the surfaces of sponges elsewhere and don't have zooxanthellae.

 A fairly common zoanthid on reef flats is Palythoa. Palythoa forms a thick cushion, with the polyps embedded in it, and not projecting. So you see a thick cushion that is a yellow, brown, or cream color, and which has lots of rings on it. Each ring is a ring of tentacles. So the connection between polyps which is the cushion they are embedded in is obvious. It is also on slopes but much rarer there. It is highly toxic, though touching it shouldn't produce any problems, just don't eat it.

#### Corallimorphs

 Corallimorphs are polyps that are usually found in clusters. They are actually separate polyps, though they are often close enough together that you can't tell whether they are connected like zoanthids, or not. Their polyps are larger than zoanthids, with the smallest species having polyps about 2-3 inches diameter, and the largest species having polyps up to about 1 foot in diameter. Corallimorph polyps have tentacles all over the oral disc (top) of their polyps.

 The most common corallimorph in shallow water and on reef slopes is Rhodactis (matu-malu). It has light brown polyps about 2-3 inches in diameter, and commonly covers dead rock, to which it is very tightly stuck. It can, however, either sting and kill some corals, or grow right over and attach to living corals. Some corals can sting it. It can make small patches a few feet in diameter or much larger patches. It is traditionally eaten, and has to be boiled first or it is toxic. Commonly the rock it is on is removed with it and the rock with them on it boiled together. In Palmyra, a sunken longliner has had a patch of it grow around it and expand to the point it now covers about one million square meters. So it would appear that iron may stimulate it. There is a fairly big patch in Tafeu bay on the north side, even though there are no people in that watershed. Our basalt rock has some natural iron- some parts of it are orange from the iron, and in some places cracks in the rock have streaks of orange going down from them, where water containing iron drains. So there is some natural iron going into the water. The corallimorphs we see all have zooxanthellae, though in some places some solitary species come out at night and don't have zooxanthellae (they look like anemones).

![](_page_171_Picture_1.jpeg)

Rhodactis

 A rare corallimorph here is Discosoma, which has smoother polyps with only tiny tentacles. The polyps can be elongated with several mouths. They can be uniform brown, or a light purple with short green tentacles, and they are found in groups like the other corallimorphs. It is on reef slopes.

 Anemonies are closely related to corallimorphs and scleractinian corals. There are several anemones in American Samoa which are large and have clownfish or anemonefish in them. They are not very common, and they are shown in most identification books.

 There is also one small species of anemone which is not in many ID books (except the book on American Samoa, where it is misidentified as a corallimorph). This species occurs in groups, and can be called a colonial anemone, though it is really in clonal groups of genetically identical separate individuals produced by fission, not a colony of connected polyps as in a coral. ### Octocorals: Gorgonians

 Gorgonians have a stiffening rod that is surrounded by tissue, and polyps extend from that tissue. If the polyps are extended, you can see each has a column and a ring of tentacles at the end. Each polyp always has exactly 8 tentacles, and each tentacle has side branches, but those are usually too small to see underwater. The stiffening rods make it possible for gorgonians to often have the shape of whips, bushes, or sea fans. They are uncommon here. When the polyps are retracted the branches have a smooth surface, otherwise they are fuzzy with polyps. Species with zooxanthellae are out in the sun and dull colored while species without zooxanthellae are in lower light and can be quite brightly colored. Their tissue contains tiny granules or spines called sclerites which are made of calcium, and it is the shape of these that the taxonomy is based on. Gorgonians and soft corals are two different shapes that used to be considered two different things, but there are some forms that have been discovered that are intermediates between them, so while they are two easily distinguishable shapes underwater and thus useful terms, they are no longer taxonomic terms.

 Rumphella forms bushy colonies up to 2-3 feet tall. They are light brown, and branches are about ¼-1/2 inch thick. They are on the reef slope and are pretty rare here. They commonly sway back and forth in the current. They have zooxanthellae. When the polyps are retracted the branches are smooth.

#### Soft corals

 Soft corals form soft fleshy colonies usually attached to hard substrate, but which may have ridges, fingers, or branches extending from them. The colonies have polyps embedded in them that can extend or contract. The polyps have exactly eight tentacles, so they are placed in the Octocorals along with the gorgonians. The colonies do not make a rigid skeleton like hard corals, but they do produce calcium "sclerites" or little granules or spines in their tissue. In some colonies these granules or spines can be seen in the nearly clear tissue, and may stick out so they can also be felt as sharp points. In some species, especially in the genus Sinularia, the sclerites may be much more numerous near the base of the colony and be deposited underneath the soft coral, which the soft coral cements together as a hard round smooth base that it is attached to. A few make so many sclerites that the base becomes quite tall and may even be branching, reaching as much as 6 feet tall or more. This material is called spiculite and is quite hard, it serves much like the skeleton of a hard coral. There used to be a lot more soft corals in Pago Pago harbor than now, and one portion of the reef in the harbor is even made of this material that they left behind. The taxonomy is primarily based on the microscopic details of the sclerites, and while some identification to genera of the live animals is possible, it is rarely possible to identify them to species.

 Cladiella is a common soft coral on the outer reef slopes. It forms small colonies usually just a few inches in diameter and maybe an inch or two tall, which are made of stubby fingers. Colonies are usually brown, but if you touch them they quickly turn white. This is caused by the brown polyps pulling into the white tissue of the finger. Other kinds of soft coral do not change color so dramatically when you touch them. They have zooxanthellae.

#### Hard Corals

 Hard corals form hard skeletons, and usually the tissue is only a very thin layer on the outside of the hard skeleton. There are three major groups, blue coral, fire corals, and Scleractinia.

#### Blue Coral

 Blue coral is actually in the same group (Octocorals) as the soft corals and gorgonians, as it has exactly 8 tentacles. The tissue deposits some iron salts in the skeleton which turn it a dark blue. Living colonies are often a blue-grey, and if the polyps are extended a white fuzz may be visible. I have never seen it around Tutuila, but it is fairly common in the pools on Ofu, and is uncommon on the slopes of Ofu and Olosega. Olosega is the eastern most limit of its geographic range which extends west to the Red Sea. It has zooxanthellae.

![](_page_198_Picture_4.jpeg)

Blue coral, Heliopora coerulea

#### Fire Coral, Millepora

 Fire coral is actually in a separate class called "hydrozoa" which alternate between polyps and medusae (jellyfish). For fire corals, the polyps are what build the hard skeleton. The polyps are tiny. The word "Millepora" means thousands of pores, which refers to the tiny holes in the skeleton which the polyps sit in (one polyp in each tiny hole). If a fire coral is backlit (so sun behind it) sometimes you can see tiny white hairs extending from the coral. Those are the polyps. There are actually two kinds, one kind is short and fatter and has a mouth, and the other kind is longer and thinner and has no mouth but has more stingers. Fire corals are called that because they can produce a fiery sting on tender skin. You can touch them with the tips of your fingers where the skin is thick and feel nothing. Their stings are not dangerous and generally don't even produce welts or a rash, but they do get your attention. Millepora colonies have zooxanthellae. We have four species, at least one of which is fairly common.

 The most common fire coral in American Samoa produces an encrusting brown or yellow-brown base, from which paddles grow upward. The paddles can be a variety of shapes and sizes, usually similar on one colony but differing between colonies. They are fairly thick, up to about ½ inch thick. In some cases they are long enough to be curtainlike. The encrusting base is usually bumpy and the paddle sides may be as well. It can be common on outer reef flats and moderately common to uncommon on reef slopes.

#### Scleractinian Corals

 These are the corals that most people think of as "corals." They have six tentacles or multiples of six tentacles, so they are in the Hexacoral group, along with black corals, corallimorphs, zoanthids, and anemonies. Those that build reefs have zooxanthellae, and they form a hard, solid, calcium skeleton underneath the living tissue. There are about 831 species currently known around the world, and we have at least 250 species, perhaps as many as 400.

 Scleractinian corals have polyps, and most reef building species are colonies of many polyps. A few are single polyps, and referred to as "solitary" corals while most have many polyps and are referred to as "colonial." Actually, about half of the species in the world do not have zooxanthellae, and most of those live in the dark and only a few build larger structures you might call reefs. Polyps vary from less than 1/16th inch (1 mm) in diameter to about 30 cm in diameter. Mushroom corals are single polyps and some species can reach about 30 cm in diameter, they are the largest coral polyps.

 Species of scleractinia are usually not easy to distinguish but in recent years books have been published making it possible to distinguish many of them. Genera are easier to distinguish, although a few genera are hard to distinguish. Coral taxonomy is almost exclusively based on the skeleton, both the overall shape and the details under the microscope, and everything in between. But for identification, we can use anything that is helpful, including color. Color is often highly variable, but sometimes it is consistent and helpful.

 A polyp is a soft tissue structure with a mouth surrounded by tentacles and a hollow body filled with water. The body wall is very simple, with just 3 layers of cells. The body wall extends into the central cavity in the form of curtains called "mesenteries." Coral polyps sit in "polyp cups" called "corallites." Actually, the whole polyp cup structure is called a "corallite" but if you are talking about the inside that is called a "calice" (rhymes with "Alice.") A corallite has ridges extending into the central space, and those ridges are called "septa." Septa often continue on the outside of the corallite as "costae." The details of the shape of the corallites, the spaces between corallites, the septa, the costae, the granules and spines on the septa and costae, the number and sizes of septa and costae, and any spines between corallites are all taxonomic features along with colony shapes, used to distinguish species.

 In live corals, you can't see the skeleton directly, but because the tissue is usually thin, you can see some of the shapes of the corallites through the tissue. Those are all cues that can help in identifying live corals.

 I will provide identifications for some of the most common corals on our reefs. I'm working on a coral field guidebook that will provide identification for many more. But it will be most helpful to you, I believe, to begin with some of the most common.

#### Lifeforms

 Lifeforms are colony shapes that are easier than genera and species to learn and often easy to identify. The following is the basic system.

Massive- rounded, solid, usually a hemisphere, any size or weight (not the usual meaning of massive meaning heavy or large).

Branching- has branches

Encrusting- a thin layer on the hard substrate, almost like paint

Foliose- flattened or leaf-like. Some are near-horizontal plates, others form whorls, some form vertical plates or blades.

Submassive: may look massive, but actually has polyps separated by deep cracks and the polyps are actually out at the ends of branches that are close together.

Mushroom: looks like an upsidedown mushroom cap, round with radiating ridges that look like mushroom gills.

Acropora- the largest genus of corals, which dominates many reefs, and which are almost always branching. They have one polyp at the end of each branch, and different polyps on the sides of branches, unlike other corals. Acropora have some of their own lifeforms.

Acropora branching

Acropora staghorn- branches that look rather like deer antlers. It is a type of Acropora branching, but there are many other branching shapes that are much more bushy.

Acropora table- the colony grows in the form of a large flat surface, held up off the substrate by a single column or pedestal. Some have the pedestal in the center, others off to the side.

Acropora digitate- colonies that have short branches which do not themselves branch (very much).

Lifeforms are very easy to learn. The down side is that often you can see a colony that seems intermediate between two lifeforms. Also, lifeforms do not correspond to species. There are many species in each lifeform, and some species can have more than one lifeform. Lifeforms just help you get started identifying a species of coral. The other thing is that lifeforms may be functional groups to some extent. So for instance, branching corals are more easily broken than massive corals so they are more subject to hurricanes, but they also tend to grow faster, and corals in deep water may be foliose to gather more light, and lifeforms on the reef crest may be sturdy to withstand waves, and branching corals may bleach more easily than massive, and so on. So lifeforms can actually be very useful ecological information.

#### Common corals: back reef pools

Staghorns are species of Acropora that are shaped like deer antlers. Because they are Acropora, they have one polyp or corallite on the end of every branch.

Colonies are about 6 inches to a foot in diameter, and branches are about a half inch in diameter and fairly close together. The bumps covering the branches are about 1/8 to 1/16 inch diameter. There are other Pocillopora species that look fairly similar, but are less common. It's a very good start to learn to ID the genus Pocillopora.

#### Montipora grisea

 The most common coral on reef slopes is Montipora grisea, which is encrusting.. Montipora grisea has tiny polyps and tiny bumps or spines on it. Montipora grisea is about like coarse sandpaper. Most of it is brown, but sometimes you can find bright green or purple colonies.

#### Porites rus

 Porites rus is the second most common coral on the reef slope. It is also in pools but is not very common in most pools.  Pavona varians is the third most common species on the reef slopes. It is fairly small, brown, often between other things or a bit recessed, so it is not as obvious as some other corals. #### Mycedium

 Mycedium forms series of thin plates in deeper water, about 45 feet deep and deeper. The plates commonly overlap like shingles. The surface is very bumpy and rough. It is grey. It is mostly from Faga'alu to the southeast section of the island.

#### Isopora crateriformis

 Isopora crateriformis forms encrusting colonies which if they are on a slope may have a plate lower edge. In a few colonies there can be multiple lower plate edges like shingles. Colonies are up to about a foot and a half diameter, and are a reddish-brown, sometimes with green centers on the corallites. The surface is covered with small tubular corallites. In the past, Isopora was considered a sub-genus of Acropora, and this species was called Acropora (Isopora) crateriformis. It appears to be more common in American Samoa than elsewhere in the Indo-Pacific.

#### Fungia fungites

 Fungia forms corals that are single polyps, the largest of all coral polyps, up to about one foot diameter. They are not attached. They are commonly called "mushroom corals" for their resemblance to an overturned mushroom cap. Fungia fungites is one of the prettiest, and is probably seen in the pools more than slopes. But it and other Fungia are also on the slopes. Wave action can carry them down the slope, so in some places like Fagatele and the mouth of Vatia Bay, there are lots of them down at the bottom of the slope. Fungia fungites is usually either a yellow-green with tentacles extended, or purple without tentacles extended. Other Fungia are almost all brown. A slit in the center is where the mouth is, and the radiating ridges are the septa.

#### Acropora hyacinthus

 Acropora hyacinthus is a table coral. When a larva settles, it grows an encrusting colony about 6 inches diameter, then the center starts growing upward and forms a column about 3-4 inches tall (sometimes taller), then it starts growing outward from the top of the column, forming a flat circular surface that is about an inch thick, mainly composed of little vertical branchlets growing up from horizontal branches. It is most common on upper reef slopes and can dominate some areas like Leone and the middle area of Fagatele Bay at about 10-15 feet deep. It is usually a brown or reddish brown, but some colonies have a slight greenish tint. They can form multiple layers, and in an area where there are lots of colonies close together, the circular table shape may be obscured by the many overlapping irregularly shaped tables.

 Diploastrea heliopora forms some of the largest colonies here. They are commonly large encrusting sheets on the slope. They are a dull green color usually, and they have fairly large corallites about half an inch diameter, which look a little like volcanoes.

#### Lobophyllia hemprichii

 Lobophyllia hemprichii dominates medium depths on reef slopes in some areas of the SE coast of the island. Colonies are often anything from 1-10 feet or more in diameter. Colonies are called "submassive" in lifeform because they appear to be massive, but actually the surface is composed of the blunt ends of branches that are close together. The outer edge of the polyp is raised and rounded, the center is depressed. There is a very thin crack between adjacent polyps. The crack can go several feet down into the colony since it separates branches. The branches can only be seen if the colony is broken.

#### Massive Porites

 There are several species of Porites which make massive colonies, so rounded solid colonies of any size from small to very large. Often their surface is a bit lumpy. # DAMAGE

 There are quite a variety of things that can damage coral reefs. Some of these, like predation and disease, can be quite hard to tell apart. Further, we don't know all of the different kinds of damage yet, so for instance we don't know which fish species are making some of the bite marks on corals, and we don't know the causes of most coral

diseases and we discover coral diseases we have not seen before fairly frequently. Four major kinds of damage we will cover here are mechanical damage, bleaching, predation, and disease.

#### Mechanical Damage

#### Breakage

 A variety of things can break coral, such as hurricanes, tsunamis, people, turtles, and fish. Newly broken corals usually have broken surfaces which are very white. The white is the bare coral skeleton.

![](_page_244_Picture_4.jpeg)

Coral (Porites cylindrica) broken by a hurricane. The tips of branches are light colored but rounded, while the broken surfaces are flat and very white. The white broken surface actually has live tissue in tiny pores all through the skeleton. In several weeks to a few months these white broken surfaces will heal over and grow new rounded tips and resume healthy growth. Larger branches will have live tissue in pores in the outer part of the broken surface, but not on the interior of the branch, and typically you can see the different color in the center of the broken surface. The outer part with tissue will heal quickly, but the center part will grow algae. The live surface may then slowly grow over the dead area, if it isn't too large

![](_page_245_Picture_0.jpeg)

Broken branches healing after the tsunami. Notice the branch at the top with algae (dark) growing in the center.

 Sea turtles break coral as they dig in finger coral or staghorns, apparently to find sponges to eat. Sponges may grow down on the dead bases of branches. Sponges are the favorite thing for Hawksbill turtles to eat, and most of our turtles are Hawksbills. They typically make a hole where they have broken the branches, and scatter the broken branches around the hole. Often I see turtles with their head in a hole in staghorns, hard at work.

![](_page_246_Picture_1.jpeg)

A hole in finger coral dug out by a sea turtle.

#### Sediment

 Sediment that settles on coral can kill it. In the photo below, the grey areas on the left are sediment sitting on the surface of a Porites rus colony, and the white areas on the right are where sediment has been swept off, revealing the dead white coral skeleton underneath. Sediment accumulates in dips in the coral. If the sediment is new, when it is removed the coral surface may be black, because the dying coral tissue went anoxic and that produces black which would stink of rot if it were above water.

![](_page_247_Picture_2.jpeg)

Porites rus with sediment and areas killed by sediment.

After the tsunami, some table corals had sediment on top of them, which killed areas which were usually in the center of the slightly concave table top.

![](_page_248_Picture_1.jpeg)

Acropora hyacinthus tables with areas on them killed by sediment from the tsunami.

#### Low Tides

 Low tides kill coral on reef flats whenever there is an unusually low tide. The corals that stick up too far get exposed to air and the hot sun for too long and die. This is natural and is like mowing your grass. Since only the highest corals are killed and the lower corals continue to grow, deeper spots can get filled in while the high spots get mowed down. This results in the reef flat being very flat at about the low tide level.

![](_page_249_Picture_2.jpeg)

Coral killed by low tide. You can see almost exactly how far down the water went (the water probably went a little lower, but the line is where the exposure was too much for the coral to survive). The dead coral was quickly colonized by turf or filamentous algae.

![](_page_250_Picture_0.jpeg)

A coral a few days after one of the lowet tides. The yellow at top is new filamentous algae. The white below is stressed coral which survived but has not yet recovered. It has lost zooxanthellae. This can be called "bleaching" even though it was not caused by high temperatures, but rather by exposure to air. Bleaching is a response to a variety of stressors, including high temperatures, low temperatures, low salinity, and exposure to air.

Coral that is killed by low tides is initially white and hard to tell from bleaching. Within a day or two it starts to get a very light yellow color, maybe from diatoms (a kind of single celled algae). If it is bright sun and you swish your hands past them and they are dead, the water motion will knock oxygen bubbles off of the algae and you will see a bunch of oxygen bubbles rise. Within 3-4 days of being killed the coral will be green with turf, and within a week or so they will be very dark green-brown or even black with dense turf.

![](_page_251_Picture_1.jpeg)

Acropora aspera on a reef flat with branches killed by low tide and now covered with turf. Acropora aspera grows higher than other corals on the reef flat, and then gets killed by low tides.

![](_page_252_Picture_0.jpeg)

This staghorn colony shows healthy coral, bleached stress coral, new yellow filamentous (turf) algae, and older black turf. The healthy is in the lower left, white stressed is above it, and yellow and black are at the top.

### BLEACHING

 Corals expel their zooxanthellae single-cell algae if the water gets too hot for too long. Once the algae have been expelled, the tissue is clear, because all the color was in the zooxanthellae. Then you can see right through the thin clear tissue to the skeleton, which is white, and which reflects the bright light. So coral that have been too hot turn white. At that point they are still alive. If the temperature was just 1-2 degrees C above the normal summer maximum, the coral may survive and get its zooxanthellae and color back. If it is more than 2 degrees C above, then the coral may die. If temperatures are high, the coral can completely bleach and everything turns white.

![](_page_253_Picture_2.jpeg)

Bleached staghorn at Amouli.

.

A few corals have pigments in the coral cells, so that when they bleach they turn colors which are usually described as being neon colors that look a bit unreal. Typical colors are purple, pink, and sometimes yellow.

![](_page_254_Picture_1.jpeg)

Bleached corals with neon colors. Notice the gradual fading from healthy brown to bleached white.

Some years we have small amounts of bleaching on the reef slopes, with scattered corals that bleach. Some kinds of corals like Pocillopora and Montastrea curta may bleach while other corals don't bleach. This is a very mild bleaching event, and typically only parts of corals are bleached. Usually the upper parts of corals bleach first, the parts exposed to light. We know that intense light increases bleaching.

![](_page_255_Picture_1.jpeg)

A Pocillopora colony with only the upper branch ends bleached.

![](_page_256_Picture_0.jpeg)

Staghorn corals with only their upper surfaces bleached. Sometimes the upper surfaces can bleach badly enough to die while the lower surfaces still have color.

Some of our corals turn blue when they bleach, like this Acropora colony.

![](_page_257_Picture_1.jpeg)

Bleached digitate Acropora colony.

Often bleaching is very patchy on a colony, with some parts white and other parts still with color. When this happens, the boundary between the white areas and colored areas are not sharp, the color diminishes gradually across the boundary area. This is one way to tell bleaching from most kinds of predation. In predation, usually there is a sharp boundary between colored (living) and white (dead) areas.

### PREDATION

 There are lots of predators on coral reefs, including predators that eat coral. Most do only minor damage to the coral, but a few can do a great deal of damage (Rotjan and Lewis, 2008).

#### Crown-of-thorns starfish

 Crown-of-thorns starfish eat coral by everting their stomach out their mouth so it is inside out, and putting the stomach on live coral and digesting the coral tissue. The starfish is soft and bendable and can bend around the tips of branching coral. On branching coral it eats the tips of branches but often can't reach the bases of branches. The dead area they leave is usually in patches about the size of the starfish or slightly smaller, which is the size the stomach can digest at one time. The starfish often hides in holes during the day and comes out at night to feed, but sometimes is out feeding during the day. It may not go far when it finishes eating one coral to go to the next coral it eats. So sometimes you can find a trail of white dead corals that it has left behind, and even figure out which direction it is going by which end of the trail has bright white coral (newly dead) and which has grey or green color (older dead coral with turf growing on it). When you see such a trail but don't see the starfish, looking in nearby holes may reveal the starfish that ate those corals.

![](_page_259_Picture_2.jpeg)

A crown-of-thorns starfish.

![](_page_260_Picture_0.jpeg)

A crown-of-thorns starfish (Acanthaster cf. solaris) eating coral. This species has long been called Acanthaster planci, but genetics show that Pacific and Indian Ocean populations are different species. Acanthaster planci is in the Indian Ocean and purple, and the Pacific species is Acanthaster cf. solaris.

![](_page_261_Picture_0.jpeg)

A crown-of-thorns starfish with a trail of dead coral it has eaten behind it.

Crown-of-thorns starfish are normally fairly rare, but occasionally there are millions of them in an "outbreak" which can eat and kill vast numbers of corals. Such an outbreak occurred here in 1978 and ate over 90% of all the corals. The primary cause is a plankton bloom right when the starfish spawn, which provides food for the larvae so huge numbers survive. One starfish can produce a million eggs. The plankton bloom may be produced by nutrients washed off of land by an unusually large rainstorm following a dry period when nutrients built up in the soil. The outbreaks around the Indo-Pacific were so bad in the 60's and 70's that some people thought they would destroy most Indo-Pacific reefs.

#### Drupella snails

 There are some small snails in the genus Drupella that also have had outbreaks some places like Japan and the Philippines. Drupella snails usually feed in a group, and can completely remove all the tissue from an area on a coral. Their small shells are bumpy and are usually covered with patches of coralline algae. They like to eat Acropora. They don't eat table corals very often, but when they do they are fairly easy to find, since they can't hide and they will be found on the border between the live and dead coral.

![](_page_263_Picture_2.jpeg)

Most often, Drupella prefer to eat staghorns, maybe in part because they can hide better in the branches. They typically are hiding in a cluster down in the branches of the staghorn. They prefer to eat the coral down in the branches, and usually leave the branch tips alive. So the pattern is the opposite from crown-of-thorns. They are often hidden well enough down in the branches that you have to search to find them.

#### Parasitic snails

 Some snails feed on coral tissue, but take so little that the coral can heal as fast as the snail eats it. The result is that the snails are in effect parasites. Commonly they hide in cracks in massive corals, or deep between branches in branching corals like Pocillopora. They have smooth shells, and may be fairly hard to spot. They are usually cap-shaped like limpets.

#### Damselfish

 Some damselfish are algae farmers. They bite off the live polyps of the coral, let the turf or filamentous algae grow, and may even weed out undesirable species of algae. Because they bite off the coral polyps, they can be considered coral predators. Some, particularly a large grey species with a black spot at the rear base of their dorsal fin, prefer staghorns. They can be seen popping in and out from between the staghorn branches, and make popping sounds. Their dense turf gardens can be seen on the base of the staghorn branches. When they clear some more staghorn surface, it produces a bright white area between the dense turf and the live brown coral. Typically the boundary between the live brown coral and the bare white skeleton is very sharp.

There are smaller black damsels that have territories on the reef slope. Their territory is typically all black compared to the purple coralline algae outside it. They, or another species, sometimes have their territory up next to the side of a massive Porites colony. Then they start eating the polyps off the side of the Porites colony and growing turf on it too. It might look like disease, but the turf is a clue that it is a damselfish garden. # DISEASE

 Disease is defined in medicine as anything causing ill health. So it not only includes infectious diseases like colds and the flu and bacterial infections, it also includes diseases like diabetes, high blood pressure, heart attacks, strokes and cancer, which are disorders but are not infectious.

 Diseases affect all organisms, they are part of nature. For an ecosystem like a coral reef, we must expect some amount of disease to be present. That is natural and not a problem for us, but sometimes there are disease outbreaks and many organisms can die, and that is a problem.

 There are quite a few diseases of corals and coralline algae, and we discover new ones every once in a while. But for most diseases, we don't know the cause yet, and we aren't even sure which are separate diseases.

### Coralline algae diseases

#### CLOD

 CLOD stands for "Coralline Lethal Orange Disease." It is bright orange bacteria that kill and digest coralline algae. Soon after it was discovered, there seemed to be an outbreak of it in Fiji, with a lot of coralline algae killed. Since then there doesn't seem to have been much of a problem, and while we have it here and some places it is pretty common, the coralline algae grows back as fast as the CLOD can kill it, and there is a lot more coralline algae than CLOD (some places you have to search to find the CLOD). Moderately common.

![](_page_280_Picture_3.jpeg)

CLOD forms a bright orange band on coralline algae, with live coralline algae on one side, and dead white coralline algae behind it. The bacteria must be able to move, here you can see places where it is streaming together off the dead areas toward the living areas. Remember that bacteria are amazingly small, much much smaller than our cells. But some bacteria can swim with a flagellum. The bacteria have not been identified.

#### Coralline Black Fungal Disease

A second disease on coralline algae is a black fungus. Originally it was only reported from American Samoa, but now it has also been found in the Line Islands at Palmyra and Kingman by Bernardo Vargas (Vargas-Angel, 2010). I've also recorded it in western Samoa. It is less common than CLOD, but not really rare. I have only seen it on the slope. It is much harder to spot than CLOD, which stands out with the bright color. Thierry Work and Greta Aeby called this "Coralline Black Fungal Disease" in their 2006 set of disease identification cards for American Samoa. It has now been identified as Malassezia restricta by Williams et al, 2014.

![](_page_281_Picture_2.jpeg)

#### White patch

Sometimes you can see a white patch on coralline algae. In this photo, the graded green shows the growth of filamentous algae or turf after the coralline algae was killed. So the white is most recently killed. The gradation from green to white shows that the dead patch is expanding across the coralline algae. Doesn't seem very common.

![](_page_282_Picture_2.jpeg)

 On rare instances you may see an orange line along the edge of the white patch. This may be caused by a disease, but the orange line may simply be where the coralline alga has lost some of its pigments but not all. Orange or pink lines can be found on coralline algae in other circumstances, like interactions with live coral.

![](_page_283_Picture_1.jpeg)

#### Target Patterns

Under overhangs on the slope, you can often find a "target" pattern. It is never out in the light, it is always in a shaded location. The cause of this is not known, it could possibly even be a predator, though it seems more likely to be a disease. Samples turn up no sign of an infectious disease organism. Uncommon but widespread.

There is also a disease which forms a thin soft yellow mat between the live coralline algae and the dead. It has been reported to be cyanobacteria. I call it a "microbial mat." It is quite rare, always on the slope, usually shaded.

### Coral Disease Signs and Lesions

 It is not possible to see diseases in living corals on the reefs, only signs of disease. Symptoms are what patients report themselves, so symptoms are only available in humans. Signs do not have a simple, one to one relationship with diseases. In human diseases, a few signs or symptoms are diagnostic, like the little red spots that indicate Chicken Pox (or measles). Other symptoms, like fever, are very non-specific, many different diseases can cause fever. A fever indicates there is likely to be a problem, but tells you very little about what the problem is. The things we view in the field are signs, and for most signs we don't know what the disease is. Further, some diseases appear to be found only in one host species, while other diseases appear to be found in several host species. Many human diseases are found only in humans, but some like rabies and bird flu, have primary animal hosts but can be transmitted to humans as well. Further, while some diseases are communicable and caused by infectious agents like bacteria, viruses, fungi, or protozoa, other diseases are not, such as metabolic diseases like diabetes, high blood pressure, stroke, high cholesterol, heart attacks, cancer and poisoning.

 In corals, what we see are signs, not diseases. In most cases we don't know what the disease is that causes the symptoms, we don't even know whether it is caused by an infectious disease agent, let alone what that agent might be. Further, we don't know if different symptoms are caused by different diseases, or whether similar-looking symptoms in different coral species are caused by the same or similar diseases or different diseases. We do know now that signs that appear the same in a species are sometimes produced by different disease agents. This is actually somewhat similar to disease diagnosis in humans, so, for instance the signs and symptoms of colds and flu and the new coronavirus are very similar, and without a lab test we often don't know which one we have. There are 4 or 5 serotypes of dengue, 4 different species of malaria, and several different causes of pneumonia, and so on.

 Because coral diseases have been so difficult to figure out, we need to be careful to not go beyond the information that we have, and presume that distinctive looking signs are caused by a disease that is different from that which causes other signs, or that the disease is caused by an infective agent. It may be, or it may not be, we don't know. We may have our suspicions, but those are essentially hypotheses that need to be tested. For these reasons, the things in the photographs presented here are referred to as signs. In some cases they may correspond to distinct diseases, in others they probably don't, and at this time we really don't know in most cases.

 Diseases are defined as anything that reduces functioning. So coral bleaching, caused by stresses like high temperature, low temperature, fresh water, and low light can technically be called a disease. Predation can also be technically considered disease. Mechanical damage from things like hurricanes, tsunamis, construction projects, and people breaking coral can also be technically considered disease. The same can be said

about sediment damage and competition with algae caused by nutrients. These have all been covered in sections above. The signs considered in this section are those that are not caused by the things listed above alone, and may be caused by infectious agents (though in most cases we don't know that for sure). Some things like bleaching and predation, can sometimes look quite similar to some of the diseases listed below, but hopefully this guide will help distinguish them. Although all we can see are just signs, which don't correspond with diseases very well, we are pretty sure that these symptoms indicate a reduction of functioning and therefore indicate diseases. What we don't know is what causes them, whether it is an infectious agent or agents, or something else. Even if there is an infectious agent, it may strike in part because the coral was weakened by some kind of stress like high temperatures. Disease outbreaks following bleaching events have been documented, so there is direct evidence for this. Some microbes or parasites live on or in a host without causing any problems until something changes such as an increase in stress or a break in surface protection, which allows the microbe or parasite to become an active disease agent.

 There are a variety of coral disease signs in American Samoa, and periodically the author finds another that we've never seen before. The causes of most are complete mysteries, but the causes of a few have been determined. The more scientific approach is to simply describe the appearance of the disease symptoms and let the scientists try to figure out what it is. However, I'm going to go out on a limb and show you some things that seem to be distinct, even though we don't know what causes them. Keep in mind that there are more things that look like disease than I am presenting here, but these are the most distinctive or common. Also, keep in mind that some of the things I am presenting as separate disease symptoms may actually be the same disease, so there may be several different "white tissue loss diseases" or some of them may be the same thing, we really don't know at this stage.

 As imperfect and incomplete as recognizing disease signs on the reef is, it is a first basic step that needs to be at least begun. We need to be able to recognize diseases in order to monitor them and study them. I think photos are critical, "a picture is worth a thousand words" as words don't always convey every important aspect of diseases (or host species for that matter). Looking through these disease photos can help you recognize things that appear to be distinct in the field, a first, if limited, step in studying coral diseases.

 At this point, the coral diseases in American Samoa look as diverse or more so than they do in the Caribbean, which has had much more study, and where a bewildering variety of coral diseases have appeared in recent years. Dr.'s Aeby and Work speak of three general groups of symptoms in Pacific coral diseases, "tissue loss," "discoloration," and "growth anomalies." Tissue loss means that tissue is dying and then is lost from the colony. When tissue loss occurs, often an area of coral appears white, so another name might be "white diseases" or "white signs." Coral tissue often doesn't die when there is discoloration, but it can. Many growth anomalies can be seen without mortality, but if you search long enough you will probably find at least a few with some tissue loss. So the three groups may not be completely separable, but they provide a good start to organizing these diverse phenomena. We will cover tissue loss, then discoloration, and last growth anomalies.

#### Tissue Loss

#### Acropora White Syndrome (AWS)

 This is a disease symptom that is well enough known to have a widely used name ("white syndrome"). It is also one of the most lethal diseases we know of in the Pacific, and most widespread. It is most common and easy to observe on table corals. Typically, there is a white area, which on one side has a sharp dividing line between it and living healthy looking coral. On the other side, the white fades into grey or green as turf grows on the dead coral skeleton. The width of the white probably tells you roughly how fast the disease is killing the coral, skeleton doesn't stay white long before turf algae colonizes it, so if the white band is wide it is moving fast, if it is narrow it is moving slowly. Typically once it starts on a table it is likely to keep going around it until it has killed the entire colony, but in an outbreak at Vatia after a hurricane, the white band stopped on many colonies before killing the whole colony. It is fairly common. It was reported to be caused by a bacterium, Vibrio sp., in the Marshall Islands. This was called "Acropora White Syndrome" in the 2006 disease card set for American Samoa by Thierry Work and Greta Aeby. It is relatively common. The author has 67 photos of this disease, including 36 on tables six on digitate colonies and two on staghorns.

#### Pocillopora White Syndrome

 Pocillopora colonies commonly get disease symptoms which have a white band that moves over each branch killing it. Sometimes yellow algae can be growing below the band where it has killed the branch some time ago. This disease is certainly lethal for branches, and it may be lethal for most entire colonies too. This colony shows a typical pattern with some live branches, some newly dead white branches, and darker branches that have been dead longer. The slopes have many live, many diseased, and many intact dead colonies of Pocillopora. This appears to be a disease that has a steady moderate abundance and causes a lot of Pocillopora mortality. It and white syndrome seem to be the most common coral diseases in American Samoa. This has been found on P. eydouxi, P. verrucosa, P. meandrina, P. setichelli and P. damicornis in American Samoa, so it appears to be on all species of Pocillopora here. It appears to be more common on P. eydouxi and less common on P. damicornis. Common.

#### Echinopora lamellosa Black Line Tissue Loss

 There are disease signs that are quite common on the species Echinopora lamellosa. This species produces clumps of thin nearly horizontal grey plates. Some clumps in Fagatele Bay were quite large before the tsunami, I would guess 15 feet or more in diameter and perhaps as tall as 6 feet. Many of the plates in some of the mounds had this disease. I also found it on much smaller colonies at Fagafue Bay on the north side. It appears that the disease begins as a black spot, and as it spreads it has a thin black line at the edge against the live coral, and a lighter area inside. Large diseased areas can have coralline algae growing in them. It appears that this disease spreads very slowly as there is never a white dead band behind the black band. I have never seen it on any other species of coral.

# Discoloration:

There are several sets of symptoms in corals in which the coral is discolored, yet tissue loss (tissue death) may not occur. In some cases, we may just not have seen tissue loss yet.

### Massive Porites White Patch Syndrome

 Some massive Porites have white patches on them. The white patches have sharp edges to them, they are irregular in size and shape, they are usually fairly small but not tiny (so about 1-3 inches across is common). They are never in pairs on bumps like fish bite marks, even though some people have suggested they are fish bites. Close examination shows no evidence of scraping. Plus, they are not uniform in size or shape and don't have the shape of fish bites. They are clearly not fish bites. Within the white patch, the coral is alive and appears as though it is healthy, tentacles can be extended. I have only once or twice seen any part of any of the patches appear to be dead (see below). The patches show no sign of becoming larger and spreading over the coral to engulf it. So, the disease, whatever it is, is very rarely if ever lethal. A few patches have the same appearance, except the patch is not white, rather it is some intermediate color between white and the color of healthy areas of the colony. That could (could!) be a patch recovering, or a patch getting the disease. Colonies with these white patches are uncommon but not rare. Lawrence et al (2015) named this "white patch syndrome" and that convention is followed here. They studied it in eastern Australia.

![](_page_391_Picture_0.jpeg)

Some colonies have only a few white patches, others have many. One colony with lots can be right up against another that has none. Notice there is some variety in the size and shape of patches. The author first saw this in the Philippines. Raymundo et al. (2005) report it from the Philippines, and call it "white blotch." Work and Rameyer (2005) show a picture of it from American Samoa and call it a "focal discoloration." They confirmed that zooxanthellae were absent, but otherwise tissue appeared normal in almost all samples. Thierry Work and Greta Aeby in their 2006 set of disease identification cards, called this "Porites focal bleaching." It certainly involves the loss of zooxanthellae like mass coral bleaching, but is surely not caused by high temperatures like mass coral bleaching, so I prefer not to call it bleaching to avoid confusion with bleaching caused by high temperatures. These white patches have only been observed on massive Porites, not on any other Porites so far. However, there is one photo of a white patch on Acropora gemmifera that looks like it could be one of these white patches, although it was called a "white tumor (growth anomaly)" (Walsh et al. 2014).

 Rarely, there is mottling inside a patch, and the mottling is often at the polyp level, one polyp has color, another doesn't. In a few colonies, the patches are all small. There are also sometimes patches that have edges that are graded in color, typically only on some sides of the patch, and may resemble a comet.

#### Growth Anomalies

#### Convex Growth Anomalies Produced by Accelerated Skeletal Extension

 These are unusual growths on corals, often lumps. Some people have called them tumors or neoplasms. They appear to be uncontrolled growth like a tumor or cancer, however, the growth (lump) is actually of the skeleton, not the tissues. At the very least, it is a loss of control of the calcification process. A more neutral term is "growth anomalies" meaning just unusual growths. Some colonies have lots of them, some have a few, and most colonies don't have any. I have seen a table (Acropora hyacinthus) with 140 on it. They are easiest to recognize on table corals, and indeed they may be most common on table corals, but they are also on other corals. They are often lighter colored than the rest of the table coral. Sometimes they have little branchlets, but the branchlets are not uniform and point in all sorts of directions. Others have what look like corallites, and others are just bumpy with no recognizable corallites or branches. Some places (like parts of Fagatele Bay and Vatia Bay) have quite a few of these, other places have very few. They are most often seen on Acropora hyacinthus, but then that is a common coral. They are the most common type of growth anomalies.

#### Fill-in and "Ice Cream Tumors"

#### Acropora Digitate Basal Fill-in Growth Anomalies

 There are also growth anomalies that seem to fill in between branches on digitate Acropora colonies. They fill in around the base of branches, and can surround more and more of the height of the branches as the growth gets thicker. Typically the filling in area is very smooth and white. Greta Aeby and Terry Work informally call them "ice cream tumors." They are uncommon.

![](_page_551_Picture_3.jpeg)

The white area between the branchlets here is hard coral with living tissue on it which lacks zooxanthellae and thus appears white. Pictures of this type of growth anomaly are presented in Work et al. (2008).

![](_page_552_Picture_0.jpeg)

An "ice cream" growth anomaly on a digitate Acropora colony. There is a dead area in the white growth area. #### Massive Porites Growth Anomalies

 Massive Porites colonies have growth anomalies that form smooth rounded low domes that are taller than the small lumps on normal areas. They retain normal coloration. They can be quite large, up to at least about 6 feet across. It may be that death occurs on white anomalies because the white tissue cannot produce its own food and if it can't get enough food from surrounding healthy tissue it starves to death. Growths that retain zooxanthellae and color may not die because they retain a sufficient food source. These are fairly common some places like the Ofu pools.

![](_page_575_Picture_2.jpeg)

On this colony, only the rippled surface is normal growth. All the smooth areas are growth anomalies. This colony appears to have several, all of the same type. Such growths have been called "hyperplasias." Thierry Work and Greta Aeby called this "Porites Growth Anomaly" in their 2006 set of disease identification cards for American Samoa.

This cyanobacteria is a bit unusual. The filaments are strong and do not break easily, and it is not slimy. It grows on live staghorn corals, and kills the branches. Some white newly dead branches can be seen here. After the coral is all dead, the black filaments disappear. Some clumps of filaments can be seen drifting in the water, and it seems likely that they get caught on the rough staghorn branches and then start to grow there and spread. Small amounts have been found on other corals like finger coral in the area of a larger growth on staghorn. It appears they do not catch as well on other species that have less rough branches. This has been seen in the airport pool and on the reef flat at Faga'alu.

![](_page_633_Picture_2.jpeg)

Cyanobacteria on staghorn coral

 One last note. It appears that quite a few of these diseases are quite specific in what species of coral they attack. Even those like White Syndrome that can attack several or many coral species, attack a few much more often than the others.

#### Pigmentation Response

Porites corals commonly produce pink color when tissues are irritated or damaged. Here, a vermatid snail embedded in a massive Porites colony puts out mucus streams and then pulls them in to eat whatever it catches. Notice that the upper parts of bumps and ridges on the coral are bluish, and all pink spots are found on the tops of bumps or ridges. The mucus often catches on the coral, and it likely pulls in coral mucus as well as its own. The irritation causes the Porites to pull in the polyps and expose the blue color of this colony, and if it is intense enough, the tissue swells and becomes pink. This appears to be a general response to irritation or damage of many kinds, and not an infectious disease (Willis and Dinsdale, 2004).

![](_page_634_Picture_2.jpeg)

Here is a photo of where the macroalgae in the top of the photo has rubbed against a Porites coral in the bottom part of the coral, abrading the coral tissue and producing a white area. Around the white area is an area where the Porites tissue is inflated and pink. This is the pigmentation response. Sometimes when it forms a narrow pink line around an injured area, people have thought that it was a disease, "pink line disease." But it is just a response to any of several kinds of irritation or damage.

### The Author

Douglas Fenner

B.A. Reed College, USA, 1971 Ph.D. University of Pennsylvania, USA, 1976

Born in Michigan, USA, the author lived in a variety of places in the states, including Florida during his high school years, which stimulated an interest in tropical marine life. During his years at Reed College in Portland, Oregon, he was introduced to biology, including invertebrate biology, studied sea urchin tube feet and respiration for his thesis and spent two summers in Hawaii studying fish behavior with his professors. Once he graduated, he attended the summer invertebrate zoology course at the Marine Biological Laboratory at Woods Hole, Massachusetts and then another summer was a course assistant for that course. Snorkeling trips to the Caribbean (including to Jamaica just before Hurricane Allen) during graduate school at the University of Pennsylvania were followed by scuba trips to the Caribbean. His coral reef research and publications began with surveys and descriptions of reefs in the Caribbean, including Cozumel, Roatan, Cayman Brac, Little Cayman, and St. Lucia. It became clear that to do transects you need to know your corals, and existing guides were inadequate, so Caribbean coral identification and taxonomy were next to be studied. By this time the author lived in Seattle, Washington. Then the author began to study corals in Hawaii, which led to his identification book for Hawaiian corals. He then worked in the Philippines for two years, learning many coral species in that area of high diversity. This was followed by six years of working with Dr. "Charlie" J.E.N. Veron at the Australian Institute of Marine Science on the "Coral ID" electronic key to corals of the world. At that time, the author began to be invited to study and record corals during Rapid Assessment Programs in a variety of places around the Indo-Pacific. In November 2003, the author began work at the Dept. Marine & Wildlife Resources, in American Samoa. He began working on coral reef monitoring there a year later and continued with that and continued to make trips to study corals around the Indo-Pacific. Currently, the author has studied coral at 14 islands in the Caribbean and 14 areas of the Indo-Pacific, plus southern Italy. He is the author of 17 book chapters and 46 peer-reviewed articles in scientific journals. He has worked as a contractor for NOAA NMFS Protected Species on the threatened coral species since 2013. That work has taken him around the Pacific each year to study corals and teach people how to identify corals. That effort includes photographing corals, writing field guides, and building "practice modules" for teaching coral ID and people to practice with. He currently works on describing new coral species and diseases and a variety of other coral reef topics. He continues to be based in American Samoa.

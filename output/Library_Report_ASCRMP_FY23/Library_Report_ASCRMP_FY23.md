#### <span id="page-3-0"></span>*Introduction*

The American Samoa Coral Reef Monitoring Program (ASCRMP) has two main goals: (1) to track the distribution and abundance of reef organisms in American Samoa and (2) to supply pertinent information to environmental managers and decision-makers for the management of coral reefs in the Territory. The primary aim of ASCRMP is to record changes occurring at monitoring locations throughout the Territory, which include variations in species composition, population sizes, and the physical structure of the reefs. By documenting these changes, the program seeks to improve the understanding of reef ecosystem dynamics and the underlying factors influencing them.

The long-term monitoring program by the American Samoan Governor's Coral Reef Advisory Group (CRAG) aims to address management questions at three spatial scales: site level, primary reef type level, and island level. Previous research by Houk et al. (2013) identified significant biological differences in benthic, coral, and fish assemblages between TutuilA's north and south sides, influenced by environmental factors such as wave energy and geology. Methods have been refined to maintain comparability with island-wide surveys conducted from 2003 to 2016, enhancing the understanding of spatial and temporal trends in coral and fish assemblages by integrating past and modern data.

CRAG selected eleven sites around Tutuila Island for long-term monitoring, representing various reef types and incorporating federal, Territory, and community-based Marine Protected Areas (MPAs). Among these, three sites (Sailele, Fagasa, and Fagaalu (CFMP updated since 2024) are part of community-based MPAs under the Department of Marine and Wildlife Resources' Community-based Fisheries Management Program. In comparison, two sites (Fagatele and Tafeu) are designated as federal MPAs.

The selection process for monitoring sites considered both watershed sizes and human usage. The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) categorizes watersheds into four classes: "pristine," "minimal," "intermediate," and "extensive" (DiDonato, 2004). Among the 11 monitoring sites, three are classified as "extensive" (Leone, Nu'uuli, and Faga'alu), three as "intermediate" (Amaua, Aoa, and Fagasa), one as "medium" (Vatia), and three as "pristine" (Fagatele, Tafeu, and Fagamalo). In 2007, an additional site, A'asu (Massacre Bay), was introduced to improve the geographical balance between the north-south and east-west orientations, ensuring even representation across the four quadrants of Tutuila (NW, NE, SE, and SW). Since 2017, standardized methods have been utilized at these sites to monitor benthic substrates, coral, and fish, allowing for the detection of changes in coral reef health over time. This monitoring builds on data from earlier programs that started in 2003 and has led to numerous publications that illustrate the connections between watersheds and reefs while addressing the impacts of climate change.

### <span id="page-3-1"></span>*Climate Change*

Changes in composition and resilience of the coral reef ecosystem due to disturbance are observed globally. In American Samoa, reef recovery is site specific with some sites being dominated by stress-tolerant coral species (Graham et al., 2020), which can modify the reef structure. Several recent climate-change-associated heat-stress events have occurred in American Samoa (Figure 1). The first significant heat-stress event was observed in Tutuila in 2015. Degree Heating Weeks (DHW) indicates accumulated heat stress and acts as a metric for assessing thermal stress in the reef environment. The first event was characterized by heat stress exceeding six degree heating weeks (DHW), or a threshold considered to be associated with mild-to-moderate bleaching. Subsequently, in 2017, a significant event occurred with DHW near eight, which is the threshold for major bleaching. In 2020, a DHW of 7.5 was reported. None of those events had been extreme. However, since November/December 2023, along with the warmer season and El Nino event, high record temperatures have been registered in American Samoa. By April 2024, we had reached an Alert Level 2 heat stress with 8-degree heating weeks (DHW), or a threshold considered to be associated with bleaching and mortality likely (Figure 2). With the longest cumulative heat stress on record for the archipelago, NOAA confirmed the fourth global mass coral bleaching event, marking the second such event in the past decade. It is unknown how sequential moderate DHW events may impact reefs. Within this report, we attribute the rise in DHW intensity to variations in coral cover and the ratio of calcifying-to-uncalcifying benthic substrates, trends that are furthered within.

#### <span id="page-6-0"></span>*Program Overview and Personnel History*

In 2005, the Department of Marine and Wildlife Resources (DMWR) launched the American Samoa Coral Reef Monitoring Program (ASCRMP) to conduct long-term monitoring of coral reefs in American Samoa, with support from NOAA's Coral Reef Conservation Program (CRCP). The ASCRMP is the first initiative to monitor representative reefs annually throughout the region, beginning with Tutuila and expanding to the Manu'a Islands, and, if possible, Rose Atoll and Swains Atoll.

The project was initially managed by two staff members funded through the ASCRMP: a Benthic Ecologist and a Coral Reef Monitoring Fish Ecologist. Lesley Whaylen held the position of Fish Ecologist starting in 2006, followed by Benjamin Carroll, who served from 2007 until August 2012. Dr. Domingo Ochavillo, the Chief Fisheries Biologist at DMWR, took over the responsibilities until February 2013. Subsequently, Alice Lawrence, the DMWR Fisheries Supervisor, and Saolotoga Tofaeono, the DMWR Fisheries Technician, filled the position part-time.

From February 2014 until 2021, Alice Lawrence was appointed full-time for the remainder of the grant award period. Dr. Doug Fenner served as the Benthic Ecologist from 2006 until August 2013, after which Dr. Mareike Sudek took over from May 2014 to January 2017. In June 2018, the role of Benthic Ecologist (now known as the Coral Reef Ecologist) was filled by an off-island contractor, Georgia Coward, who served until 2021.

A Coral Reef Monitoring Technician role was created and initially filled by local graduate Motusaga Vaeoso from May 2015 to 2018. The training for this position included SCUBA certification, monitoring techniques, and skills for identifying fish, benthic organisms, and invertebrates. From February 2018 to 2023, the Monitoring Technician position was occupied by local graduate Natasha Ripley, who earned a double bachelor's degree from UH Hilo.

Beginning in 2022, CRAG experienced high staff turnover, which delayed projects and monitoring activities. In April 2022, local graduate Fuamai Ativalu Tago took on the role of Coral Reef Fish Ecologist. In August 2023, Andrea Nicte-Ha Muñoz Arroyo was onboarded as the Coral Reef Benthic Ecologist.

#### <span id="page-8-0"></span>*Benthic Surveys*

Until 2014, the line-point intercept method (LPI) was utilized to assess benthic cover. Four 50 m transects were laid out along a depth contour of 8-10 m, and benthic categories were recorded every 50 cm (100 data points/transect). In addition, a roving dive was conducted to record species diversity recorded on the DACOR scale. For more information on these methods, see reports from 2005 to 2013 compiled by Doug Fenner.

In 2015, the methods were altered to increase data quality, add additional data, and make it comparable to data collected by other monitoring programs. The LPI method was changed to photo quadrats to increase the data points collected and decrease the time spent collecting data. Additionally, coral demographic data is collected along the transect to make inferences about size distribution and recruitment.

Two 100-meter tapes are placed end to end along the 30-foot depth contour on the reef slope, divided into six sections, or considered as six replicate transects (0-25m, 30-55m, 60-85m, 90-115m, 120-145m, 150-175m). Each of the six sections is marked with a rebar at the start (0, 30, 60, 90, 120, and 150m). Benthic photos are taken every meter using a 1 m2 monopod (26 photos per section; 156 photos per site). These photos are currently analyzed with CoralNet, a web-based platform using machine learning for coral reef image classification, replacing the previous software used for photo analyses called Coral Reef Point Count or CPCe.

#### <span id="page-9-0"></span>*Coral Surveys*

Coral surveys have been conducted opportunistically when taxonomic expertise has existed within the broader ASCRAG network. While no taxonomic expertise currently exists, we briefly mention protocols. A 1 x 1 m quadrat is randomly placed on the reef every 20m (10 per site). Each coral colony within the quadrat is identified by genus, growth form assigned, and its largest diameter and width measured.

#### <span id="page-9-1"></span>*Coral Bleaching Monitoring*

Coral bleaching monitoring is conducted annually in October / November before the warmer season to survey reefs before a potential bleaching event. Survey sites include twelve reef slope sites (five of which overlap with existing ASCRMP sites), reef flat sites (seven in Tutuila: Sailele, Auto, Onesosopo, Utulei, Gataivai, Nuuli, Amanave and four in Manu'a), and three lagoon pools (Fagaalu, Airport pools and Alofao). At each location, two HOBO® Pendant temperature loggers (www.onsetcomp.com) are retrieved from the previous year and replaced with new loggers. In the past, loggers have been reused by replacing batteries. However, there was a high failure rate and data loss. During 2019, 75% of the new HOBO loggers deployed that were retrieved at the end of the year were flooded, and therefore data was unable to be downloaded. As a result, it was decided to upgrade the HOBO devices to a more robust version. It is slightly more costly but is also used by the National Park of American Samoa's marine team to collect water temperature data at their monitoring sites. This will ensure a more robust data collection method and allow more effective calibration and comparison between CRAG and NPSA datasets.

The approximate depth range of the reef slopes is 30 ft. Two 100 m tapes were laid out end to end on the 30 ft depth contour along the reef slope consisting of six individual transects (0-25 m, 30-55m, 60-85m, 90-115m, 120-145m, 150-175m). The approximate depth range of the reef flats is 3ft-5ft depth and in the pools is 6ft-13ft; Three 25 m transects were laid out parallel to each other with a 10-20 m distance between them. On the slope, the beginning of each transect is permanently marked with a rebar (six rebars total at 0, 30, 60, 90, 120, and 150m). In pools and on reef flats, each transect's beginning and end are marked with a rebar (six rebars total, two rebars/transect at 0 and 25m). For each transect, benthic photos are taken every meter using a 1m long monopod on slopes and a 0.75m monopod on reef flats and in pools (26 photos per transect; a total of 156 images per site on reef slopes and a total of 78 photos per site on reef flats). These photos are then analyzed using CPCe and Coral Net software to obtain coral cover estimates for the site. The coral cover estimates can then be compared to cover data after a bleaching event to assess the impact on the reef assemblage. Two temperature loggers are installed at each site near two stakes using zip ties. The logger (length 5 cm) is attached to an area of the reef (solid rock) away from living coral.

Although not covered by this agreement, during a bleaching event, these additional survey steps would be undertaken as per the "American Samoa Coral Bleaching Response Protocol" (see Table 2): Boat snorkel tow surveys will be conducted to assess the spatial extent and identify critical areas for more detailed surveys. At critical sites, a belt survey will be conducted to obtain data on the percentage of bleached coral and how different genera are affected. Every coral colony within a 1 m wide belt along each transect will be identified to genus and assigned a bleaching severity ranging from 0-4 (0 = no bleaching; 1 = partially bleached; 2 = white; 3=bleached and partly dead; 4 = recently dead; see Marshall & Schuttenberg 2006). In addition, both divers will estimate the general proportion of coral affected by

bleaching for the reef as a whole using a scale from 0-4 (0 = <1% - no bleaching; 1 = 1-10% - mild bleaching; 2=10-50% - moderate bleaching; 3 = 50-90% - high bleaching; 4= >90% - extreme bleaching; see Marshall & Schuttenberg 2006). Additionally, aerial images from the reef flats in different priority sites around the Island have been captured using a drone.

In 2015, the program staff initiated snorkel baseline monitoring surveys on the shallow reef flats (3ft-5ft) and pools on Ofu and Olosega islands to provide information on temporal and spatial trends within the islands. Rose and Swains atolls are monitored when conditions and funds allow.

Different survey schedules are recommended depending on the event classification, habitat types, and available resources. The survey methods (Figure 4) should be organized in a tiered layout, starting with the basic large-scale survey and developing progressively into more intensive small-scale surveys (see Table 2).

#### <span id="page-11-0"></span>*Monitoring Protocols*

Once the Tier 1 survey is completed and resources allow, progress to Tier 2 surveys at priority sites and/or random sites within the island's four quadrants (NE, NW, SE, and SW). If resources allow, surveying the start, middle, and end of the bleaching event is advantageous to collect information on bleaching progression through time. Once Tier 2 surveys are completed and resources allow, progress to Tier 3 surveys at priority sites.

#### <span id="page-11-4"></span>*Fish Surveys*

Until 2013, the fish survey methods included recording all diurnally active, non-cryptic reef fish to species level using belt transects. Six replicate 30 m transects were sampled at each site, with several

passes and widths being used to sample different groups. The first pass was used to sample larger, more mobile species 7.5 m on either side of the transect as the transect is laid out. On the second pass, parrotfish were recorded 5 m on either side of the tape. The third pass surgeonfish were recorded at 2.5 m on either side of the tape. Every other species was recorded at 2.5 m on either side of the tape on the fourth pass, except damselfish, which was recorded on the fifth at 1m on either side. Each fish was counted, and an estimation of length was recorded. The fish surveyor and the benthic surveyor conducted their surveys separately, and at times, the distance between the divers underwater could be up to 150 meters.

More recently, updated fish survey protocols have been tested and adopted between January 2013 and September 2014 due to low statistical power associated with past surveys. Survey methods were designed to increase the statistical power of the data to answer questions at multiple spatial scales and make it more comparable to past data collected by the ASEPA coral reef monitoring program between 2003 and 2016 (Houk et al., 2013), as well as the NOAA CREP protocols (Heenan et al., 2014). The subordinate phase starts at 0 m, and every 20 m, a Stationary Point Count (SPC) is conducted in a 7.5 m radius from the diver for 3 minutes. A total of 12 replicate SPCs are completed along the transect tape. Each SPC is a visually estimated 15 m diameter cylindrical plot extending from the substrate to the limits of vertical visibility.

Each count consists of two components. The first is a 1-minute species enumeration period in which the diver records the taxa of all species observed within their cylinder. At the end of the 1 min period, the diver begins the tallying portion of the count, systematically working through their species listing and recording each fish's number and estimated size (total length, TL, to the nearest cm). The tallying portion is conducted as a series of rapid visual sweeps of the plot, with one species-grouping counted per sweep. To the extent possible, the diver remains at the six centers of their cylinder throughout the count. However, small, generally site-attached, and semi-cryptic species, which tend to be underrepresented in counts made by an observer remaining in the center of a 7.5 m radius cylinder, are left to the end of the tally period. At this time, the observer swims through their plot area, carefully searching for those species. Surveys are not conducted if horizontal visibility is < 7.5 m, i.e., when observers cannot distinguish the edges of their cylinder. Biomass per fish is then calculated using the standard length-weight equation.

The main difficulty encountered in conducting consistent, annual coral reef monitoring has been the maintenance issues associated with the DMWR vessel. In addition, a recent shortage of trained staff since COVID with limited capacity for conducting surveys presents a secondary challenge. Despite these obstacles, the program has been very committed to surveying the 12 priority sites since 2017, and our database has incorporated comparable data since 2003 in some instances from the ASEPA program (Figures 5 & 6). The power of combining programs to capture past data can be seen while looking at our effort graphs that depict the presence of data to understand both status and trends.

#### <span id="page-13-0"></span>*Data Analyses*

The present report provided a trend analysis for all benthic and fish data in the long-term ASCRMP database. We first examined benthic data aggregated by major category groupings (coral, crustose coralline algae, fleshy coralline algae, turf algae, and sand). These generalized graphs depict the overall trends in reef health by comparing calcifying substrates (coral and crustose coralline algae) to others for long periods. Hence, we can evaluate whether reefs have changed in calcification rates and the overall impacts on corals from recent heat-stress events.

Subsequently, we examined coral assemblages across major taxonomic families (Acroporidae, Poritidae, Merulinidae, and others). These families depict significant reef structures (Acroporidae and Merulinidae in particular) and whether stress-tolerant corals have begun to emerge from heat stress events (Porites and other corals to a lesser extent). We examined trends at the island scale, the major reef type scale, and the individual site scale to highlight generalized trends and uniqueness.

Finally, we examined fish assemblages to indicate current status and trends concerning major function groups and dominant species. These analyses help to understand the status of MPA, reef-type trends, and changes associated with heat stress and coral loss. Beyond examining trends, we lastly performed regression analyses to understand the drivers of fish trends. We examined watershed influences (size, population, nitrogen loading from ASEPA data) and indicators of fishing access (wave energy, boat distances from major ports, and road distances from major populations). From these regression plots, we can appreciate potential drivers of the fish populations and understand where management or enforcement needs may exist. Technical details of the regression analyses follow. Regression analyses were performed using R base packages to build multiple regression models. All potential predictors were standardized, and best-fit models were indicated by the highest explanatory power (R2 values) while maintaining normally distributed residuals. These models will continue to be updated as new data emerge.

#### <span id="page-14-0"></span>*Results*

Temporal trends were examined for these same major benthic substrates. For all regions, there was positive reef growth from corals and crustose coralline algae between 2017 and 2019 following the heat stress event in 2015 that impacted American Samoa (Figures 7 and 8). Positive trends were evident until about 2020 when the third heat stress event occurred and diminished calcification. The impacts of climate-induced heat stress were considered moderate to date, with most reef types recovering quickly following disturbances. However, we see limited change through time despite heat stress for wave-protected environments and sandy bays. Here, reef calcification is consistently below the 50% threshold, and the corals and substrates are being reduced to tolerant taxa known to have limited impacts from disturbances. Some sites seem to have maintained the same trend of calcification reported in the last four years, while others show a positive trend (see coral trends below).

Benthic trends were examined across each of the individual sites through time. When reviewing the benthic cover trends across the monitored sites over time, it is observed that most sites exhibited the same trends concerning climate-induced heat stress events noted above (Figures 7, 8, 9, 11). Except for Faga'alu, Fagasa, and Vatia, all sites display values above the 50% line and rapid recovery following heat stress, consistent with reef observations. In contrast, Fagasa and Vatia exhibit net negative growth and demonstrate a significant increase in non-calcifying substrates, such as fleshy crustose coralline algae, cyanobacteria, and sponges. Faga'alu experienced positive crustose coralline algae growth but consistent declines in coral cover until 2020. Nonetheless, in the last four years (2020-2024), this pattern seems to be changing, with a slight increase in coral cover and a negative crustose coralline algae growth at this site (Figure 12).

In contrast to these three sites, the highest percent of coral cover, approximately 50%, included Tafeu, Aunu'u, Fagatele, Leone, and Aoa, according to data collected until 2020. For Aunu'u, this tendency seems to be maintained until 2020, and according to data collected in the last two years (2022-2024), there appears to be an apparent increase (Figure 10). Fagamalo saw an evident decrease in coral cover and crustose coralline algae from 2021 to 2022. Nonetheless, from 2022 to 2024, there seems to be a recovery with a positive pattern in both crustose coralline algae growth and coral cover (Figure 10). On the other hand, for Leone, until 2022, there was an increase in both coral cover and crustose coralline algae. Still, according to the most recent data (2023-2024), this pattern seems to change with an apparent decrease in both variables (Figure 12). Although Amaua and Nu'uuli display greater calcifying substrate and coral

cover in the southern region, the ratio leans more towards calcifying substrate until 2020. Yet, examining the coral species composition on these reefs alongside major benthic trends is crucial to gaining deeper insights into their dynamics.

This event triggered the fourth mass coral bleaching event, with effects still yet to be seen in the next months in American Samoa. #### <span id="page-65-0"></span>Fish Surveys

Fish data were first examined by averaging data for each site and reef type across all years and subsequently analyzed for temporal trends. Fish data averaged across all years enabled a comparison between sites to identify significant differences or similarities in the main components of fish biomass. In the northern region, characterized by low human population densities and isolated villages, Fagamalo and Tafeu exhibit high fish biomass, averaging 7.5 kg/m². Conversely, in the southern area, Nu'uuli (8 kg/m²) stands out, closely followed by Amaua (7 kg/m²) and Leone (6.5 kg/m²). Vatia, however, displays comparatively lower fish biomass at 5 kg/m². Among the wave-protected sites, Aoa and Fagasa demonstrate similar fish biomass levels, averaging 5 kg/m². Interestingly, these trends follow the benthic and coral trends, similarly identifying locations with the highest and lowest fish and corals.

The dominance of small herbivores, including species with estimated sizes at maturity less than 30 cm, is evident across all sites. These include small-bodied snapper, small-bodied acanthurids, and small-bodied parrotfish. Other fish guilds also exhibited low numbers, consistent across all sites.

![](_page_65_Figure_3.jpeg)

Figure 18. Analysis of fish biomass averaged across different time points and sites.

Temporal trends were similar for the north and south reefs between 2008 and 2015, whereby a generally slow decline in the biomass of large-bodied fish was found. However, following climate-inducted heat stress events, fish biomass nearly doubled. Heat stress removes corals and creates an abundance of non-calcifying turf and macroalgae that provide food for small-bodied parrotfish and surgeonfish. These

two fish functional groups were the primary response to climate disturbances between 2015 and 2021. These broad trends have been seen elsewhere across the Pacific so were expected, however, the length of time increased fish biomass remains following heat-stress may be related to reef resilience. Responses to heat stress were greatest for the north and south reef types and limited for the wave-protected reef types (Figure 17). These temporal trends underscore the complex dynamics of fish populations following environmental disturbances such as coral bleaching, where different fish functional groups exhibit diverse trends in biomass changes.

Figures 18 and 19 offer a more site-specific insight into fish trends. Across all north sites, there has been a positive response from heat stress since 2015 and 2016. A positive response from heat stress was noted despite the no-take MPA Fagatele showing relatively low fish biomass compared to other sites. While fish biomass appears to be sufficient to support reef recovery following heat stress for most major reef types, the composition of mainly small-bodied herbivores is a potential indication of fishing pressure across the island, as well as the gradual decline in fish biomass between 2008 and 2015 before heat stress occurred (see regression analyses below).

Lastly, species composition and dominance were investigated to understand fish communities' species richness and complexity. High diversity indicates a balanced ecosystem where various ecological roles are fulfilled, contributing to overall ecosystem health and resilience. Furthermore, species composition is a critical indicator of ecosystem conditions, with certain species acting as proxies for environmental changes or habitat degradation. Figure 20 presents the primary fish species composition within each of the 12 priority sites. Dominant species included mainly small-bodied herbivores such as *Chlorurus japanensis, Chlorurus spilurus, Scarus oviceps, Scarus spitticus, and Ctenochaetus striatus*. There was a limited presence of large-bodied species and tertiary predators. Sites with larger fish appeared to be associated with higher wave energy and were further from the main boat ports. These general observations were furthered in the regression analyses.

#### <span id="page-69-0"></span>*Fish regression analyses with environmental variables*

We calculated a metric previously used to summarize the overall condition of fish assemblages (see Houk et al. 2013). The fish assemblage metric represents the combined values of fish biomass, predator biomass, fish size, and fish diversity (Figure 21). By combining these factors, we generated an overall fish assemblage score that could be examined in relation to environmental factors.

![](_page_69_Picture_3.jpeg)

**Figure 23**. Fish condition scores that were used to evaluate the influence of environmental factors on the fish assemblages.

For both north and south reef types, the condition of fish assemblages was similarly limited by two key metrics that limit fishing access: distance from the main marina at Pago Pago and wave energy. High wave energy associated with trade winds potentially restricts boats and fishers' ability to access sites,

while far distances limit access because of fuel costs. Wave energy was calculated based on long-term 10-year wind records translated to wave heights previously defined by Houk et al. (2013). High wave energy also has the potential to influence benthic assemblages by limiting the development of reef framework for fish habitat. Thus, while wave energy represented a natural factor limiting fish abundance due to habitat and increasing fish assemblage conditions due to limited access, the regression analyses revealed a positive influence of wave energy that was potentially related to fishing access. In addition, distance and fuel costs are known to limit fishing access. Analyses revealed a positive relationship with higher wave energy, further distances from the main harbor of Pago Pago, and better fish assemblage condition scores (Figures 22 and 23). Sita, Masausi, Maloata, and Amouli represent more remote locations with higher wave energy and the highest fish assemblage scores. Notable for the south reefs, Alega, despite being near Pago Pago harbor, exhibited relatively high fish assemblage composition based upon relatively high wave energy. Conversely, Pago Pago harbor experiences low wave energy immediate access and low water quality, potentially resulting in the lowest fish scores.

Wave-protected sites had differing relationships with fish assemblage scores (Figure 24). Two factors influencing wave-protected reefs were nitrogen loading based on previously published ASEPA water quality data (Comoros et al. 2018) and human population density in the adjacent watershed. Interestingly, nitrogen loading positively influenced fish assemblages, potentially contributing to an increasing abundance of small-bodied herbivores. Meanwhile, a high human population served to reduce scores, especially in Aua and Fagasa. High population density correlates with high fishing pressure and low fish assemblage for these areas. In contrast, Alofau, situated farther from the island's central location and with a lower population, appears to experience less fishing pressure but relatively high nitrogen loading. While small herbivores benefitted from high nitrogen loading, ultimately, negative consequences are expected from nitrogen loading to the reef habitat (see Comoros et al. 2018).

#### <span id="page-72-0"></span>*Discussion*

The analysis of benthic cover trends across the monitored sites reveals that most sites exhibit net positive growth. This trend becomes particularly evident when the blue colors representing calcifying substrates and coral cover cross the black dashed line, signifying the 50% threshold. However, certain sites show negative growth, including Vatia in a sandy reef environment, Fagasa in wave-protected areas, Faga'alu in the southern region, and Fagamalo in the north. Despite these findings, most sites demonstrate either stability or recovery of calcifying substrate and coral cover, especially following the major bleaching events in 2015 and 2017 and fluctuations in earlier years. Upon examination of benthic data focusing on coral trends, we can identify the predominant coral types in each site. With shifting reef dynamics, we anticipate gradual changes in coral communities from *Acroporidae* species to *Poritidae* species like *Porites rus*, influenced by local stressors and historical heat stress. Although there has been a decline in *Acroporidae*, a crucial reef-building coral, signs of recovery are noticeable in 2021, suggesting optimism for reef ecosystem resilience. However, given that heat stress events had become more frequent and severe, for instance, the unprecedented heat stress event experienced during 2024 triggered the fourth mass coral bleaching event. It is necessary to continue with the monitoring program and the data collection to fully understand the impacts of such events on the coral reef of American Samoa.

Fish biomass, averaged across various time points and sites, provides valuable insights into long-term trends and ecosystem health, facilitating comparisons between sites and identifying significant differences or similarities in fish biomass dynamics across locations. Small-bodied herbivores, such as the parrotfish species *Chlorurus japanensis*, *Chlorurus spilurus, Scarus oviceps, and Scarus spitticus,* and the surgeonfish *Ctenochaetus striatus,* dominate across all sites, while other fish guilds consistently exhibit low numbers across all sites, in particular large-bodied fish species. This poses a concern regarding the ecological implications of their dominance within these ecosystems. Overall, the study underscores the

importance of monitoring and analyzing fish biomass and species composition trends to enhance our understanding of coral reef ecosystem health and support conservation efforts.

#### <span id="page-73-0"></span>*Applications of the Monitoring Data*

The extensive dataset provided by the ASCRMP program is a valuable resource for understanding the dynamics of coral reef ecosystems. This report examines the response of fish populations to climate change over time, noting variations in sensitivity among different coral and fish species. Regression analysis was employed to investigate the factors driving fishing pressure, particularly focusing on proxies of fishing accessibility.

The ASCRMP dataset analyzes coral reef ecosystems, studying fish and benthic responses to climate change and fishing pressure factors. Results show varied impacts of fishing accessibility on fish assemblage across sites, highlighting the need for customized conservation approaches based on local conditions in American Samoa.

#### <span id="page-73-1"></span>*Recommendation for the Future*

Based on this report's findings, we strongly recommend implementing adaptive management strategies for coral reef conservation in American Samoa. These strategies should address the impacts of climate change on reef ecosystems, including their effects on fish and benthic populations across various sites. Considering the ongoing increase in ocean temperature, acidity, sea level rise, and the presence of local stressors, all of these factors must be considered when developing management plans for villages in the future.

Furthermore, we emphasize the importance of continuous monitoring and research efforts to gain deeper insights into the future impact of climate change on coral reef ecosystems. It is essential to be prepared to respond to major bleaching events promptly and effectively to understand how ecosystem functions may change and potential management interventions. This informed approach will contribute to these invaluable ecosystems' long-term sustainability and resilience.

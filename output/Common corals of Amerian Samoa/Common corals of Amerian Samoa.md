# Common Corals of American Samoa

![](_page_0_Picture_1.jpeg)

The Coral Reef Advisory Group (CRAG) coordinates American Samoa's coral reef management efforts and activities. The group is a collaboration of five local agencies: the Department of Marine and Wildlife Resources (DMWR), the Department of Commerce (DOC; including AS Coastal Management Program & Fagatele Bay National Marine Sanctuary), the American Samoa Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), the American Samoa Community College (ASCC), and the National Park of American Samoa (NPS). Member agencies work together by mutual consensus to manage coral reefs with the vision "to protect and conserve reefs for the benefit of the people of American Samoa, the United States, and the world". The group works toward this vision by implementing strategies to address various areas of coral reef conservation; from education and outreach, policy and enforcement, to scientific research and monitoring.

## Introduction

 American Samoa is part of the Samoan Archipelago near the center of the Pacific Ocean, 2500 miles south of Hawaii. It consists of seven islands, five of which are high, volcanic islands, and two of which are small atolls. American Samoa is lined with fringing coral reefs that harbor a multitude of the marine life.

 Coral reefs are geological structures composed of calcium carbonate rock, made by living organisms. Corals are a major contributor of the calcium carbonate that builds the reef, and form the hard framework that holds it together and provides habitat for many other species in the ecosystem. Coral reefs are the most diverse marine ecosystem known in the world, harboring an estimated million species. Major groups of organisms living on reefs include corals, fish, algae, and many different kinds of invertebrate animals.

 Coral reefs break the power of waves, and protect tropical shorelines from being eroded away. Reef fish provide high-protein food for millions of people around the world. Coral reefs also are ideal for snorkeling and diving, with warm, clear water, and a kaleidoscope of colorful animals and plants. Reef tourism is an important part of the economy in many parts of the world.

#### Reef structure

 In American Samoa the fringing reefs lining the shoreline are divided into four different zones (Fig. 1). In a few areas, pools can be found near the shore that are up to 25 feet deep and called "backreef pools." Along most of the shoreline though, there is hard, nearly flat reef that begins right at the low tide line, called "reef flat." It is the area that people walk out on at low tide and it can be snorkeled at mid to high tide. The outer edge of the reef flat, where the waves break, is called the "reef crest". Beyond the reef crest, the reef slopes down into deep water, hence called "reef slope" or fore reef. It extends from near the very shallow at the reef crest, down to as much as 100 feet deep. At the bottom of the reef slope, it reaches a nearly flat shelf, most of which is covered with sand or rubble and has little or no coral on it.

#### The coral animal

 Corals are closely related to sea anemones, and more distantly related to jellyfish. Thus, they are animals. The basic unit of the body of a coral is called a polyp, which is a hollow bag filled with sea water, with finger-like structures called tentacles in a circle on the top (Fig. 2). In the center of the ring of tentacles is a mouth. Corals, like anemones and jellyfish are predators. They have stingers on the tentacles that can stun small organisms, which are then moved to the mouth and digested. Each polyp sits in its own shallow "cup" called corallite which can vary in size and shape depending on species. Corals are unusual animals because they are modular, meaning they are made up of thousands of repeating units, the polyps, each connected to one another. Hence, corals are also referred to as "coral colonies".

#### Zooxanthellae

 Corals are very unusual among animals, in that their tissues contain tiny, single-celled plants or algae, called "zooxanthellae" (Fig. 3). When sunlight strikes these tiny algae cells, they convert it into food, just like the leaves of plants on land. The algae cells leak most of that food out, which is taken up by the coral polyp, helping the coral to grow. As a result, reef building corals live only in sun-lit, relatively shallow waters, less than 300 feet deep.

#### The coral skeleton

 Corals build a hard calcium carbonate skeleton which is all one piece, unlike our skeleton. They add to it continually throughout their life, so coral reefs are constantly growing. However, that growth is slow in many species. Depending on the coral species, growth rates can range from 0.1 to 4 inches per year. The shape of the skeleton is also very different in different coral species. Some species produce a branching skeleton, others produce a single, mound-like (massive) skeleton, and still others produce a very thin skeleton that covers the bottom like a layer of paint (encrusting), or even a plate-shaped skeleton (Fig. 4). The shape of the coral is the primary way that species are distinguished. But not just the shape of the whole coral colony, but also the details of the corallite (cup) that the polyp sits in, are important to distinguish a species. The color of the coral is usually not very helpful as it can be quite variable within a species.

### Summary

A coral is very unique in that it is an animal that contains tiny algae cells, and produces a rock skeleton. Corals are found throughout the different reef zones and are conspicuous, yet they can be quite difficult to identify to species. There are over 250 coral species in American Samoa alone, and coral colonies even within a species can be variable in their appearance. The species composition in the different reef zones as well as between locations is often variable. However, some common species are relatively easy to distinguish. This guide presents the 22 most common species found in American Samoa, organized by reef zone.

## Common corals: back reef pools

#### Acropora intermedia (Staghorn coral) - Previously referred to as A. nobilis

 Acropora intermedia forms staghorn colonies that look similar to A. muricata and A. pulchra (see below). However, it has thicker branches than the other two species. The tip of the branch is about ½ inch thick and the base of the branch can be up to an inch thick or more. Another distinguishing feature are the corallites lining the sides of the branches. They are short, uniform and have thick walls with angled openings pointing towards the tip of the branch. Big and small corallites are interspersed between each other. It is usually brown but can be tan, and always has a white branch tip. A. intermedia is the least common of the staghorn corals found in backreef pools, and it is also found on reef slopes.

![](_page_6_Picture_3.jpeg)

![](_page_6_Picture_4.jpeg)

#### Acropora muricata (Staghorn coral) - Previously referred to as A. formosa

 Acropora muricata forms staghorn colonies with branches about ½ inch thick that are covered in corallites in the shape of small thin tubes that are sticking out from the branches (in contrast to A. intermedia). It is tan to brown, and it has a purple tip on the end of every branch. The purple tip is a very good distinguishing feature of this species in American Samoa. A. muricata is by far the most common "staghorn coral" in the backreef pools, but it is not found on the reef slopes.

![](_page_7_Picture_2.jpeg)

#### Acropora pulchra (Staghorn coral)

 Acropora pulchra forms staghorn colonies with branches about ½ inch thick (the same size as A. muricata). A distinguishing feature is that it usually has its tentacles extended during the day, giving it a smooth look from a distance, and a fuzzy look closer up. A close look will also reveal a few ear-shaped corallites sticking out between the tentacles. It is usually a little more reddish in color than A. muricata, and has white branch tips. A. pulchra is the second most common "staghorn coral" found in the backreef pools but it is not found on reef slopes.

![](_page_8_Picture_2.jpeg)

![](_page_8_Picture_3.jpeg)

#### Pocillopora damicornis (Cauliflower coral)

 Pocillopora damicornis can reach about 10 inches in diameter and forms small bushy colonies with lots of small branches that are packed close together. The branches appear very irregular and knobby, but can on occasion be farther apart and straighter. Branch tips are always round and are usually a shade of light brown. Colonies and are relatively common in some pools and on the reef crest. It is also found on the reef slope.

![](_page_9_Picture_2.jpeg)

![](_page_9_Picture_3.jpeg)

#### Porites cylindrica (Finger coral)

 Porites cylindrica can form fields in which it is difficult to distinguish between individual colonies. The branches are finger-like and have rounded tips. It is most commonly a grey-brown, but some colonies are yellow-green. It dominates backreef pools along with Acropora muricata but it is also found on the reef slope.

## Common corals: reef flats

#### Acropora aspera

 Acropora aspera colonies are somewhere in between being a small staghorn and being bushy. Branches are usually about ¼ inch in diameter and tend to grow straight upward. Corallites lining the branches are short, thick walled, and have openings angled towards the tip of the branch. It is usually brown and always has white branch tips. It is only found on the reef flat and at low tide clumps of branches commonly stick out of the water.

![](_page_11_Picture_3.jpeg)

![](_page_11_Picture_4.jpeg)

#### Pavona frondifera

 Pavona frondifera forms very crinkly colonies of small vertical plates that have many ridges running down the sides. The ridges grow until they themselves become small plates. Plates turn and twist in many directions and are densely packed together. It is yellow-brown and prefers reef flats and edges of backreef pools.

## Common corals: reef crests

#### Acropora nana

 Acropora nana colonies are usually around 3-6 inches diameter and form small bushes of radiating thin branches. Branches are about the thickness of a pencil, and usually are vertical or radiate from the center. Corallites lining the branches point toward the tip, though some are tubular and project. It is commonly a reddish-brown. This species is usually the most common species on reef crests.

![](_page_13_Picture_3.jpeg)

![](_page_13_Picture_4.jpeg)

#### Millepora platyphylla (Fire coral)

 Millepora platyphylla forms encrusting colonies with thick paddle-shaped columns and plates growing upwards. The paddles can be a variety of shapes and sizes, and are up to about ½ inch thick. The upper edges of the paddles and plates are flat or rounded and the encrusting base is usually bumpy. It is brown or yellow-brown in color. It can be common on outer reef flats, reef crests and moderately common on reef slopes. Fire corals have their name because they can produce a fiery sting on tender skin. Their stings are not dangerous and only cause light pain for a short period of time.

![](_page_14_Picture_2.jpeg)

![](_page_14_Picture_3.jpeg)

#### Pocillopora verrucosa

 Pocillopora verrucosa forms dense colonies of thick, nearly round branches that are covered with little bumps. Colonies reach about 6 inches to a foot in diameter, with branches being about ½ inch thick. Colonies are usually light brown, and sometimes pink or green. P. verrucosa is particularly common on the reef crest but also found on the reef flat and the reef slope.

## Common corals: reef slopes

#### Acropora clathrata (Table coral)

 Acropora clathrata forms large table corals composed of little branchlets that grow nearly horizontal, pointing outward and slightly upward. The branchlets are often fused to each other on their sides and retain a uniform thickness throughout the table. Colonies are typically grey. Especially common on offshore banks, but also present on the reef slope and occasionally in backreef pools.

![](_page_16_Picture_4.jpeg)

#### Acropora hyacinthus (Table coral)

 Acropora hyacinthus forms tables that in some cases can have multiple tiers. In contrast to A. clathrata, the upper surface is composed of small vertical branchlets that taper strongly. Branchlets are lined with corallites that have a projecting lower lip. It is usually a brown or reddish brown, but some colonies have a slight greenish tint. This species is most common on upper reef slopes.

![](_page_17_Picture_2.jpeg)

![](_page_17_Picture_3.jpeg)

#### Diploastrea heliopora

 Diploastrea heliopora colonies are among the largest coral colonies found in American Samoa. It forms large encrusting sheets on steep slopes with thick overhanging lower edges. The corallites are very large, about ½ inch diameter, and resemble little volcanoes with costae (ridges) running down their side. They are a dull green or sometimes grey color with light centers.

![](_page_18_Picture_2.jpeg)

![](_page_18_Picture_3.jpeg)

#### Fungia fungites (Mushroom coral)

 Fungia fungites forms discs that are composed of a single polyp (in contrast to most other corals found on the reef). The slit in the center is where the mouth is. It is "free-living", meaning it is not attached to the substrate unlike most other corals. It can reach up to about one foot in diameter and has radiating ridges (septa) that are close together and have small visible teeth. Small green tentacles are usually extended. It is commonly referred to as "mushroom coral" for their resemblance to an overturned mushroom cap. It is usually either a yellow-green with tentacles extended, or purple without tentacles extended. It is common in backreef pools and on the reef slopes.

![](_page_19_Picture_2.jpeg)

![](_page_19_Picture_3.jpeg)

#### Isopora crateriformis

 Isopora crateriformis forms encrusting sheets, which on a slope can have a plate lower edge. The surface often has an irregular network of thin ridges and is covered with small round tubular corallites that sit very close together. Colonies are up to about a foot and a half in diameter, and are a reddish-brown, sometimes with green centers on the tubular corallites. It is most commonly observed on upper reef slopes, and is most abundant in some areas on the SW part of the island. This species has recently been listed under the Endangered Species Act (ESA).

![](_page_20_Picture_2.jpeg)

![](_page_20_Picture_3.jpeg)

#### Leptoria phrygia (Brain coral)

 Leptoria phrygia is often referred to as "brain coral" due to its appearance. It forms massive, encrusting or lumpy colonies with small meandering ridges that are very short, rounded, smooth and uniform. It is most often brown or cream but sometimes it has fluorescent green between the ridges. This coral is common on reef slopes and uncommon on reef flats.

![](_page_21_Picture_2.jpeg)

#### Lobophyllia hemprichii

 Lobophyllia hemprichii colonies are often large, from 1 to10 feet or more in diameter. The surface is composed of large corallites that commonly are about 2 inches in diameter, and are often oval or even lobed. The outer edge of the polyp is raised and rounded, the center is depressed. There is a very thin crack between adjacent polyps which can go several feet down into the colony because each polyp actually sits on the end of a branch. However, the branches can only be seen if the colony is broken. L. hemprichii is brown and common on medium depth reef slopes.

![](_page_22_Picture_2.jpeg)

#### Montastrea curta

 Montastrea curta forms small "massive" or encrusting colonies. The corallites are about ¼ inch in diameter, circular and relatively uniform. A narrow groove separates the corallites from each other. Smaller, daughter corallites that appear as though they have been squeezed into the available space can be observed. This process is called "extra-tentacular budding" and it gives the colony a crowded look. M. curta is cream to white in color and common on reef slopes.

![](_page_23_Picture_2.jpeg)

![](_page_23_Picture_3.jpeg)

#### Montipora grisea

 Montipora grisea, forms thin encrusting colonies. The surface is covered in tiny polyps with tiny bumps or spines in between giving this species the appearance of coarse sandpaper. Most commonly it is brown, but sometimes you can find green or purple colonies. It is the most common coral on reef slopes.

![](_page_24_Picture_2.jpeg)

![](_page_24_Picture_3.jpeg)

#### Pavona chiriquensis

 Pavona chiriquensis forms encrusting colonies with small but widely spaced bumps. Corallites are small and irregularly spaced between the bumps. The bumps have small ridges running up the side. It is generally a shade of brown or grey and it is often found a bit recessed, in cracks or between other reef substrate. It is common on the reef slopes and sometimes also found on reef flats.

![](_page_25_Picture_2.jpeg)

#### Pavona varians

 Pavona varians forms small encrusting colonies usually less than 10 inches in diameter. The surface is covered with small winding ridges. Corallites are small and barely visible between the ridges. It is often found a bit recessed, in cracks or between other reef substrate. It is generally a shade of brown and very common on the reef slopes.

![](_page_26_Picture_2.jpeg)

![](_page_26_Picture_3.jpeg)

#### Porites rus

 Porites rus colonies form a combination of thin nearly horizontal plates and vertical columns that are irregular and slightly bumpy. In some cases the columns can be fused together, and a colony with fused columns may not have any plates. Colonies can get very large, over 5 feet in diameter. Colonies are often surrounded by lots of small colonies as broken off pieces start to form new colonies. A distinctive pattern of little white ridges can be seen on the tops of columns. It is almost always brown, but rarely blue-grey. P. rus is very common on the reef slope and it is occasionally also found in backreef pools.

![](_page_27_Picture_2.jpeg)

![](_page_27_Picture_3.jpeg)

#### Porites (mound)

 There are several species of Porites that form mounding (also referred to as massive) colonies which are difficult to distinguish. We therefore only refer to them as mounding Porites in this guide. Mounding Porites form rounded, solid colonies of any size from small to very large. The surface of the colony is often slightly bumpy. They are commonly a yellow-brown or green, sometimes brown, rarely purple. They are very common on reef slopes, but also on inner reef flats and in backreef pools.

Fun fact: The southwest coast of Ta'u contains multiple giant Porites colonies which are among the biggest in the world. The largest of these colonies was named "Big Momma", with a circumference of 135 feet and a height of 20 feet! It was estimated to be over 500 years old.
